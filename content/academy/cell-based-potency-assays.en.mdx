# Cell-Based Potency Assays (Bioassays)

Potency answers the only question that ultimately matters: does the product do
what it is supposed to do? For biologics, that must be measured biologically.
A correctly sequenced antibody can still be inactive if it is misfolded or its
binding region is modified, so structure alone cannot stand in for function.
The governing framework is the USP bioassay trilogy - &lt;1032&gt; Design and
Development of Biological Assays, &lt;1033&gt; Biological Assay Validation,
and &lt;1034&gt; Analysis of Biological Assays - together with ICH Q6B, which
expects a potency assay reflecting the mechanism of action in the
specification.

## Common assay formats

| Format | Readout | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporter gene assay | Luminescence/fluorescence from a signaling-responsive promoter | Mechanism-of-action potency for antibodies, cytokines, Fc-fusion proteins |
| Proliferation / anti-proliferation | Cell growth or its inhibition | Growth factors, cytokines, cytotoxic products |
| Cytotoxicity / ADCC / CDC | Target-cell killing via immune effector functions | Therapeutic antibodies with effector function |
| Binding assay (ELISA, SPR) | Target binding signal | Justified surrogate when binding correlates with function |

Regulators increasingly expect the potency assay to reflect the mechanism of
action. A pure binding assay may be accepted as a surrogate only with a
documented scientific bridge to biological function.

## Why relative potency

Cell-based assays are too variable for absolute measurements: the same sample
can read differently on different days, plates, and cell passages. The solution
is **relative potency** - the test sample is always compared against a
reference standard run on the same plate, and potency is reported as a
percentage of the reference (about 100% means "as potent as the reference").
The reference standard absorbs most of the assay's day-to-day noise because the
comparison, not the absolute signal, is what is measured.

This makes the reference standard a critical reagent: it is qualified against a
higher-tier standard (international or primary), bridged carefully when
replaced, and trended over its life. A drifting reference silently shifts every
batch result.

## The 4PL curve and parallelism

Dose-response data are sigmoidal. The standard model is the **4-parameter
logistic (4PL)**: lower asymptote, upper asymptote, slope (Hill coefficient),
and EC50 (the concentration giving half-maximal response). Relative potency is
derived from the horizontal shift between the test and reference curves - the
ratio of their EC50 values - which is meaningful **only if the curves are
parallel**: same asymptotes and same slope, differing only in horizontal
position.

Parallelism is therefore a gate, not a courtesy. USP &lt;1032&gt; supports both
significance-testing and equivalence-testing approaches to similarity; modern
practice favors equivalence tests with predefined margins, because a
non-significant difference is not proof of similarity. A sample that fails
parallelism is telling you something real - partial degradation, aggregation,
or a variant with altered dose-response - and its potency result is not
reportable from that run.

## Assay design that controls noise

USP &lt;1032&gt; treats design as the cheapest place to buy precision:

- enough dilutions (commonly 8 or more across the curve) to define both
  asymptotes and the slope;
- replicate wells per dilution and, for critical assays, replicate
  independent runs;
- the same number of dilution steps and comparable concentration ranges for
  test and reference;
- plate-layout randomization or blocking to control edge and position
  effects;
- controlled cell passage window and consistent cell seeding density, since
  cell state is a dominant variability source;
- predefined system-suitability criteria (curve-fit quality, reference EC50
  range, asymptote windows, signal-to-background) that must pass before any
  sample result is read.

## Validation per USP &lt;1033&gt;

Bioassay validation follows the ICH Q2 logic adapted to relative potency:

- **specificity** - the assay responds to the product's mechanism, not the
  matrix or related molecules;
- **range** - relative-potency interval over which results are accurate and
  precise, demonstrated with samples spiked or degraded to known activity
  levels;
- **intermediate precision** - across days, analysts, plates, and cell
  passages; this, not repeatability, is the realistic variability estimate;
- **accuracy of relative potency** - measured potencies of samples with known
  relative activity recover the expected values within pre-set margins.

Acceptance criteria for a bioassay specification are then set with the assay's
demonstrated variability in mind; an unrealistically tight potency spec on a
noisy assay manufactures OOS results.

## Living with variability in routine use

- reference standard on every plate, with its own parameters trended;
- replicate independent runs combined into a reportable value when justified
  by the validation;
- control charts on system-suitability parameters to catch assay drift before
  it fails a batch;
- OOS investigation that always asks "assay or sample?" first - cell passage
  anomalies, reagent lot changes, and plate effects are the usual suspects;
- trending of batch potency over time, because a slow drift across batches is
  more informative than any single result.

## Common findings and mistakes

- Potency reported from non-parallel curves because "the EC50 looked fine."
- No predefined similarity/parallelism criteria; decisions made after seeing
  the data.
- Reference standard replaced without a bridging study against its
  predecessor.
- Cell passage number uncontrolled or untracked as a variability source.
- Specification limits set tighter than the validated assay variability can
  support.
- System suitability defined so loosely that a bad plate still "passes."
- Replicates treated as independent runs to average away a failed result
  rather than investigating it.
- Method transfer attempted without accounting for the receiving lab's cells,
  reagents, and plate readers.
