# Objectionable Organisms in Non-Sterile Products

Passing the USP &lt;61&gt; microbial enumeration tests and the USP &lt;62&gt;
tests for specified microorganisms is the *entry ticket* for a non-sterile
product — not the whole microbiological story. An organism can be present at
counts well within acceptance criteria, absent from the &lt;62&gt; specified
list, and still render a batch unacceptable. That is the **objectionable
organism** problem, and it is the single most litigated concept in non-sterile
microbiology: FDA warning letters and hundreds of recalls — most famously the
wave of *Burkholderia cepacia* complex contamination in aqueous oral products —
trace back to sites that released product on "meets USP &lt;61&gt; limits"
while an organism hazardous to that product's patients was present.

The governing framework: **USP &lt;61&gt;** (enumeration), **USP &lt;62&gt;**
(specified organisms), **USP &lt;60&gt;** (tests for *Burkholderia cepacia*
complex, official since 2019), **USP &lt;1111&gt;** (acceptance criteria —
which explicitly requires evaluation of the *significance* of recovered
organisms), and **FDA's 2021 guidance** *Microbiological Quality Considerations
in Non-sterile Drug Manufacturing*. ICH Q9 supplies the risk-assessment method.

## The Legal and Compendial Basis

USP &lt;1111&gt; states the principle directly: the significance of
microorganisms recovered must be evaluated in terms of the **use of the
product**, the **nature of the product**, and the **potential hazard to the
user**. Beyond the listed organisms, other recovered organisms should be
evaluated for objectionability — and &lt;1111&gt; names factors including the
route of administration, the patient population, and whether the formulation
supports growth.

FDA's position in CGMP (21 CFR 211.113) is that non-sterile products must be
free of "objectionable" microorganisms — deliberately undefined in the
regulation, because the determination is product-specific. The agency's 2021
guidance consolidates its expectations: manufacturers must assess the risk of
any recovered organism, not merely count CFU against a table.

## The Risk Assessment: What Makes an Organism Objectionable

Three dimensions, assessed together:

**1. Route of administration and site of use.**

| Route / product type | Risk level | Organisms of particular concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inhalation, nasal | High | *Burkholderia cepacia* complex, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, other Gram-negative non-fermenters, molds |
| Ophthalmic (non-sterile contexts, precursors) | High | *P. aeruginosa*, *S. aureus*, fungi |
| Oral liquid, pediatric / immunocompromised use | Medium–High | *E. coli*, *Salmonella* spp., BCC, *E. cloacae* and other enterics |
| Oral solid, standard population | Lower–Medium | Enterobacteria depending on water activity; molds affecting quality |
| Topical, intact skin | Lower | *S. aureus* and *P. aeruginosa* become significant on broken skin or burns |
| Vaginal / rectal / otic | Medium–High | Pathogens of the respective mucosa |

**2. Patient population.** Immunocompromised, neonatal, elderly, cystic
fibrosis, or otherwise vulnerable patients shift the threshold downward — an
organism tolerable in an adult oral solid may be objectionable in the same
formulation dosed to an oncology ward. BCC is the canonical case: rarely
dangerous to healthy adults, potentially fatal to CF patients.

**3. Product characteristics.** Can the organism survive and proliferate in
*this* formulation? Drivers: water activity (aw — aqueous liquids are the
high-risk class; dry tablets with low aw rarely support growth), preservative
system and its verified effectiveness (USP &lt;51&gt;), pH, and nutrient
content. An organism recovered from a preservative-free aqueous syrup is a
different event than the same isolate from a compressed tablet.

## The Decision Framework

When an isolate is recovered, work the sequence — and capture every step in a
decision record:

1. **Identify to species.** "Gram-negative rod" is not an identification.
   Genotypic methods (16S sequencing, MALDI-TOF) are the expectation for any
   isolate that could matter; phenotypic strips alone have repeatedly
   misidentified BCC members. See
   [Microbial Identification](/library/microbial-identification).
2. **Could it survive or proliferate in this formulation?** Assess water
   activity, preservative efficacy data, pH, and storage temperature.
3. **Is the route/patient vulnerable to it?** Map the isolate against the
   route matrix and the product's actual patient population per the label.
4. **Is it on a regulatory watch list?** BCC for aqueous non-sterile products
   (USP &lt;60&gt; testing expectation per FDA's guidance for at-risk
   products); *Salmonella* in any oral product; molds in inhalation products.
5. **Quantify and trend.** Count, frequency of recovery, and comparison to
   historical flora — a first-time isolate of a water-borne Gram-negative in
   an aqueous product is a water-system signal, not only a batch question.
6. **Conclude and document.** Objectionable → reject, investigate source,
   assess distributed batches, consider recall obligations. Not objectionable
   → document the full rationale with the evidence above. Both paths are
   written down; neither is verbal.

Write this logic into a product-specific microbial risk assessment **before**
any excursion. A decision framework improvised during a batch-disposition
meeting, under commercial pressure, is exactly what investigators disassemble.

## BCC: The Deep Dive

The *Burkholderia cepacia* complex — a group of closely related
Gram-negative species — earned its own USP chapter and its own FDA guidance
attention for good reasons:

- **It lives in water.** BCC forms biofilms in purified water systems,
  especially in low-flow dead legs, hoses, and carbon beds. It can be present
  in the water system at levels below routine plate-count detection and
  concentrate in the product. See
  [Pharmaceutical Water Systems](/library/pharmaceutical-water-systems).
- **It survives preservatives.** Multiple recall investigations found BCC
  persisting in preserved aqueous formulations — some strains tolerate or
  degrade parabens and other common preservatives. Passing a USP &lt;51&gt;
  preservative-effectiveness test with standard challenge organisms does not
  demonstrate control of BCC.
- **Identification is hard.** BCC members are frequently misidentified as
  *Pseudomonas* or other non-fermenters by phenotypic systems; selective media
  (USP &lt;60&gt;) plus confirmatory identification are required for a
  defensible "absent" claim.
- **The patient harm is documented.** Contaminated aqueous oral liquids
  (including dioctyl sodium sulfosuccinate and other products) caused
  outbreaks of serious infection in vulnerable patients — the basis for FDA's
  2017 advisory and the 2021 guidance's emphasis.

For aqueous non-sterile products, FDA's guidance expects a risk-based BCC
testing program: USP &lt;60&gt; testing of at-risk products and components,
water-system vigilance, and investigation of any Burkholderia-like isolate to
species level.

## The Objectionable-Organism Decision Record (Template)

A defensible record contains, per isolate event:

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Isolate identification | Method, result to species, confidence, laboratory |
| Recovery context | Product, batch, test, count, specification result |
| Product factors | Dosage form, water activity, preservative system and AET status, pH |
| Patient/route factors | Labeled population, route, vulnerability assessment |
| Regulatory watch check | BCC / &lt;60&gt; applicability, compendial specified organisms |
| Historical comparison | Prior recoveries of this organism; site flora baseline |
| Survival assessment | Evidence the organism could/could not proliferate in the product |
| Conclusion | Objectionable / not objectionable, with rationale |
| Disposition and actions | Batch decision, source investigation, CAPA, risk-assessment update |
| Approvals | Microbiology and QA sign-off with dates |

Roles are deliberately separated: the analyst generates the identification and
count data; the microbiology supervisor interprets survival and flora context;
QA owns the objectionability conclusion and batch disposition. Conflating those
roles — an analyst "deciding" an isolate is fine — is a recurring data-integrity
and governance finding. The decision record above is signed by both
microbiology and QA, and the underlying risk assessment is a controlled,
versioned document, not a notebook judgment.

## Recovering From a Microbiology Warning Letter

The recurring warning-letter pattern: release on total-count compliance,
identification capability limited to Gram stain or genus level, no documented
objectionability assessment, and a water system quietly harboring the
organism. Credible remediation is systemic, not batch-level:

1. **Expand identification capability** — genotypic or MALDI-TOF identification
   for at-risk isolates, with analyst training.
2. **Build the product-specific risk assessments** — per product family:
   route, population, formulation, at-risk organisms, testing program.
3. **Re-examine the water system and ingredient bioburden** as the source —
   sampling, sanitization review, isolate matching between water and product.
4. **Add USP &lt;60&gt; testing** for at-risk aqueous products and components.
5. **Retrain and re-procedure** objectionable-organism evaluation so the
   decision record above is the routine, not the exception.
6. **Trend isolates over time** — flora shifts are the earliest warning of
   loss of control. See
   [Microbial Excursion Investigation](/library/microbial-excursion-investigation).

The regulator's test is whether the *system* now reasons about
objectionability — not whether the one cited batch was handled. Expect
follow-up inspection to sample that reasoning directly: investigators will
pull a recent isolate record and walk the decision chain end to end.

## Common Failure Modes (What Inspectors Find)

1. **"Meets USP &lt;61&gt;" as the whole release rationale** for an aqueous
   product with an unidentified Gram-negative present.
2. **No written risk assessment** defining objectionable organisms per product.
3. **Phenotypic-only identification** of non-fermenters — BCC missed by
   misidentification.
4. **Preservative effectiveness assumed** for organisms never challenged.
5. **Water-system isolates never compared** to product isolates — the source
   link uninvestigated.
6. **&lt;60&gt; testing absent** for at-risk aqueous products after the 2019
   chapter and 2021 guidance.
7. **Objectionability decisions made verbally** in batch review with no
   record.
8. **Mold recoveries dismissed** in inhalation/nasal products where the route
   demands scrutiny.

## Worked Example: One Isolate, Two Products

*Fictional but realistic.* QC recovers a Gram-negative rod, identified by
MALDI-TOF as *Ralstonia pickettii*, at 8 CFU/g in an oral syrup (within
&lt;61&gt; limits) and, the same week, at 2 CFU/g in a topical cream.

- **Syrup (aqueous, pediatric label)**: water activity supports growth;
  *Ralstonia* is a classic water-system organism and opportunist in
  immunocompromised patients. Risk assessment concludes **objectionable**:
  batch rejected, water system investigated (isolate matched to a polishing
  loop sample), distributed-batch assessment performed, BCC/&lt;60&gt;
  applicability re-checked, risk assessment updated.
- **Cream (preserved, intact-skin topical)**: low free water, effective
  preservative per AET, standard population. The same species is assessed as
  **not objectionable at this level** — documented with the full rationale,
  plus a CAPA to verify the preservative system and monitor for recurrence.

Same organism, opposite dispositions — and both defensible, because each
decision was made by the framework, not by the count table.
