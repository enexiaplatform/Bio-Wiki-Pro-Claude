# Elemental Impurities (ICH Q3D)

Elemental impurities — traces of catalysts, reagents, processing equipment, or
container components in a drug product — are controlled today under **ICH
Q3D(R2) Guideline for Elemental Impurities**, mirrored in **USP &lt;232&gt;
Elemental Impurities — Limits** and Ph. Eur. 5.20. The framework replaced the
old sulfide-precipitation "heavy metals" limit test (former USP &lt;231&gt;),
which was retired because it was neither element-specific nor sensitive enough:
a color comparison cannot tell you how much lead is actually in the product.

## The PDE Concept

Every element of concern carries a **Permitted Daily Exposure (PDE)** — the
maximum acceptable daily intake, derived from toxicological assessment, in
micrograms per day. PDEs are **route-specific**: oral, parenteral, and
inhalation products each have their own values, because bioavailability and
toxicity differ sharply by route. Parenteral PDEs are generally far lower than
oral ones.

| Class | Meaning | Elements | Example PDEs (oral / parenteral, µg/day) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Known human toxicants; restricted or banned in pharma use | As, Cd, Hg, Pb | As 15 / 15; Cd 5 / 2; Hg 30 / 3; Pb 5 / 5 |
| 2A | Route-dependent toxicants with relatively high probability of occurrence | Co, Ni, V | Co 50 / 5; Ni 200 / 20; V 100 / 10 |
| 2B | Route-dependent toxicants with low probability of occurrence unless deliberately added | Ag, Au, Pd, Pt, Se, Tl, and others | Pd 100 / 10; Se 150 / 80 |
| 3 | Relatively low oral toxicity; may need control for parenteral/inhalation | Ba, Cr, Cu, Li, Mo, Sb, Sn | Cu 3000 / 300; Li 550 / 250 |

Other elements (e.g. Al, Fe, Zn) are not assigned PDEs by Q3D but may need
consideration from product-quality or regional requirements.

## Who Must Be in the Risk Assessment

Class 1 elements and any element **deliberately added** in manufacture (e.g. a
palladium hydrogenation catalyst) must be evaluated in **every** risk
assessment, regardless of class. Class 2A elements join them because of their
relatively high natural occurrence in excipients and minerals. Class 2B and
Class 3 elements enter the assessment only when there is a reason — added
catalyst, known source, or parenteral/inhalation route.

## Building the Risk Assessment Step by Step

1. List every component and contact source: API synthesis, excipients, water,
   manufacturing equipment, and the container closure system.
2. Mark deliberately added elements (catalysts, reagents) — they are always
   in scope regardless of class.
3. Gather data: supplier CoAs and questionnaires, compendial grades,
   published excipient surveys, equipment material knowledge, and any
   available screening results.
4. Estimate each element's contribution per daily dose and compare the total
   against the PDE and the 30% control threshold.
5. Assign a control option, define any testing or justification, and document
   a conclusion per element.
6. Reassess on change: new supplier, new site, new container, or a process
   change that could add or remove a source.

## The Risk Assessment and Its Four Control Options

The expected deliverable is a documented assessment of all potential sources:
drug substance (synthesis, catalysts), excipients, water, manufacturing
equipment, and the container closure system. ICH Q3D then offers four control
options:

| Option | Logic | When it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Apply common permitted concentration limits across all components of a product with daily intake ≤ 10 g | Simple products, conventional excipients, no red flags |
| 2A | Common permitted concentration limits applied to actual component amounts | Like Option 1 but using real formulation quantities |
| 2B | Component-level limits set from the actual composition and summed against the PDE | Products where one component dominates the risk |
| 3 | Test the finished drug product directly against the PDE | When component data are weak or the product is the only sensible test article |

The pivotal number is the **control threshold: 30% of the PDE**. Where the
assessment shows total elemental impurity levels are consistently below 30% of
the PDE, additional routine testing is typically not required. Levels between
30% and 100% of the PDE call for additional control measures — periodic
testing, tighter component controls, or process changes.

## Measuring: USP &lt;233&gt; and ICP

When testing is needed, **USP &lt;233&gt; Elemental Impurities — Procedures**
governs how. The workhorse techniques are:

- **ICP-MS** (inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry) — the sensitivity
  needed for parenteral and inhalation PDEs;
- **ICP-OES/AES** (optical emission) — adequate for many oral-product levels
  and less demanding limits.

Sample preparation is usually microwave-assisted acid digestion — and it is
where methods fail: volatile elements (notably mercury) are lost with open
digestion, and some matrices simply refuse to dissolve. USP &lt;233&gt;
requires the procedure to be validated or verified with accuracy, precision,
specificity, and limits appropriate to the target concentrations, including the
**J-value** concept for setting limit-test thresholds against the PDE. A
compendial procedure applied to a new matrix still needs documented
verification under actual conditions of use.

## Common Mistakes and Inspection Findings

- No documented risk assessment at all — "we test sometimes" is not a control
  strategy.
- Risk assessment ignores excipients and container closure, focusing only on
  the API.
- Class 2A elements (Co, Ni, V) skipped because "we never add them" — they
  occur naturally in mineral-derived excipients and must still be assessed.
- A deliberately added catalyst (Pd, Pt) missing from the assessment.
- One generic assessment copied across products with different routes of
  administration and daily intakes.
- Data in the 30–100% PDE band with no additional controls defined.
- Digestion procedure never verified for the matrix — especially mercury loss.
- Old heavy-metals thinking: a single "pass" with no element-specific data.
