# Residual Solvents (ICH Q3C)

Residual solvents are volatile organic chemicals used or produced during the
manufacture of drug substances, excipients, or drug products that remain in
the material. They have no therapeutic benefit and real toxicity, so the
control question is never "is there solvent?" but "is the level within a
toxicologically justified limit?"

The governing framework is **ICH Q3C Impurities: Guideline for Residual
Solvents** (current revision R8), implemented in the pharmacopeias as **USP
&lt;467&gt; Residual Solvents**, **Ph. Eur. 5.4 Residual Solvents**, and the
identification/control chapters that point to them. Q3C's core trick is
toxicology-based classification: instead of one blanket limit, each solvent
carries a class and (for Class 2) a Permitted Daily Exposure derived from
animal NOAEL data with adjustment factors.

## The Three Classes

| Class | Meaning | Examples | Control approach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Class 1 — solvents to be avoided | Known or strongly suspected human carcinogens, or environmental hazards | Benzene (2 ppm), carbon tetrachloride (4 ppm), 1,2-dichloroethane (5 ppm), 1,1-dichloroethene (8 ppm) | Do not use; if unavoidable, the stated concentration limit applies and justification is required |
| Class 2 — solvents to be limited | Non-genotoxic animal carcinogens or agents of other irreversible/reversible toxicity | Methanol (3,000 ppm), acetonitrile (410 ppm), methylene chloride (600 ppm), NMP (530 ppm) | PDE in mg/day; Option 1 or Option 2 compliance |
| Class 3 — low toxic potential | Solvents with low toxicity to humans; no health-based limit needed below 0.5% | Ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, isopropanol | 5,000 ppm (0.5%) generally acceptable without further justification |

A fourth Q3C table lists solvents for which no adequate toxicological data
exist (e.g. methyl isobutyl ketone, MTBE). Using one obliges the manufacturer
to justify the residual level — treat "no PDE" as "you own the derivation,"
not as "no limit."

The concentration values above are the Q3C/USP &lt;467&gt; compendial limits;
the 1,1,1-trichloroethane limit (1,500 ppm) reflects its environmental rather
than direct toxicity basis.

These values come from the ICH Q3C tables (revision current at time of
writing — Q3C has been revised repeatedly, including PDE re-evaluations and
new solvent additions such as 2-methyltetrahydrofuran, cyclopentyl methyl
ether, and tert-butanol). Always confirm against the current revision before
writing a specification.

## How a PDE Is Derived

The PDE arithmetic matters because you will eventually meet a solvent Q3C
does not cover. The derivation follows the same NOAEL logic as other
health-based limits:

```
PDE = (NOAEL × weight adjustment) / (F1 × F2 × F3 × F4 × F5)
```

- **F1** — interspecies extrapolation (5 for rat, 12 for mouse, up to 12 for
  other species);
- **F2** — inter-individual human variability (fixed at 10);
- **F3** — study duration (1 for lifetime, up to 10 for very short studies);
- **F4** — severity of effect (1–10; teratogenicity or neurotoxicity raise
  it);
- **F5** — 10 when no NOAEL was established (LOEL only), otherwise 1.

Weight adjustment uses an arbitrary 50 kg adult in Q3C. The point for a QC
professional is not to run toxicology, but to recognize that every PDE is a
documented derivation — and that a solvent with no PDE requires exactly this
kind of derivation, performed by a qualified toxicologist, before a limit
can be defended.

## PDE and the Two Compliance Options

For Class 2 solvents, Q3C publishes a **Permitted Daily Exposure (PDE)** in
mg/day, derived from the NOAEL with modifying factors (F1–F5) for
interspecies scaling, study duration, and severity. Compliance is then shown
one of two ways.

**Option 1 — universal concentration limit.** Assumes any product may be
taken at up to 10 g/day:

```
Concentration limit (ppm) = (1,000 × PDE in mg/day) / 10 g/day
```

So a solvent with PDE 6.2 mg/day has an Option 1 limit of 620 ppm. If every
component of the product is below the Option 1 concentration, the product
complies — no summing required. Simple, conservative, and the compendial
default in USP &lt;467&gt;.

**Option 2 — dose-based, summed.** Uses the product's actual maximum daily
dose and sums contributions across all components:

```
Allowable concentration in a component (ppm) = (1,000 × PDE) / actual daily dose (g)
```

Every component need not individually meet this level; what matters is that
the patient's **total daily intake of the solvent, summed over all
components, stays within the PDE**. Option 2 permits higher concentrations in
low-dose products and is the usual route when Option 1 is unachievable.

## Worked Comparison: Same Solvent, Two Options

*Fictional product, real arithmetic.* A Class 2 solvent with PDE 6.2 mg/day.

- **Option 1:** limit = 1,000 × 6.2 / 10 = **620 ppm** in each component. A
  drug substance at 900 ppm fails Option 1 outright.
- **Option 2:** the tablet's maximum daily dose is 1.5 g total (0.5 g API,
  1.0 g excipients). Allowable concentration = 1,000 × 6.2 / 1.5 ≈
  **4,133 ppm** distributed across components. The API at 900 ppm contributes
  900 µg/g × 0.5 g = 450 µg/day; excipients at 200 ppm contribute
  200 µg/day; total 650 µg/day — comfortably under the 6.2 mg/day PDE. The
  product complies under Option 2 with the calculation documented in the
  specification rationale.

The decision rule: try Option 1 for simplicity; move to Option 2 when a
component legitimately exceeds Option 1 but the dose makes total exposure
safe. Document which option each specification relies on — auditors ask.

## GC Headspace Method Essentials

USP &lt;467&gt; implements the compendial procedures; most labs run headspace
GC with flame-ionization detection (GC-HS-FID), with GC-MS for confirmation
and non-compendial scope.

Key method decisions:

- **Sample diluent** — water for water-soluble articles (Procedure A/B); DMF,
  DMSO, or DMA for water-insoluble articles (Procedure C considerations).
  Diluent choice changes partitioning into the headspace, so it is a method
  variable, not a convenience.
- **Equilibration temperature and time** — typically 80 °C for 45–60 min in
  the compendial procedures; raising temperature improves sensitivity for
  high boilers but risks vial pressure and analyte degradation.
- **Static vs dynamic headspace** — static (equilibrium) headspace is the
  compendial mode; purge-and-trap gives sensitivity for trace Class 1 work at
  the cost of complexity.
- **Standard preparation** — Class 1, Class 2A, and Class 2B standard
  solutions per USP &lt;467&gt;; for site-specific methods, spike at the
  specification level and demonstrate recovery from the actual matrix.
- **System suitability** — resolution of the critical pair (e.g.
  1,1,1-trichloroethane / 1,1-dichloroethene in the Class 1 mixture per the
  compendial SST), signal-to-noise at the limit, and standard-response
  repeatability.
- **Matrix effects** — the article itself shifts gas/liquid partitioning;
  standard addition or matrix-matched calibration is how you prove accuracy.
  Validation per ICH Q2(R2) covers specificity, LOD/LOQ (or a limit-test
  demonstration), linearity, accuracy, and precision at the limit.

Compendial procedures applied to a specific article still require
verification under USP &lt;1226&gt; — suitability in *your* matrix is not
automatic.

## The Risk Assessment That Sizes Your Testing

Q3C is explicit that testing every product for every solvent is not the
expectation. A documented risk assessment decides what is tested where:

1. **Inventory** every solvent used or formed across synthesis, excipient
   manufacture, granulation/coating, and cleaning — including recovered
   solvents and solvent carried in excipients.
2. **Classify** each (1/2/3/no-PDE) and record its limit or PDE.
3. **Assess carry-through** — volatility, process order, drying steps, and
   purge efficiency determine what can plausibly remain.
4. **Place the control** — drug-substance specification, excipient supplier
   control, in-process drying control, or finished-product testing; each with
   justification. Control upstream is legitimate and usually smarter.
5. **Class 1 confirmation** — demonstrate absence/avoidance; if a Class 1
   solvent is genuinely unavoidable, the limit, the justification, and the
   testing regime are all documented and, where relevant, agreed with the
   regulator.

A process using only Class 3 solvents with validated drying typically needs
no routine finished-product residual-solvent testing — but the *reason*
needs to be on file.

## Drying Is Where Control Actually Lives

Residual-solvent outcomes are decided in the dryer, not the chromatograph.
Vacuum-drying temperature and time, tray vs agitated vs fluid-bed
configuration, cake thickness, and the solvent's interaction with the
crystal lattice (solvate formation can lock solvent into the solid) all
determine what survives to release. When a Class 2 solvent persistently
trends near its limit, the investigation should start with drying-cycle
parameters and API particle properties, not with tightening the test.
Changes to drying are process changes with residual-solvent impact — run
them through change control with a documented solvent assessment.

## Documentation for a Defensible Control Strategy

- solvent inventory across API synthesis, excipients, and processing;
- classification and PDE/limit for each solvent, with the Q3C revision
  cited;
- carry-through rationale (volatility, process order, drying capability);
- the Option 1 / Option 2 decision per product, with the Option 2 dose
  arithmetic where used;
- specification placement (API, excipient, finished product) with
  justification;
- method validation or compendial-verification report for the GC-HS method;
- recovered-solvent control procedure and testing;
- supplier agreements covering solvent specs for excipients;
- change-control assessments for route, solvent, or drying changes.

## Common Failure Modes (Inspection Themes)

- Option 1 applied blindly to a product where a component exceeds it, with
  no Option 2 calculation — a specification that cannot be defended either
  way.
- Recovered solvents used without assessing what they carry over (including
  solvents from *other* campaigns) — a classic cross-contamination finding.
- Excipient solvent contributions ignored in the exposure sum; the API met
  its limit while the finished product did not.
- Compendial USP &lt;467&gt; applied to a water-insoluble matrix without
  verification — recovery never demonstrated in the actual diluent.
- Class 1 solvent present from a route decision made years ago, with no
  documented justification or limit testing.
- Solvents with no Q3C PDE (Table 4 solvents) used with no derived limit at
  all.
- Standard prep in a diluent different from the sample diluent, biasing
  headspace partitioning — found during data review, not validation.
- Drying parameters changed without re-assessing residual-solvent impact.
