# Supplier Qualification

Supplier qualification is the documented process for deciding whether a
supplier can provide a material, component, or service without creating
unacceptable quality risk - and for keeping that decision true over years
of changing ownership, processes, and sub-tier sources.

The framework: **ICH Q10** treats outsourced activities and purchased
materials as part of the pharmaceutical quality system, so accountability
stays with the regulated company. **21 CFR 211.84** requires testing and
approval/rejection of components, containers, and closures before use.
For APIs, **ICH Q7** governs the supplier's own GMP obligations and your
evaluation of them. In the EU, **GMP Chapter 5** (starting materials) and
**Chapter 7** (Outsourced Activities) set the expectations, and the
Falsified Medicines Directive added GDP obligations for API supply chains.
FDA's **Guidance for Industry: Contract Manufacturing Arrangements for
Drugs: Quality Agreements (2016)** is the clearest statement of what
quality agreements should contain.

The high-stakes reality: your CoA review is only as good as the supplier
behind it. A supplier that changes a synthesis route, a resin grade, or a
sub-tier source without telling you can invalidate your validation, your
registration, and your batches - and the regulator will ask why your
program allowed it.

## Risk-based tiering

Qualification depth must follow risk. A defensible model:

| Tier | Examples | Typical qualification package |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High / critical | API, primary packaging with product contact, sterile components, contract sterilizers, critical contract labs, sole-source materials | Full technical review, on-site audit, quality agreement, enhanced incoming testing initially, close monitoring |
| Medium | Important excipients, secondary packaging, routine outsourced services, suppliers with mixed performance | Questionnaire + documentation review, audit (on-site or remote) where justified, quality agreement where risk warrants, periodic review |
| Low | Indirect or non-critical materials, no product contact, readily substitutable | Questionnaire and documentation review; periodic confirmation |

Score risk on at least four axes: **product impact** (does it touch the
product or its sterility/identity?), **process criticality** (can you
detect a failure downstream?), **supply position** (sole source?
geographic concentration?), and **supplier maturity** (regulatory history,
quality culture). Document the scoring - the tier decision is itself
inspectable.

## The qualification lifecycle

1. **Initial risk assessment** - define the material, its use, and its
   risk tier.
2. **Information gathering** - questionnaire, regulatory status (DMF/CEP
   references, FDA inspection history), certifications, technical
   documentation.
3. **Technical evaluation** - specifications vs your needs, impurity
   profiles, manufacturing process understanding, change history.
4. **Audit** where justified by tier.
5. **Quality agreement** for critical suppliers and outsourced GMP
   activities.
6. **Approval with defined scope** - entry on the approved supplier list.
7. **Incoming verification** - testing per a justified sampling/testing
   plan, trending of results.
8. **Ongoing monitoring** - performance metrics, complaints, deviations,
   audit findings.
9. **Requalification** - periodic, risk-based, and for-cause.

## Define the approved scope

"Approved supplier" is incomplete unless the scope is exact. The file
should state:

- material, grade, specification, and intended use;
- manufacturing site **and** testing site (these are often different
  legal entities);
- distributor or broker role, if any, and the original manufacturer
  behind them;
- approved route of supply;
- whether supplier CoA results can be relied upon, and for which tests
  (per 211.84(d)(2), component testing can be reduced based on a
  documented supplier reliability program - but identity testing of each
  lot remains yours);
- special restrictions or extra incoming checks.

Scope creep is a real failure mode: a supplier approved for one grade
quietly shipping another, or product manufactured at an unapproved second
site, is a qualification failure even if every lot passes testing.

## The quality agreement, clause by clause

For critical suppliers and any outsourced GMP activity, the quality
agreement is the control document. FDA's 2016 Quality Agreements guidance
structures the content; work through it clause by clause:

| Clause | What it must pin down | The trap it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | Exact materials, services, sites covered | "We thought the other site was included" |
| Specifications & CoA | Agreed specification versions, CoA content, test methods used | Silent spec divergence between your file and their CoA |
| Testing & release responsibilities | Who tests what, who releases, use of supplier data | Both parties assuming the other tests |
| Change notification | Prior notice of changes to material, process, site, method, specification, sub-tier supplier | Unassessed changes arriving in the next shipment |
| Deviation/OOS/complaint communication | What events are reported, in what detail, within what time | Learning about a supplier OOS from your own failing batch |
| Inspection & recall cooperation | Notification of regulatory inspections relevant to your material; recall support | Discovering a supplier warning letter via the press |
| Right to audit | Access, frequency, for-cause triggers, sub-tier audit rights | No lever when performance slides |
| Records & traceability | Retention periods, batch genealogy, sub-tier traceability | Broken traceability during a recall |
| Sub-tier control | How the supplier qualifies *their* suppliers | The real change happens two tiers down |

Two drafting rules: make change notification **prior** (before
implementation or shipment, with a defined notice period), and make the
agreement **specific** - a template signed unread protects no one.

## The audit program

Audits are the verification layer:

- **Frequency by tier** - critical suppliers commonly audited every 2-3
  years (common industry practice - confirm against your SOP), medium
  tier 3-5 years, with for-cause audits any time.
- **On-site vs remote** - remote/document-based audits are acceptable for
  lower-risk reviews; sterile-component, API, and contract-sterilizer
  audits need physical presence to see what documents hide.
- **Scope** - quality system, data integrity, the specific process for
  your material, change control, sub-tier management, cleaning and
  cross-contamination controls.
- **Findings and follow-up** - classified observations, CAPA commitments
  with dates, and verified closure. An audit report with unclosed
  critical findings and continued supply is an inspection liability.
- **Auditor competence** - technical knowledge of the audited process,
  not just checklist GMP.

See [Supplier Audit Program](/library/supplier-audit-program) for audit
execution detail.

## Distributors, brokers, and the traceability gap

Buying through a distributor does not transfer the qualification duty.
For distributed materials, the file should identify the **original
manufacturer and its site**, confirm the distributor's GDP controls
(storage, transport, repackaging status), and define how manufacturer
changes reach you - a distributor that can silently switch sources is an
unqualified supplier wearing an approved one's label. For APIs, EU GDP
rules for active substances expect the full supply chain to be known and
documented; treat broker chains with unknown intermediate holders as a
red flag requiring enhanced verification.

## Ongoing monitoring: the part that gets skipped

Qualification is a lifecycle, not an event. Monitor:

- incoming test results and CoA discrepancies (trend them - drift within
  spec is a signal);
- supplier-related deviations and their recurrence;
- complaint history linked to supplier lots;
- audit findings and CAPA closure discipline;
- late or absent change notifications (discovered changes count double);
- regulatory events - warning letters, import alerts, recalls affecting
  the supplier or their site.

Define **for-cause requalification triggers**: repeated CoA discrepancies,
an adverse regulatory event, an unapproved change, merger/acquisition of
the supplier, site relocation, or a performance trend breach. A poor
trend should trigger reassessment even if the calendar requalification
date has not arrived.

## Change-notification traps: where programs fail

The expensive deviation almost always starts with a supplier change that
nobody assessed:

1. **The sub-tier change.** Your supplier's supplier changes a resin, a
   mining source, or a synthesis step - invisible unless your agreement
   reaches down a tier and your supplier's program is real.
2. **The "equivalent" material.** A grade swap presented as identical;
   the impurity profile or particle size differs just enough to move your
   process.
3. **The site transfer.** Same legal entity, new factory - your
   qualification covered the old site.
4. **The silent process optimization.** Yield improvements at the
   supplier that change polymorph ratio, residual solvents, or
   endotoxin load.
5. **The distributor black box.** You qualified the distributor; the
   actual manufacturer changed underneath.

Defence: a strong prior-notification clause, incoming testing capable of
detecting the plausible changes (not just compendial minimums), and a
change-control process on *your* side that asks "could this shipment
differ from what we qualified?" Every supplier notification - and every
discovered change - enters your change control for assessment before the
next lot is used.

## Worked example: tiering and monitoring a new excipient

*Fictional but realistic.* A formulator proposes a new disintegrant for a
tablet product.

- **Risk scoring:** product-contact material, but non-sterile oral dose,
  detectable failures downstream (dissolution testing), dual-source
  available, supplier with clean regulatory history - scored **medium
  tier**.
- **Package:** questionnaire, DMF reference verified, documentation
  review, remote audit of the manufacturing site, quality agreement with
  90-day prior change notification.
- **Approval scope:** specified grade, specified particle-size range,
  manufacturing site A only.
- **Incoming plan:** full testing on the first ten lots, then reduced
  testing with identity on every lot and periodic full verification -
  consistent with 211.84(d)(2) supplier-reliability logic.
- **Month 14:** trending shows moisture content creeping upward within
  specification across five lots; a discovered (not notified) dryer
  change at the supplier is confirmed. For-cause review: moisture specs
  tightened in the agreement, notification discipline formally flagged,
  audit moved forward a year. The trend is captured in the APR.

The point: the monitoring caught a change the notification clause should
have surfaced - and the program treated that as a supplier-quality event,
not just a data point.
