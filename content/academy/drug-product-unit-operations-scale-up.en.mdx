# Drug product unit operations and scale-up control

## Decision question

Which unit-operation, equipment, hold, sampling, scale and process-control
evidence is needed before a drug-product scale-up or receiving-site decision is
reviewed by qualified roles?

## Applicability and do-not-use boundary

The framework is general; OSD is the worked example. It is not a batch record,
process instruction, scale rule, hold-time limit, sampling plan, process
capability conclusion or validation authorization. Confirm equipment and
dosage-form applicability with process development, engineering, manufacturing,
statistics, validation and quality reviewers.

## Controlled source map

| Source | Decision use | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ICH Q8(R2) | Link unit operations, material attributes and process understanding | It does not provide a scale-up equation |
| ICH Q9(R1) | Prioritize equipment, hold, sampling and failure-mode questions | Risk priority is not an acceptance limit |
| ICH Q10 | Carry process knowledge into transfer and change governance | It does not authorize execution |
| FDA Process Validation guidance | Organize lifecycle evidence | It does not prescribe a universal batch plan |

## Separate eight process evidence objects

1. **Product and process baseline** — dosage form, strength/presentation,
   formulation/material versions, process version, site, scale and decision.
2. **Unit-operation purpose** — the transformation or control contribution
   expected from each operation and the attributes entering and leaving it.
3. **Equipment context** — geometry, operating mode, control/measurement
   architecture, material contact, capacity and known differences.
4. **Material genealogy** — API/excipient lots, intermediates, rework or hold
   states and the batches or samples they entered.
5. **Process history** — additions, sequence, endpoint observations, holds,
   interruptions, alarms, interventions and deviations.
6. **Sampling and measurement** — location, timing, representativeness, sample
   handling, method purpose, capability and version.
7. **Scale and mechanism hypothesis** — how mixing, transfer, densification,
   drying, mechanical energy or another relevant phenomenon could change.
8. **Lifecycle disposition** — supported inference, limitation, open evidence,
   reviewer, next action and trigger for renewed review.

Similar endpoint results do not prove similar mechanisms, and similar nominal
equipment settings do not prove similar product or process histories.

## Run the unit-operation workflow

### 1. Freeze process versions and handoffs

Draw the process from material dispensing through the relevant finished-product
state. For each handoff, record material state, hold condition, equipment,
sample identity and version. Name the exact question: development scale choice,
equipment change, site transfer, deviation assessment, validation evidence or
continued-verification signal.

**Gate:** do not pool or compare data from mixed process, material or method
versions without an explicit comparability rationale.

### 2. Map mechanisms to observable responses

For each operation, state the intended function, plausible failure mechanisms
and observations capable of distinguishing them. In an OSD example, blend,
granulation, drying, milling, lubrication, compression and coating may transmit
material history differently. Other dosage forms need their own mechanism and
applicability review.

**Gate:** an unmeasured mechanism remains a hypothesis and cannot be promoted
to a process control.

### 3. Compare equipment and scale by function

Record geometry and mode differences, measurement locations, control response,
load state, transfer paths and intervention opportunities. Identify which
differences could affect the named mechanism. “Same model family,” “same
setpoint” or “same endpoint” is not a complete scale rationale.

**Gate:** every claimed similarity has a locator and scope; every material
difference has an evidence question or justified exclusion.

### 4. Challenge holds, sampling and data quality

Map planned and unplanned holds, intermediate storage, resumption sequence,
sampling locations, composites, sample preparation and method versions. Review
whether the sampling scheme can see the heterogeneity or transient behavior
being discussed. Preserve missing, censored and non-comparable results.

**Gate:** a process conclusion is blocked when measurement or sampling could
plausibly create the apparent signal.

### 5. Frame studies and lifecycle review

Define mechanism-based questions, representative material/process states,
responses, data lineage, protocol ownership, safety review and escalation for
discordant evidence. Then connect the result to qualification, validation,
transfer, analytical/stability review and change control.

**Gate:** Atlas supplies the evidence architecture, never site settings,
process ranges, sampling instructions, adjustment rules or authorization.

## Evidence and study architecture

| Question | Review-ready evidence | Fail-closed outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can an equipment difference affect the operation's intended function? | Function/geometry comparison, mechanism, representative process evidence and qualified engineering/process review | `Evidence required` when only nominal settings or vendor descriptions are available |
| Does a process signal represent material, process or measurement change? | Batch/material/sample genealogy, capable methods, chronology and competing hypotheses | `Measurement or attribution unresolved` when versions cannot be reconciled |
| Is a hold or interruption understood? | Exact material state, duration/exposure record, resumption history, product/process observations and controlled study rationale | `Product-specific study required`; no universal hold time |
| Is the scale decision ready for validation or transfer review? | Supported scope, limitations, residual risks, receiving-stage inputs and accountable reviewers | `Qualified review required`; no validation conclusion |

## Common failure modes

- Copying development or sending-site settings to different equipment.
- Using one finished-product endpoint to claim every unit operation is
  equivalent.
- Losing material genealogy between intermediate containers and samples.
- Treating an in-process method as capable without intended-use evidence.
- Ignoring intervention, hold, sampling or software/control-system differences.
- Turning a process-capability calculation into validation or batch disposition.

## Evidence chain

1. Identify the process version, unit-operation sequence, equipment geometry,
   intended scale or site change and decision owner.
2. Map material attributes, mixing/heat-transfer or compression context,
   additions, holds, sampling locations and analytical comparability.
3. Separate observed data from hypotheses; retain deviations, missing data,
   competing explanations and actions.
4. Link the scale-up question to validation, transfer, change and lifecycle
   review rather than declaring equivalence from one endpoint.

## Discovery questions

- Which unit operations and process parameters are expected to influence the intended quality attributes?
- What equipment, material, scale and hold differences could make a simple transfer assumption unsafe?
- Which in-process observations or sampling triggers would expose loss of control early?
- What evidence must be closed before the next scale-up, validation or receiving-site review?

## Decision table

| Evidence position | Record | Bounded next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equipment difference is known but impact is open | Difference, affected operation and uncertainty | Request engineering, process and analytical review |
| Process signal is observed without a stable explanation | Observation, competing hypotheses and data quality | Preserve the signal and define targeted evidence work |
| Scale-up evidence is directional only | Applicability and missing evidence | Keep the lifecycle decision under qualified review |
