# Statistical Process Control & Trending

Statistical Process Control (SPC) converts a stream of quality results into
a decision: is this variation the ordinary voice of the process, or a signal
that something changed? In pharmaceutical manufacturing this is not an
optional statistical garnish - it is the operational engine of **Stage 3
Continued Process Verification** under FDA's 2011 process validation
guidance, the trending backbone of the EU GMP Chapter 6 expectation that
results are trended and reviewed, and the evidence base of the **Annual
Product Review / Product Quality Review** required by 21 CFR 211.180(e) and
EU GMP Chapter 1 (PQR).

The stakes are symmetric and both directions hurt: react to noise and you
destabilize a good process (tampering); ignore a real signal and you ship
drifting product until an OOS forces the conversation. SPC exists to tell
the two apart.

Reference framework: FDA Process Validation guidance (2011, Stage 3); ICH
Q8/Q9/Q10 lifecycle concepts; 21 CFR 211.180(e) and EU GMP Chapters 1 and 6
for review/trending duties; the statistical content follows the classical
texts - Western Electric's Statistical Quality Control Handbook (1956) for
the WE rules and Nelson (1984) for the expanded run rules. Acceptance
conventions (Cpk 1.33 and the like) are industry practice, not regulation -
confirm against your procedures.

## Common Cause vs Special Cause: The Only Distinction That Matters

- **Common-cause variation**: the inherent, random, always-present variation
  of a stable process. Individual points carry no assignable meaning. The
  correct response to common-cause variation is **process improvement**
  (reduce the noise floor) or **no action** - never investigation of
  individual points.
- **Special-cause variation**: an assignable, unusual signal - a shift,
  trend, cycle, or outlier with a findable reason. The correct response is
  **timely investigation** of that point or pattern.

Confusing the two is the central failure mode of all trending programs:
investigating every wiggle (tampering, which mathematically *increases*
variation) or filing every excursion as "normal variation" (drift to
failure). Control charts are the instrument that separates them.

## Choosing the Right Chart

Chart selection follows data structure. Get this wrong and every downstream
limit is wrong.

| Chart | Data structure | Pharma examples | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I-MR (individuals & moving range) | One value per sampling point; no rational subgroups | Batch assay, content uniformity mean, yield, dissolution result, pH | The default pharma CPV chart; sensitive to non-normality of individuals - check distribution; MR chart flags sudden jumps |
| Xbar-R / Xbar-S | Subgroups of n = 2-10 (R) or n larger (S) per point | Multiple tablets per batch, fill weights sampled each hour, vial weights per head | Subgrouping must be rational - within-subgroup variation should represent only common cause; mixing days/shifts inside a subgroup destroys the limits |
| p / np chart | Proportion or count defective, varying (p) or constant (np) sample size | Reject rate per batch, visual-inspection defect proportion | Needs large enough n (n×p conventionally at least about 5) or limits behave badly |
| u / c chart | Count of defects per unit, varying (u) or fixed (c) area of opportunity | Particles per unit volume, defects per 100 units, deviations per month | Poisson assumption; same size-sensitivity as p charts |
| EWMA / CUSUM | Any of the above where small sustained drifts matter | Slow assay drift, gradual impurity creep, water-system trending | Detects small shifts far earlier than Shewhart charts; pair with, don't replace, the Shewhart chart; design parameters need justification |

One structural caution before charting anything: the
[Measurement Systems Analysis](/library/measurement-systems-analysis) must
show the measurement system can resolve the variation you intend to chart.
An ndc below 5 turns your control chart into a picture of gauge noise.

## Building Limits Correctly

1. **Baseline period**: compute limits from data representative of the
   process in control - conventionally 20-25+ points; in batch
   manufacturing that may mean limits mature over the first commercial
   year. State the baseline rule in the SOP.
2. **Center line and limits**: mean plus/minus 3 standard deviations of the
   plotted statistic (estimated via MR-bar/d2 for I-MR, or subgroup
   statistics for Xbar charts - software handles this; know what it is
   doing).
3. **Control limits are not specifications.** Limits come from process
   data; specifications come from registration/compendia. A process can be
   perfectly in control and totally incapable (always predictable, always
   near the spec edge) - or out of control while every result still passes.
   The two questions are independent and both must be answered.
4. **Recalculation**: limits are revised only after a confirmed, understood,
   and sustained process change - not because points keep hitting them, and
   never silently. Recalculation events belong in change control or the
   periodic review record.

| Limit type | Source | Meaning | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control (±3σ) | Process data | Boundary of common cause | Investigate special cause |
| Alert / warning (often ±2σ) | Internal convention | Early warning zone | Heightened attention; no rejection |
| Action limit | Internal, risk-based | Pre-defined intervention threshold | Defined action / formal investigation |
| Specification | Registered / compendial | Pass/fail for the product | OOS procedure |

For microbiological and utility data (EM counts, water), pharmacopeial
practice adds its own alert/action framework - same logic, compendial
vocabulary.

## Run Rules: Western Electric and Nelson

A point beyond 3σ is the weakest signal a good program reacts to. The run
rules catch non-random patterns *within* the limits. Western Electric
(1956), the classic set:

1. One point beyond 3σ from the mean.
2. Two of three consecutive points beyond 2σ, same side.
3. Four of five consecutive points beyond 1σ, same side.
4. Eight consecutive points on one side of the mean (a shift).

Nelson (1984) adds, among others:

5. Six consecutive points steadily increasing or decreasing (a trend).
6. Fifteen consecutive points within ±1σ (stratification - suspiciously
   *little* variation; often a subgrouping or data-integrity problem).
7. Fourteen consecutive points alternating up and down (over-control or
   mixed streams).
8. Eight points in a row beyond 1σ on both sides with none within 1σ
   (bimodal mixture).

Do not arm all rules blindly on every chart: each additional rule raises the
false-alarm rate. Common practice is to adopt a defined subset per data
type (e.g. rules 1, 4, and 5 for batch CQAs), written into the trending
SOP with the response each rule triggers. A rule that triggers without a
defined response is decoration.

## Capability: Cp, Cpk, Ppk

Capability answers the second question - can this (stable) process meet the
specification?

| Index | Uses | Tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cp | Overall (long-term) σ estimate; two-sided specs | Potential: spec width vs process spread, ignoring centering |
| Cpk | Overall σ; two-sided or one-sided | Performance including centering: distance from mean to nearest spec, scaled |
| Pp / Ppk | Same formulas with long-term (all-data) σ | Long-run performance including drift between subgroups - the index usually reported in APR/PQR and CPV summaries |

Formulas (two-sided): Cp = (USL − LSL) / 6σ; Cpk = min[(USL − mean), (mean
− LSL)] / 3σ. When σ is estimated within-subgroup (short-term), the same
formulas give Cp/Cpk; with overall σ they give Pp/Ppk.

Interpretation anchors (assuming normality): Cpk = 1.00 corresponds to about
0.27% outside a two-sided specification for a centered process (the classic
±3σ tail area); Cpk = 1.33 to roughly 63 ppm; Cpk = 1.67 to roughly 0.6 ppm.
Common industry targets: 1.33 as a minimum for established processes, 1.67
for critical or new ones - convention, not regulation; set targets by
criticality in your procedures.

Three honesty rules for capability:

- **Capability is meaningless without control.** Cpk computed on an
  unstable process describes a process that does not exist.
- **Check the distribution.** Batch CQAs are often skewed or bounded
  (degradants pile up near zero); normal-theory capability on skewed data
  misleads - transform or use non-normal methods, and say which you did.
- **Watch the measurement system.** Capability of a noisy measurement
  understates the process and overstates your risk - see
  [Measurement Systems Analysis](/library/measurement-systems-analysis).

## Wiring SPC into CPV and the Annual Product Review

Stage 3 Continued Process Verification *is* ongoing SPC: chart CQAs and
CPPs batch to batch, react to special causes through deviation/CAPA, reduce
common-cause variation through improvement projects, and keep the charts
honest with periodic limit reviews. The
[Annual Product Review](/library/annual-product-review) then aggregates a
year of charts into judgements: Did the process stay in control? Did
capability hold against specification? Are alert/action limits still right
for the data? Which trends (even sub-signal ones - a slow creep visible
only across 40 batches) need action next year?

Practical wiring:

- define per-product trending plans (which attributes, which charts, which
  rules, which review cadence);
- route rule violations into the
  [Out-of-Trend investigation](/library/out-of-trend-investigation) process
  with predefined depth (a WE-rule hit is not automatically a deviation -
  but the triage decision must be documented; the
  [OOT Trend Triage Planner](/tools/oot-trend-triage-planner) structures
  this);
- summarize control and capability in the APR/PQR with year-over-year
  comparison, and re-justify limits;
- feed recurring special causes into CAPA, and recurring common-cause
  complaints into process improvement - the two tracks must not merge.

## Worked Example: Fill-Weight Trending (Fictional but Realistic)

A capsule filler produces one fill-weight mean per batch; specification
248-252 mg, target 250 mg. The last 30 batches give mean = 250.1 mg,
MR-bar = 0.28 mg → estimated σ(individuals) = 0.28/1.128 ≈ 0.248 mg; I-chart
limits ≈ 250.1 ± 3 × 0.248 ≈ 249.35 to 250.85 mg.

- Batches 1-28 sit inside the limits with no pattern - the process is in
  control.
- Batch 29 and 30 land at 250.71 and 250.79 mg: inside 3σ, but both beyond
  2σ on the same side → WE rule 2 triggers a documented triage.
  Investigation finds a feeder auger replaced at batch 29 with a slightly
  different pitch - a special cause with a known assignable reason.
- Capability with the current data: Cp = (252 − 248)/(6 × 0.248) ≈ 2.69;
  Cpk = min(252 − 250.1, 250.1 − 248)/3σ... using the *post-change* mean
  250.75 for honesty: Cpk = min(252 − 250.75, 250.75 − 248)/(3 × 0.248) =
  min(1.25, 2.75)/0.744 ≈ **1.68** - still above the site's 1.33 target,
  so the decision is to accept the new mean, recenter the feeder setup
  target to 250.0, and recalculate limits after 20 further batches under
  the documented change.

The shape of the decision is the lesson: signal → triage → assignable
cause → capability check → controlled limit revision. Every step leaves a
record.

## Common Failure Modes and What Inspectors Look For

1. **Charting against specification limits** - spec lines drawn as if they
   were control limits, or control charts never computed at all. Defense:
   process-derived limits; specs shown (if at all) as clearly distinct
   references.
2. **Tampering** - every ±2σ wiggle generates a deviation or a setpoint
   adjustment. Defense: defined rules with defined responses; common-cause
   noise addressed by improvement, not knob-turning.
3. **Signals without response** - rule violations visible in the data, no
   triage records. Defense: documented triage per signal, even when the
   conclusion is "no action justified."
4. **Limits recalculated to absorb drift** - moving limits every time
   points approach them. Defense: recalculation only after confirmed,
   documented process change.
5. **Capability reported on unstable processes** - Cpk theater for the APR.
   Defense: control demonstrated first; distribution checked.
6. **Wrong chart for the data structure** - I-MR on subgrouped data, p
   charts on tiny samples. Defense: chart-selection table in the SOP.
7. **Trending islands** - QC trends assay, production trends yield,
   engineering trends utilities, nobody integrates. Defense: per-product
   trending plans feeding one APR/PQR narrative.
8. **APR as a copy-paste ritual** - tables restated without statistical
   evaluation or conclusions. Defense: year-over-year control and
   capability comparison with explicit judgements and actions.
9. **No measurement-system check** behind the charts. Defense: MSA
   evidence for charted methods.
10. **Microbiological/utility data exempted from trending** - EM and water
    excursions handled case-by-case with no alert/action review. Defense:
    the same run-rule and limit-review discipline applied to utility data.
