# Electronic Records & Signatures (21 CFR Part 11 / Annex 11)

When GMP records and signatures moved from paper to computers, two regulations
defined what "trustworthy" means: **FDA 21 CFR Part 11 (1997)** in the US and
**EU GMP Annex 11 (2011)** in Europe. They are the technical backbone of data
integrity - and they are high-stakes because almost every modern 483 or
Warning Letter data-integrity observation (deleted sequences, shared logins,
unreviewed audit trails, trial injections) is, at root, a Part 11 / Annex 11
failure.

This lesson covers how to scope which records are in, the clause-by-clause
mapping between the two frameworks, what makes an electronic signature
compliant, the reality of hybrid paper/electronic systems, and how to run a
gap assessment that survives inspection.

## The Golden Rule: Predicate Rules Come First

Part 11 does not create record-keeping requirements. **Predicate rules** - the
GxP regulations themselves (21 CFR Parts 210/211 for drugs, 58 for GLP, device
QSR/QMSR, and so on) - define which records must exist. Part 11 then applies
when those records are **created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved,
or transmitted in electronic form** and relied upon to perform regulated
activities.

This gives you the scoping test:

1. Is the record required by a predicate rule (batch record, test result,
   stability data, training record, complaint file)?
2. Is it held or relied upon electronically - including as the source data
   behind a printout?

If both are yes, Part 11 controls apply to the electronic record. A common
error is scoping by "is there a signature" - Part 11 Subpart B (electronic
records) applies even where nobody signs electronically. FDA's 2003 guidance
**Part 11, Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures - Scope and Application**
narrowed enforcement to records required by predicate rules and confirmed
enforcement discretion on some legacy aspects, but the core Subpart B
expectations - validation, audit trails, access control, copies, retention -
are enforced fully today, especially after the 2018 data-integrity Q&A
guidance.

## Part 11 vs Annex 11: Clause Mapping

Annex 11 is shorter and principle-based; Part 11 is prescriptive about
signatures. Both are read with their parent GMPs (Annex 11 with EU GMP Chapter
4 on documentation). The practical mapping:

| Control area | 21 CFR Part 11 | EU GMP Annex 11 (2011) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Validation for intended use | 11.10(a) | Clause 4 (Validation) - risk-based, lifecycle |
| Accurate and complete copies for inspection | 11.10(b) | Clause 8.2 (printouts/exports with metadata) |
| Record protection, retention, retrievability | 11.10(c) | Clauses 7 (Storage), 16 (Business Continuity), 17 (Archiving) |
| Limiting system access to authorized individuals | 11.10(d) | Clause 12 (Security) |
| Secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails | 11.10(e) | Clause 9 (Audit Trails) - with risk-based regular review |
| Authority checks before action | 11.10(g) | Clause 12.4 (access rights management) |
| Operational system checks / sequencing | 11.10(f) | Clause 5 (Data) and workflow design |
| Training and qualification of users | 11.10(i) | Clause 3 (Suppliers and Service Providers), Chapter 2 (Personnel) |
| Electronic signature manifestation and linkage | 11.50, 11.70 | Clause 14 (Electronic Signature) - same security/integrity expectation |
| Signature components and controls (ID/password) | 11.100, 11.200, 11.300 | Clause 14 plus security expectations in Clause 12 |
| Risk management as the sizing principle | FDA 2003 scope guidance | Clause 1 (Risk Management) - explicit |

Two structural differences matter in practice:

- **Annex 11 makes risk management the explicit sizing dial** (Clause 1):
  controls must be proportionate to the GMP impact of the system and data.
  Part 11 achieves the same outcome through FDA's scope guidance and
  enforcement practice, but Annex 11 says it in the text.
- **Part 11 is far more specific on signatures** (Subpart C): uniqueness,
  two-component signing, linkage to the record. Annex 11 Clause 14 requires
  equivalent security and integrity but leaves mechanism to you.

Other frameworks say the same thing: **MHRA GxP Data Integrity Guidance
(2018)**, **PIC/S PI 041 (Good Practices for Data Management and Integrity,
2021)**, and **WHO guidance on data integrity (TRS 996 Annex 5, 2016)** all
converge on ALCOA+ expectations for electronic records.

## Subpart B in Practice: The Six Controls That Fail Inspections

**1. Validation (11.10(a)).** The system must be validated for its intended
use - accuracy, reliability, consistent intended performance. This is where
Part 11 meets CSV: see the
[Computer System Validation lesson](/library/computer-system-validation).
"Vendor said it is Part 11 compliant" is not validation; no software is
compliant in the abstract - only your configured, intended use of it.

**2. Copies (11.10(b)).** You must produce accurate and complete copies -
including metadata and audit trail context - in human-readable form for FDA
inspection. If your CDS can only print a flattened PDF without audit trail,
your copy provision is weak.

**3. Record protection and retention (11.10(c)).** Records must be retrievable
throughout their retention period - through software upgrades, server
migrations, and vendor changes. Retention periods come from predicate rules
(e.g. 21 CFR 211.180: batch records at least one year past expiry; longer for
many record types - confirm against the applicable rule and your SOPs).

**4. Access control (11.10(d), (g)).** Unique accounts, role-based least
privilege, authority checks. Shared logins destroy attributability - the "A"
in ALCOA - and are among the most cited observations.

**5. Audit trails (11.10(e)).** Secure, computer-generated, time-stamped,
recording operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete records
- without obscuring previously recorded information. The trail itself is a
record: it must be retained with the record and **reviewed**. FDA's 2018
data-integrity guidance expects audit trails that capture changes to critical
data to be reviewed with each record and before batch release; MHRA expects
risk-based regular review. An enabled-but-never-read audit trail is treated
as no control at all.

**6. Time and sequence (11.10(f)).** Operational checks enforce permitted
sequencing (you cannot approve before review), and system time must be
synchronized and protected from user edits - backdating is a classic
falsification vector.

## Access-Control Matrix

Map roles to least-privilege permissions and review the matrix periodically
(access review is itself an Annex 11 / PI 041 expectation):

| Role | Acquire data | Reprocess | Modify method | Delete | Admin/config | Approve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Analyst | Yes | Yes (logged, with reason) | No | No | No | No |
| Lab supervisor | Yes | Yes (logged, second-person verified) | Yes (change-controlled) | No | No | No |
| Reviewer / QA | View | No | No | No | No | Yes |
| System admin (IT) | No | No | No | No | Yes | No |

The cardinal rule: **no one who generates data should be able to delete it,
overwrite it, or disable its audit trail.** Administration belongs to people
without a stake in the results. Where a small site cannot fully segregate,
compensating controls (enhanced audit trail review by QA, dual authorization)
must be documented and risk-assessed.

## What Makes an Electronic Signature Compliant

Part 11 Subpart C, in operational terms:

- **Unique to one individual** (11.100): never reused, reassigned, or shared.
  A signature applied by "LabGroup1" fails.
- **Verified identity before issuance** - the organization confirms the
  signer's identity and certifies to FDA (a one-time agency certification per
  11.100(c)) that e-signatures are legally equivalent to handwritten ones.
- **Two components at first signing** (11.200(a)): e.g. user ID plus password
  both applied during a single continuous session; subsequent signings in the
  same session need at least one component. Biometrics follow 11.200(b).
- **Linked to the record** (11.70): the signature cannot be excised, copied,
  or transferred to falsify another record - technically enforced, not by
  policy alone.
- **Manifestation** (11.50): the signed record displays the signer's printed
  name, date/time of signing, and the **meaning** (review, approval,
  responsibility, authorship). A bare "Signed: yes" flag fails 11.50.
- **Credential controls** (11.300): passwords aged, complexity enforced,
  lockout after failed attempts, loss management, tokens/cards controlled.

## Hybrid Systems: Paper plus Electronic

The real world runs hybrid: a CDS holds the electronic chromatograms while a
paper printout gets a wet-ink signature; a LIMS result is transcribed into a
paper batch record. Hybrid is acceptable when you answer, in writing:

1. **Which record is the GxP record relied upon for decisions?** FDA's 2018
   data-integrity Q&A is explicit: where electronic and paper coexist, the
   electronic record is generally the source - a signed printout of a
   chromatogram is not the complete record, because dynamic data (integration
   changes, reprocessing history, audit trail) lives electronically.
2. **Is the relationship defined?** The paper must reference the electronic
   record unambiguously (file paths, sequence IDs, versions) and vice versa.
3. **Is transcription controlled?** Any manual transfer needs second-person
   verification or validated interfacing; unverified transcription is a
   perennial 483 theme.
4. **Are both retained and synchronized?** Discrepancies between paper and
   electronic versions are treated as potential falsification until resolved.

The defensible long-term direction per regulators: minimize hybrid states,
eliminate transcription, and make the electronic system the system of record
with full Part 11 / Annex 11 controls.

## Scoping Assessment: A Worked Method

Run this inventory per system (spreadsheet register owned by QA, refreshed
periodically):

| Step | Question | Example outcome (fictional CDS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Records inventory | Which predicate-rule records does this system create or hold? | Chromatograms, integration results, sequence audit trails, e-signed test reports |
| 2. Electronic reliance | Do we rely on the electronic form for GxP decisions? | Yes - batch disposition uses CDS results |
| 3. Part 11 applicability | Subpart B applies? E-signatures used (Subpart C)? | Both apply |
| 4. Control mapping | Map each 11.10 / Annex 11 clause to existing controls | Audit trail present; copy function lacks audit trail export |
| 5. Gap and risk scoring | Score each gap for data-integrity and patient impact | Copy gap = high (inspection exposure); missing time-sync control = high |
| 6. Remediation plan | Actions, owners, dates, interim mitigations | Configure full export; lock clock to domain time; interim QA review of raw data |

Document the conclusion even when the answer is "not applicable" - inspectors
ask how you decided.

## Common Failure Modes and What Inspectors Look For

Recurring FDA 483 / Warning Letter and MHRA themes:

1. **Shared or generic accounts** ("QC1", "HPLC_Admin") - attributability
   destroyed. Defense: unique accounts, disabled shared credentials, access
   review evidence.
2. **Audit trail enabled but never reviewed** - or review SOP exists with no
   completed records. Defense: risk-based review schedule tied to batch
   release; the [Audit Trail Review Triage tool](/tools/audit-trail-review-triage)
   structures it.
3. **Users can delete or overwrite data** - recycle-bin level "protection" or
   local folder storage. Defense: controlled network storage, no delete
   rights, authority checks tested in validation.
4. **Unreviewed electronic raw data behind signed paper** - trial injections,
   aborted sequences, "test" runs invisible to reviewers. Defense: reviewers
   examine the complete electronic sequence, not the printout.
5. **System time under user control** - clock editable, enabling backdating.
   Defense: centralized time sync, admin-only access, verified in IQ/OQ.
6. **No accurate-and-complete copy capability** - exports strip metadata and
   audit trails. Defense: test copy provision during validation; include in
   supplier assessment.
7. **E-signatures not linked or not manifested** - signature as a checkbox
   with no meaning, name, or timestamp on the record. Defense: verify 11.50 /
   11.70 behavior in UAT with falsification attempts.
8. **Backup untested, archive unreadable** - restore never demonstrated;
   legacy data orphaned. Defense: scheduled restore tests with records;
   migration verification.
9. **Spreadsheet shadow systems** - results recomputed in uncontrolled Excel.
   Defense: inventory, validate or eliminate; see the
   [Computer System Validation lesson](/library/computer-system-validation).
10. **Vendor "Part 11 compliant" certificates filed as the assessment** -
    no evaluation of your configuration and use. Defense: documented
    scoping and gap assessment per system, owned by QA.

## Worked Example: Hybrid CDS Assessment (Fictional but Realistic)

A QC lab runs HPLC assays on a networked CDS. Analysts print chromatograms,
sign the paper, and file it in the batch record; the electronic data sits on
the CDS server. The scoping assessment finds: predicate-rule records (assay
results) are relied upon electronically (results are transcribed from CDS to
LIMS); therefore Subpart B applies. Gaps found: (1) reviewers sign paper
without opening the electronic sequence - aborted runs invisible; (2) two
shared accounts exist for "night shift"; (3) the CDS export omits the audit
trail. Remediation: unique accounts within two weeks; review procedure changed
so second-person review covers the full electronic sequence including audit
trail, documented per batch; transcription replaced by a validated interface;
copy/export configuration corrected and verified in a change-controlled
update. The paper printout is redefined as a convenience copy - the electronic
record is the GxP record. Total effort: six weeks, mostly procedural - the
typical shape of Part 11 remediation.
