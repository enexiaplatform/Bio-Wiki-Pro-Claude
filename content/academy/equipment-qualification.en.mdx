# Equipment Qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ)

Equipment qualification is the documented evidence that a piece of equipment is
suitable for its intended use, is installed correctly, operates as specified, and
performs reliably under real production conditions. It is the equipment-level
counterpart to process validation: FDA's 2011 Process Validation guidance places
facility, utility, and equipment qualification inside Stage 1 (Process Design),
and EU GMP Annex 15 (Qualification and Validation, 2015) defines the classic
DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ stages for the EU framework. ASTM E2500 adds the risk- and
science-based twist: apply verification effort where the risk to product quality
actually lives.

The logic chain is simple and auditors will walk it end to end: the URS says
what you need, DQ shows the design can deliver it, IQ shows it was installed as
designed, OQ shows it works across its ranges, and PQ shows it keeps working in
routine use.

## The qualification lifecycle

| Stage | Core question | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| URS | What must the equipment do, and under what constraints? | Process needs, capacity, materials of construction, utilities, data requirements, regulatory commitments |
| DQ | Does the proposed design meet the URS and GMP? | Design review vs URS, vendor assessment, drawings, material certificates, software architecture review |
| IQ | Was it delivered and installed as designed? | As-built verification, utility connections, instrument list and calibration status, manuals, spare parts, software installation checks |
| OQ | Does it operate correctly across its specified ranges? | Operating-range challenge, alarms and interlocks, set-point verification, worst-case conditions, sequence tests |
| PQ | Does it perform consistently under routine load? | Consecutive runs under production conditions with representative product, load, and operators |
| Maintained state | Does it stay qualified over time? | Calibration, preventive maintenance, trending, periodic review, change control, requalification |

## The URS anchors everything

A User Requirement Specification written before purchase is the acceptance
standard for every later stage. A useful URS is specific, testable, and owned by
the user, not copied from a vendor brochure. It should state:

- process requirements: capacity, throughput, temperature/pressure ranges,
  cycle times, and product-contact conditions;
- product-contact materials and surface finish (for example 316L stainless
  steel, Ra values, where relevant);
- cleaning and sanitization requirements;
- instrumentation and control requirements, including accuracy classes;
- data-integrity expectations: audit trail, user access levels, electronic
  records where applicable;
- utility requirements and environmental constraints;
- applicable regulatory and compendial requirements.

A weak URS ("autoclave, 121 degrees C, big enough") forces teams to negotiate
acceptance criteria after the equipment arrives, which is exactly backwards.

## DQ: prove the design before you buy

Design Qualification documents that the chosen design can meet the URS. In
practice this means a documented design review, a vendor assessment (quality
system, track record, support capability), and review of drawings, materials,
and control architecture against each URS line. For standardized commercial
equipment, Annex 15 allows a risk-based approach where vendor documentation and
a documented selection rationale can cover much of DQ. For custom-built or
highly integrated systems, expect a formal DQ protocol.

Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) and Site Acceptance Testing (SAT) sit beside
DQ/IQ rather than replacing them: FAT catches build and function problems while
fixing them is still cheap; SAT confirms nothing was damaged or changed in
shipping and installation. Where FAT/SAT documentation is sound, Annex 15
permits leveraging it into IQ/OQ instead of repeating tests, provided the
rationale is documented.

## IQ: installed as designed

Installation Qualification verifies, against approved specifications and
drawings, that what arrived and was installed matches what was qualified on
paper. Typical IQ content:

- equipment identification: make, model, serial numbers;
- verification against purchase specification and as-built drawings;
- utility connections (power, air, steam, water) within required quality and
  capacity;
- installation environment: room classification, temperature, humidity, space
  for operation and maintenance;
- instrument inventory with calibration status and certificates;
- materials of construction certificates for product-contact parts;
- software installation: version, configuration baseline, backup verification;
- documentation delivered: manuals, P&IDs, wiring diagrams, spare-parts lists,
  lubrication and maintenance schedules;
- safety features present and tagged.

Every deviation found during IQ (wrong gasket material, missing certificate,
different instrument range) is recorded and resolved or formally accepted
before OQ starts.

## OQ: challenge the operating envelope

Operational Qualification demonstrates that the equipment functions as intended
across its specified operating ranges, in its final installed state. OQ is where
you deliberately push the edges:

- run at minimum and maximum set points, not just the nominal value;
- challenge alarms, interlocks, and safety cut-offs by triggering them;
- verify control-system functions: sequences, recipes, access levels, audit
  trail entries, power-loss and recovery behavior;
- test worst-case conditions justified by risk: minimum/maximum load, extreme
  fill volumes, highest viscosity, lowest line speed;
- verify instruments under operating conditions against calibrated references.

OQ is normally performed with placebo, water, or dummy loads; the point is the
equipment, not the product. Acceptance criteria come from the URS and the
manufacturer's specification, tightened where the process needs more.

## PQ: prove it in routine use

Performance Qualification demonstrates that the qualified equipment performs
consistently under actual routine production conditions: real product or
representative material, real loads, real operators, real SOPs. PQ typically
runs consecutive successful batches or cycles and is often integrated with
process performance qualification. The PQ report closes the loop by mapping
results back to URS requirements and releasing the equipment for GMP use.

Qualification also feeds the downstream system: PQ results become the operating
ranges cited in process validation, cleaning validation, and the control
strategy. That link is why qualification records must be retrievable, not
archived and forgotten.

## Who does what

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| User / process owner | Writes and owns the URS; defines what "suitable for intended use" means |
| Engineering | Executes IQ/OQ, manages vendors, maintains drawings and technical files |
| Vendor | Supplies documentation, executes FAT/SAT, supports IQ/OQ under agreed scope |
| QA | Approves protocols and reports, confirms deviations are closed, releases equipment for GMP use |
| Validation group | Owns the validation plan, risk assessments, traceability, and periodic review |

Annex 15 explicitly allows tasks to be contracted to vendors or third parties,
but the responsibility for qualification stays with the manufacturer. A vendor
protocol signed unread by the site is a finding, not a delegation.

## Requalification triggers

Qualification is a state, not a date. Requalification (full or partial, decided
by documented risk assessment) is typically triggered by:

- modification of the equipment, control system, or software;
- relocation, even within the same building, for affected equipment;
- major maintenance: replacement of critical components, sensors, or
  product-contact parts;
- change in use: new product, new operating range, new load configuration;
- performance drift seen in calibration results, monitoring trends, or
  recurring deviations;
- periodic review concluding that accumulated small changes amount to a
  significant change;
- long shutdown or return to service after a contamination event.

Many sites add a time-based periodic review (industry practice, commonly annual
for critical equipment) whose job is to decide whether the accumulated evidence
still supports the qualified state. The review can conclude "no requalification
needed", but it must conclude it with data.

## Common GMP findings

- No URS, or a URS written after the purchase order.
- FAT/SAT performed but never leveraged or referenced in IQ/OQ, or the reverse:
  vendor tests accepted with no site review.
- OQ run only at nominal set points; alarms and interlocks never challenged.
- Calibration certificates missing or expired at the time of qualification.
- Software treated as invisible: no version control, no configuration baseline,
  no audit-trail verification.
- Relocation or major repair done under maintenance work orders, bypassing
  change control and requalification.
- PQ performed with ideal operators and hand-picked loads, then released for
  routine conditions never demonstrated.
- No traceability from URS requirement to the test that verified it.

## Documentation checklist

A defensible qualification file contains:

- validation/qualification plan and risk assessment;
- approved URS with version history;
- DQ review or vendor/design assessment;
- FAT/SAT reports with deviations;
- IQ and OQ protocols and reports with raw data;
- instrument list and calibration certificates;
- drawings (as-built) and material certificates;
- software configuration baseline and data-integrity assessment;
- PQ protocol, report, and release statement;
- SOPs for operation, cleaning, maintenance, and calibration;
- periodic-review schedule and change-control linkage.
