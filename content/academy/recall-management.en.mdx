# Product Recall Management

A recall removes a defective or potentially harmful product from the market.
It is the quality system's emergency procedure: rarely used, but when used,
it must work **fast, completely, and provably** - under regulatory and media
scrutiny, often within hours of the decision.

The governing framework in the US is **21 CFR Part 7** (Enforcement
Policy), particularly Subpart C, which defines recall strategy,
classification, and public notification, together with **21 CFR 211.150**
(distribution procedures) and **211.180** (records that make traceability
possible). In the EU, **GMP Chapter 8** (Complaints and Product Recall)
requires a designated person responsible for recalls, written procedures,
rapid notification of competent authorities, and recorded reconciliation;
the QP is typically central to the decision. Globally, the WHO GMP recall
guidance follows the same architecture.

The high-stakes reality: in a recall, the regulator grades your *system*,
not your intentions. Distribution records that cannot produce a consignee
list in hours, a reconciliation that never balances, or a mock recall never
performed - each converts one product problem into a company-level finding.

## Recall classification: FDA and EU

| Class | FDA definition (21 CFR 7.3(m)) | Typical examples | Depth and urgency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Class I | Reasonable probability that use will cause serious adverse health consequences or death | Sterility failure in an injectable; wrong API strength; label mix-up between two potent products | To consumer/patient level; immediate; public warning typical |
| Class II | Use may cause temporary or medically reversible consequences, or probability of serious harm is remote | Sub-potent batch still within therapeutic margin; particulate contamination in a non-critical product | Usually to retail/pharmacy level; days |
| Class III | Use not likely to cause adverse health consequences, but the product violates regulations | Minor labeling deviation with no safety impact; container defect without product compromise | Often wholesale level; routine pace |

The EU uses the same Class I/II/III convention under Chapter 8 practice,
with depth defined as wholesale, retail/pharmacy, or patient level.
Classification is proposed by the firm and confirmed by the authority -
FDA's Health Hazard Evaluation committee formally assesses it. Never
self-classify downward to reduce urgency: an understated class that the
authority later upgrades is a credibility event on top of a quality event.

## The health-hazard evaluation

**21 CFR 7.41** lists the factors FDA weighs, and they double as the firm's
own assessment template:

1. Whether disease or injury has already occurred from the product.
2. Whether existing conditions (age, disease state, concomitant therapy)
   in the exposed population could contribute to harm.
3. Assessment of the hazard to the various population segments - children,
   elderly, immunocompromised - with greatest-risk segments driving the
   call.
4. Degree of seriousness of the health hazard.
5. Likelihood of occurrence.
6. Consequences (immediate and long-range) of occurrence.

Document this evaluation even when the answer is "no recall": the
structured no-action decision is what separates a mature quality system
from a lucky one.

## Recall decision tree

1. **Signal** - complaint, OOS, stability failure, deviation, adverse
   event, or regulator query.
2. **Health-hazard evaluation** - severity x probability x exposed
   population (above). This drives the class.
3. **Decide the action type** - recall, market withdrawal (no violation),
   stock recovery (within the firm's direct control), or field correction.
   The vocabulary matters to regulators; a recall relabelled a "market
   withdrawal" to avoid reporting is a serious finding.
4. **Decide scope** - which batches, which markets, how deep (wholesaler /
   pharmacy / patient).
5. **Classify** (I/II/III) and set timelines.
6. **Notify** - regulators within required windows; consignees with clear
   written instructions and response forms.
7. **Retrieve and reconcile** - track returns against distributed
   quantity.
8. **Effectiveness check and close** - confirm retrieval; close with CAPA
   and trend.

The decision is a documented quality decision by the designated recall
committee or responsible person - not an individual under commercial
pressure.

## Notification timelines

- **US:** Part 7 expects firms to notify FDA promptly - for Class I
  situations, contact the district office immediately; FDA will confirm
  classification and may issue or request public warning. Recall
  communications to consignees go out per the approved recall strategy
  (21 CFR 7.42), which FDA reviews.
- **EU:** Chapter 8 requires informing the competent authorities of any
  defect that could result in a recall or abnormal restriction on supply -
  without delay; for serious quality defects in critical products, national
  authorities expect notification within hours to days (confirm against
  your national requirements and MA obligations).
- **Other markets:** many agencies (Health Canada, TGA, MHRA) publish
  market-specific recall notification rules. A multinational recall needs a
  market-by-market notification matrix prepared *in advance*.

## The communication tree

A recall is won or lost in the first hours, and the first hours are pure
communication. The tree must be written, current, and tested:

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Recall coordinator | Single point of command; runs the log, timeline, and tasking |
| Recall committee / decision group | QA head (or QP in the EU), regulatory affairs, medical/pharmacovigilance, legal, supply chain |
| Regulatory affairs | Authority notifications, recall strategy submission, public warning wording |
| Supply chain / distribution | Consignee lists, 3PL activation, return logistics, quarantine of stock |
| Medical / pharmacovigilance | Adverse-event interface, medical risk input to the hazard evaluation |
| Communications / legal | Customer letters, press response, liability posture |
| Distributors and 3PLs | Downstream consignee identification and retrieval |

Every entry needs a name, a deputy, and a 24-hour contact route. A
communication tree with personal mobile numbers from two reorganisations
ago fails exactly when needed - re-verify it at every mock recall.

## Reconciliation math

Reconciliation answers one question: *can we account for every unit of the
affected batch?*

```text
% accounted = (units in own stock + units returned by consignees
             + units confirmed destroyed/consumed) / total distributed
            x 100
```

Worked logic:

- **Total distributed** comes from the distribution records - itself a
  GMP-controlled dataset.
- **Own stock** includes warehouse, in-transit, and returned-to-stock
  quantities under quarantine.
- **Consignee responses** count only when documented (response form,
  return receipt, or confirmed destruction record). "They didn't reply" is
  not accounted for - it is an open gap that drives follow-up calls and,
  for Class I, escalation.
- Sites commonly target full accountability, with defined tolerance and a
  documented rationale for any unreconciled remainder (e.g. consumed
  product supported by pharmacy dispensing data - common industry
  practice; confirm your SOP's acceptance criterion).

Reconciliation never perfectly balances in the real world; what inspectors
expect is that the gap is **small, explained, and chased**, with
effectiveness checks documenting how far retrieval actually went.

## Mock recall exercise design

A mock recall is a full-dress rehearsal of traceability and reconciliation
against the clock:

- **Objective** - e.g. account for 100% (or the SOP's target) of a chosen
  batch within a defined time (24-48 h is common industry practice -
  confirm against your SOP).
- **Scope** - pick a real, recently distributed batch with realistic
  multi-tier distribution (wholesalers, pharmacies, possibly export).
  Choosing an easy batch teaches nothing.
- **Method** - trace from batch record to distribution records to
  consignee lists; issue mock notifications; collect responses; reconcile
  quantities; time every step.
- **Acceptance criteria** - percent accounted for and elapsed time versus
  target; consignee list completeness; contact-tree performance.
- **Output** - gap report (slow consignee data, missing contacts, system
  query limits), CAPAs, and a re-test date.

Run mock recalls at a defined frequency - at least annually is common
industry practice - and after any major change to distribution networks,
ERP systems, or 3PL contracts. Decide in advance whether downstream
partners are informed it is an exercise; uninformed drills are more
realistic but must be agreed with distributors contractually.

## What inspectors look for: failure modes

1. **No mock recall ever performed**, or one performed so long ago the
   contacts are dead. FDA 483 observations routinely cite missing or stale
   recall exercises.
2. **Distribution records that cannot produce consignee lists quickly** -
   the traceability requirement exists on paper only.
3. **Reconciliation never completed** in past real recalls - the file
   shows returns arriving but no final accounting.
4. **Recalls classified downward** without a documented health-hazard
   evaluation.
5. **Regulator notified late**, or after commercial stakeholders were
   managed first.
6. **No effectiveness checks** - nobody verified that consignees actually
   acted.
7. **Recall ends at retrieval** - no CAPA on the root cause, so the same
   defect ships again in a different batch.
8. **Export markets forgotten** - domestic recall executed, international
   consignees informed weeks later.

## Worked example: reconciliation under pressure

*Fictional but realistic.* A Class II recall: sub-potent assay confirmed on
retain samples for batch RX-4412, an oral antihypertensive.

- Total distributed: 84,200 units to 61 consignees across 3 markets.
- Hour 4: own warehouse stock located and quarantined - 21,750 units.
- Hour 24: distributor and 3PL reports in - 58,900 units traceable at
  wholesale level; pharmacy-level lists requested.
- Day 5: consignee responses document 57,100 units returned or
  quarantined at pharmacy level; 1,800 units confirmed dispensed to
  patients (pharmacy records).
- Reconciliation: (21,750 + 57,100 + 1,800) / 84,200 = 95.8% accounted.
  The residual 3,450 units are chased with a second notification round;
  2,900 more are located, leaving 550 units (0.65%) unreconciled -
  documented as dispensed/destroyed at sites with incomplete records,
  with the rationale signed by QA.
- Effectiveness checks: 94% of consignees responded within 10 days; the
  remainder received follow-up calls with logged outcomes.
- Closure: CAPA on the granulation process root cause; the recall file
  shows decision, notifications, reconciliation, effectiveness, and
  CAPA linkage.

## How recalls connect to the QMS

A recall is rarely standalone: it originates in a **complaint**, an
**OOS/stability** failure, or a **deviation**, and it always ends in
**CAPA** and trending. The recall procedure should reference these
processes so the root cause is fixed - not just the market cleared.
Recurring recalls of the same type are a serious quality-system signal for
management review under ICH Q10.
