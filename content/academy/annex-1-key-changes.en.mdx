# EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) — Key Changes

The 2022 revision of EU GMP Annex 1 (Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products)
was the most significant overhaul of sterile manufacturing guidance since the
2008 edition, and arguably since the annex was first issued. Published on 25
August 2022 after public consultations in 2017 and 2020, it became effective on
25 August 2023, with a single delayed provision — paragraph 8.123 on lyophilizer
sterilization and loading/unloading controls — taking effect 25 August 2024.

The revision changed more than requirements: it changed the *logic* of the
document. The 2008 edition read largely as a checklist of grades, limits, and
frequencies. The 2022 edition reads as a **principles- and risk-based system** in
which every individual control must trace back to a documented, site-wide
Contamination Control Strategy (CCS). For QC/QA professionals, the practical
consequence is that "we meet the limits in the table" is no longer a sufficient
answer — you must show *why* each control exists, *what risk* it addresses, and
*what data* proves it works.

Although Annex 1 is an EU GMP document, its influence is global: PIC/S adopted
the revision (PE 009), WHO aligned its guidance, and FDA investigators
increasingly reference its concepts (CCS, PUPSIT, barrier technology) when
assessing aseptic operations worldwide.

## Change Map: 2008 vs 2022

| Area | Annex 1 (2008) | Annex 1 (2022) | Operational impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Structure and scope | Checklist of grades and limits for sterile manufacture | Principles-based; scope extended to facilities where control of microbial, particulate and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination is needed, including some non-sterile steps | Non-sterile and ATMP/biologics operations now explicitly pull Annex 1 principles into their control strategy |
| Contamination control | Controls described piecemeal per section | Documented, holistic **CCS mandatory** (section 2), reviewed and updated continuously | A new master document that must own and link every contamination control |
| Risk management | QRM mentioned at a high level | QRM (ICH Q9) woven through every section; limits and designs must be risk-justified | Every "we do it this way" needs a documented rationale |
| Barrier technology | RABS/isolators acknowledged, limited detail | Dedicated expectations for RABS and isolators (section 4); technology should separate operators from the critical zone wherever practical | Design and operational justification for open vs RABS vs isolator; glove management program |
| Airflow visualization | "Airflow pattern" studies implied | Smoke studies explicitly expected to demonstrate unidirectional airflow over critical operations, including interventions | Video-documented studies, repeated after changes and used in APS/training review |
| Cleanroom requalification | Annual requalification typical | Grade A/B requalified every 6 months; Grade C/D every 12 months (classification) | Doubled classification workload for A/B areas |
| Environmental monitoring | Limits in a table; sampling plan loosely defined | Detailed EM expectations: continuous viable + non-viable in Grade A/B, trend analysis, identification of isolates, investigation of any Grade A recovery | EM program redesign; isolate identification capability becomes essential |
| Personnel | Gowning qualification required | Explicit gowning qualification and periodic requalification (at least annually), participation in successful APS, limits on personnel numbers, monitoring of gloves and gown | Formal personnel qualification lifecycle with disqualification criteria |
| Media fills (APS) | Acceptance table; frequency defined | Zero-contamination expectation strengthened; intervention design, worst-case simulation, APS at least twice per year per process/line/shift; each operator at least one successful APS per year | APS program redesign around intervention matrices and operator coverage |
| Sterilizing filtration | Post-use integrity test expected | Post-use integrity test mandatory; **PUPSIT** expected unless justified by documented risk assessment (section 8.87); redundant filtration addressed | New pre-use testing step or a formal documented justification per product |
| Utilities | General requirements | Detailed WFI/clean steam/gas expectations; WFI by distillation or a purification process equivalent or superior to distillation | Membrane-based WFI production formally acceptable with enhanced monitoring |
| Single-use systems (SUS) | Not addressed | Dedicated section: extractables/leachables risk, supplier qualification, integrity on receipt and use | SUS lifecycle governance program |
| Lyophilization | Brief | Chamber sterilization, Grade A transfer of partially stoppered vials, loading controls (section 8.123, effective 25 Aug 2024) | Lyo loading/sterilization frequency justified in the CCS |
| Cleaning and disinfection | Rotation expected | Validated disinfection program, sporicidal agent use, monitoring of disinfectant effectiveness, residues controlled | Disinfectant qualification against site isolates becomes expected |

## 1. The Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) — the Headline Change

Section 2 of the 2022 annex requires every sterile manufacturer to implement a
documented CCS across the facility. The CCS is not a new SOP; it is the
**master index of your contamination-control reasoning**. It must describe how
the following elements work together to assure sterility (Annex 1, section 2.3):

- design of plant and processes, including associated documentation;
- premises and equipment;
- personnel;
- utilities;
- raw material controls, including in-process controls;
- product containers and closures;
- vendor approval and management of outsourced services;
- process risk management, process validation, and sterilization validation;
- preventive maintenance;
- cleaning and disinfection;
- monitoring systems, including feasibility assessments of new technology;
- continuous improvement based on information from all of the above.

Section 2.5 requires the CCS to be **reviewed and updated** in a periodic
review — in practice, through the product quality review (PQR/APR) and
management review cycles — so that deviations, excursions, and monitoring trends
demonstrably feed back into it. See
[Contamination Control Strategy (CCS)](/library/contamination-control-strategy)
for a section-by-section build guide.

## 2. QRM Woven Throughout

ICH Q9 is no longer a parallel exercise. The 2022 annex invokes risk management
as the basis for decisions on monitoring locations and frequencies, intervention
design, EM alert/action levels, barrier selection, filter configuration, and the
CCS itself. Two practical implications:

1. A decision that deviates from a common industry practice is defensible **if**
   the risk rationale is documented, current, and supported by data.
2. A decision that follows common practice **without** a documented rationale is
   now a finding. "Everyone does it" is not a QRM output.

Keep risk assessments versioned, owned, and linked into the CCS. An FMEA or
risk register that has not changed in five years is itself inspection evidence —
of the wrong kind.

## 3. Barrier Technology and Airflow

The annex takes a clear stance: contamination risk from personnel is the
dominant risk in aseptic processing, and technology should separate operators
from the critical zone wherever practical. Consequences:

- **Isolators and RABS** receive dedicated requirements (glove integrity
  management, decontamination cycles such as vaporized hydrogen peroxide for
  isolators, leak testing, background environment classification).
- **Smoke studies** (airflow visualization) are expected to demonstrate
  unidirectional airflow and the absence of turbulence or reflux over critical
  zones — including during interventions, not just at rest. Studies should be
  video-recorded, repeated after equipment or airflow changes, and reviewed
  when investigating APS or EM failures.
- Open Grade A operations in a Grade B background remain possible but carry the
  heaviest justification burden in the CCS.

## 4. Cleanroom Classification and Requalification

Classification follows ISO 14644-1 particle limits. Notable 2022 points:

- **Requalification frequency**: Grade A/B every 6 months; Grade C/D every 12
  months.
- For Grade A, the ≥5.0 µm particle limit is listed as "no limit" — sampling
  artifacts (electronic noise, sampling errors) at that size in low-count
  environments make fixed limits unreliable. The expectation shifts to
  continuous ≥0.5 µm monitoring and investigation of any excursions.
- Classification (at rest/in operation, formal study) is distinct from routine
  EM (operational monitoring) — both are required and both feed the CCS.

## 5. Environmental Monitoring — the Revised Limits

The 2022 annex retains the familiar four-method viable EM table but embeds it in
a trend-driven program with explicit expectations: continuous viable and
non-viable monitoring in Grade A (and Grade B where justified), sampling at
worst-case locations identified by airflow studies and QRM, and identification
of microorganisms recovered in Grade A and B (and trending of flora elsewhere).

Recommended maximum limits for microbial contamination (Annex 1, 2022, EM
table):

| Grade | Air sample (CFU/m³) | Settle plates, 90 mm (CFU/4 h) | Contact plates, 55 mm (CFU/plate) | Glove print, 5 fingers (CFU/glove) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | &lt;1 | &lt;1 | &lt;1 | &lt;1 |
| B | 10 | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| C | 100 | 50 | 25 | - |
| D | 200 | 100 | 50 | - |

Three interpretive rules inspectors apply:

1. Grade A is a **no-recovery** zone in practice: any CFU in Grade A triggers
   investigation, even though the table shows an average limit.
2. The limits are maxima, not targets. Alert and action levels must be set from
   your own trend data below these limits.
3. An excursion is judged against your alert/action levels **and** the trend —
   a single in-limit result in a rising trend is still a signal.

## 6. Aseptic Process Simulation (Media Fill)

The 2022 annex hardens the APS regime (section 9; see
[Media Fill & Aseptic Process Simulation](/library/media-fill-aps)):

- **Frequency**: initial validation with consecutive satisfactory runs; routine
  APS at least twice per year (approximately every six months) for each aseptic
  process, each filling line, and each shift. Each operator must participate in
  at least one successful APS per year.
- **Acceptance** (per APS): fewer than 5,000 units filled — no contaminated
  units; 5,000–10,000 units — one contaminated unit triggers investigation and
  consideration of a repeat, two trigger revalidation; more than 10,000 units —
  one triggers investigation, two trigger revalidation.
- **Design**: the APS must simulate the worst case — maximum duration, maximum
  operators, all permitted interventions (inherent and corrective), shift
  changes, and the most challenging container/closure format.

The philosophical shift: an APS is no longer a twice-a-year exam. It is the
periodic demonstration that the interventions your process *permits* are
aseptically sound — which makes the intervention matrix, not the unit count,
the heart of the document.

## 7. PUPSIT and Sterilizing Filtration

Section 8.87 requires the integrity of the sterilized product filter to be
**verified before use** (pre-use, post-sterilization integrity testing) and
**confirmed after use**. Where PUPSIT is genuinely not possible — for example,
very small batch volumes where the test would consume the batch — the annex
permits a documented, risk-based alternative, but expects in-depth process
knowledge and compensating controls in exchange.

This was the single most debated provision in the consultations (industry
groups argued the test setup adds its own aseptic risk), and inspectors know
it: a "PUPSIT not applicable" claim without a rigorous written rationale is a
predictable finding. See
[Sterilizing-Grade Filtration & Integrity Testing](/library/sterilizing-grade-filtration).

## 8. Utilities, Water, and Gases

- WFI must be produced from potable or purified water **by distillation or by a
  purification process equivalent or superior to distillation** (reverse
  osmosis coupled with other appropriate techniques such as electrodeionization
  and ultrafiltration) — formally opening the door to membrane-based WFI in the
  EU, with enhanced monitoring expectations to match.
- Gases contacting product or sterile surfaces must be filtered through
  sterilizing-grade filters at the point of use, with integrity testing
  expectations analogous to liquid filters.
- Utility performance is part of the CCS: your WFI, clean steam, and compressed
  gas data are contamination-control evidence, not just engineering records.

## 9. Personnel — the Largest Risk, Now Formalized

- Only the minimum required number of personnel in clean areas, especially
  Grade A/B.
- Gowning qualification before unescorted access; **periodic requalification at
  least annually**, including gowning monitoring and participation in a
  successful APS.
- Failed qualification or adverse monitoring trends require removal from
  aseptic duties and requalification.
- Grade A/B gowning: sterile gown covering all skin and hair; sterile,
  powder-free gloves; gloves sanitized at defined intervals and monitored
  (glove prints) on exit and after critical interventions.

## 10. Single-Use Systems

New in 2022: a dedicated SUS section covering supplier qualification, quality
agreements, extractables/leachables risk assessment, integrity on receipt and
before/after use, and the sterility assurance of the SUS supply chain. If your
process depends on single-use bags, manifolds, or connectors, the CCS must
address how their sterility and integrity are assured — including the risk of
the aseptic connections made with them.

## Transition Program: How to Run the Gap Assessment

A defensible Annex 1 transition is a managed project, not a document edit.
Typical workflow, roles, and outputs:

1. **Scope and inventory** (QA lead + production + engineering): list every
   sterile process, line, room, and utility. Output: facility/process register.
2. **Section-by-section gap assessment** (cross-functional team): map each
   Annex 1 clause to current practice and evidence. Rate Compliant / Partial /
   Gap. Output: gap register with risk ratings (use ICH Q9 tooling — see
   [Quality Risk Management (ICH Q9)](/library/quality-risk-management-q9)).
3. **Risk-prioritize** (QA + site leadership): rank gaps by sterility risk, not
   by ease of closure. PUPSIT rationale, EM redesign, and CCS authoring usually
   top the list. Output: prioritized remediation plan with owners and dates.
4. **Author or rebuild the CCS** (QA micro lead, with production, engineering,
   validation): integrate existing controls, name an owner, define the periodic
   review trigger. Output: approved CCS v1.0.
5. **Remediate**: revised SOPs, smoke studies, APS redesign, gowning
   requalification, disinfectant qualification, SUS assessments. Output: change
   controls, protocols, and reports.
6. **Verify closure**: effectiveness checks against monitoring data; feed into
   PQR and management review. Output: CCS v1.1+, demonstrating the loop works.

## CCS Gap-Analysis Checklist (Mapped to Annex 1 Section 2)

Work through each and rate Compliant / Partial / Gap, with evidence cited:

- [ ] A single, approved CCS document exists and names an owner.
- [ ] Facility/equipment design rationale (flows, pressure cascade, segregation).
- [ ] Barrier technology decision (open / RABS / isolator) justified by risk.
- [ ] Grade A airflow visualization (smoke studies) current, video-recorded,
      and includes interventions.
- [ ] PUPSIT decision documented with a risk rationale (perform it, or justify
      not, per product).
- [ ] EM program: continuous viable + non-viable in Grade A/B; limits and
      alert/action levels trend-derived; isolates identified.
- [ ] Media-fill program at zero-contamination expectation; all permitted
      interventions scripted; operator coverage at least annually.
- [ ] Utilities (WFI/PW, clean steam, process gases) controls and monitoring
      linked into the CCS.
- [ ] Each control's effectiveness reviewed against real monitoring data.
- [ ] CCS feeds — and is visibly updated by — deviations, excursions, and APS
      results.

## What Inspectors Look For (and What They Find)

Observed themes from FDA 483s, warning letters, and MHRA deficiency reports
since the 2022 revision:

1. **CCS as a binder, not a system.** The CCS exists but cannot be traced to a
   single real decision or monitoring trend. Defense: version the CCS and cite
   the deviation/excursion that changed it.
2. **No rationale for deviations from the annex's explicit expectations** —
   most commonly PUPSIT and Grade A monitoring coverage. Defense: a current,
   data-backed written risk assessment per product.
3. **Smoke studies that omit interventions.** At-rest airflow is clean; the
   first intervention creates reflux over the open vial. Defense: script
   interventions into the study and keep the video.
4. **EM alert/action levels copied from the annex table** instead of derived
   from site trend data. Defense: statistical review of your own data at a
   defined frequency.
5. **Grade A recoveries not investigated as failures.** A single CFU closed as
   "within limit." Defense: treat every Grade A recovery as an investigation
   with organism identification.
6. **Operator requalification gaps.** Annual gowning requalification or APS
   participation missed for individuals, including supervisors who enter
   rarely. Defense: an automated qualification-status tracker that gates access.
7. **Disinfection program never qualified against site flora.** Defense:
   disinfectant efficacy studies using representative surfaces and site
   isolates (see [Disinfectant Qualification](/library/disinfectant-qualification)).
8. **SUS sterility assumed from the supplier certificate** with no integrity
   verification at receipt/use. Defense: documented SUS risk assessment and
   incoming integrity strategy.

## Worked Example: One Line, Six Months

*Fictional but realistic.* A small fill-finish site runs one open RABS vial
line, two shifts, three sterile products.

- **Month 1**: gap register identifies 41 findings; top three by risk: no
  documented PUPSIT rationale (gap), EM action levels equal to annex limits
  (partial), no CCS document (gap).
- **Month 2–3**: CCS v1.0 authored by QA micro with production/engineering;
  PUPSIT assessment concludes pre-use testing is feasible on the existing
  filter skid with a minor piping change — change control raised.
- **Month 3–4**: smoke studies repeated with the top five corrective
  interventions scripted; one intervention (stopper-bowl refill) shows reflux —
  intervention redesigned and re-studied.
- **Month 5**: EM action levels re-derived from 24 months of trend data; APS
  redesigned around the new intervention matrix; all 14 operators' annual
  qualification status loaded into a tracker.
- **Month 6**: gap register review closes 36 of 41 items; the remaining five
  (SUS integrity verification) have dates and owners. The inspection-ready
  story is not "we are compliant" — it is "here is the register, the rationale,
  and the trend data."
