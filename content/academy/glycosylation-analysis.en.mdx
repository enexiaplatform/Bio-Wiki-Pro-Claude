# Glycosylation Analysis

Most therapeutic monoclonal antibodies and many recombinant proteins carry
N-linked oligosaccharides installed during cell culture. These glycans are
not decoration: a single conserved N-glycan at Asn297 in the Fc region
controls how the antibody engages Fcγ receptors and complement, and the
distribution of glycoforms shifts with every upstream process variable.
That combination — direct functional impact plus extreme process
sensitivity — is why the glycan profile is a **critical quality attribute
(CQA)** under ICH Q8/Q6B and one of the most informative things QC trends.

## Why Specific Glycan Features Are Critical

Each glycan feature maps to a functional or safety consequence, and that
mapping is the rationale the specification must cite:

| Glycan feature | Functional consequence | Why QC controls it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core fucosylation (G0F, G1F, G2F levels) | Fucose on the Fc glycan sterically hinders FcγRIIIa binding; **afucosylated** antibodies show markedly higher ADCC | Direct potency link; afucosylation is deliberately engineered in some mAbs (e.g. glycoengineered products) |
| Galactosylation (G0 vs G1/G2) | Terminal galactose influences CDC (complement) activity; modest effect on FcγR binding | Potency-relevant; drifts with culture age and feed |
| High-mannose species (Man5–Man9) | Immature glycans signal incomplete processing; high-mannose glycoforms clear faster from serum via mannose receptors and can raise ADCC | Half-life/clearance impact; process-immaturity marker |
| Sialylation | Sialic acid caps extend half-life (especially in Fc-fusion proteins and hormones); low sialylation shortens exposure | Pharmacokinetics; critical for sialylated non-mAb products |
| Non-human glycans (α-Gal, NGNA) | Gal-α1,3-Gal and N-glycolylneuraminic acid from non-human cell lines can be immunogenic; some patients carry pre-existing antibodies | Safety; cell-line and raw-material control |

The practical consequence: a glycan specification without a stated
functional rationale is indefensible, and an investigation into a glycan
shift is impossible without knowing *why* the attribute matters.

## Reading the Nomenclature

Glycan reports use a shorthand that encodes structure; QC professionals
must read it fluently:

| Symbol | Structure | Functional note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G0 | Biantennary complex, no galactose, no core fucose | Afucosylated species; ADCC-enhancing |
| G0F | G0 with core fucose | The dominant species in typical CHO mAbs |
| G1F / G2F | One / two terminal galactoses, core fucose | Galactosylation level; CDC-relevant |
| G0F-N or similar truncated forms | Missing one antenna | Processing-completeness marker |
| Man5 (M5)–Man9 | High-mannose, unprocessed | Faster clearance; immaturity marker |
| A1/A2 (with S1/S2) | Sialylated antennae | Half-life-relevant in sialylated products |
| G0-GN, hybrid types | Incomplete complex processing | Culture-condition markers |

A released-glycan chromatogram of a standard CHO mAb is typically dominated
by G0F, with G1F and G2F next and small high-mannose and afucosylated
fractions — the exact profile is the product's fingerprint, and deviations
from it are what trending exists to catch.

## N-Linked vs O-Linked Glycans

Everything above describes N-linked glycosylation — sugars attached to
asparagine in a consensus sequence (Asn-X-Ser/Thr), the dominant and
best-characterized case for mAbs. O-linked glycans attach to serine or
threonine without a consensus motif, are common in Fc-fusion proteins and
some hormones, and require different analytics: no universal release enzyme
equivalent to PNGase F exists, so O-glycan analysis typically uses
beta-elimination chemistry or direct glycopeptide/intact MS. If the product
carries O-glycans, that is a separate CQA with its own method and its own
validation — do not let an N-glycan program silently claim to cover it.
Bispecifics and fusion proteins with multiple glycosylation domains raise
the same point in a second form: each domain needs its own site-resolved
control logic.

## The Three Analytical Levels

Glycosylation is measured at three levels of structural detail, chosen per
question:

| Level | Technique | What it answers | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Released glycans | PNGase F release, fluorescent label (2-AB, 2-AA, RapiFluor-MS), HILIC separation with FLD ± MS | Relative % of each N-glycan species across the whole molecule (G0F, G1F, G2F, Man5, etc.) | Routine release/characterization of the overall profile |
| Glycopeptide mapping | Proteolytic digest (trypsin), LC-MS(/MS) of glycopeptides | Which site carries which glycan; site occupancy (including % unglycosylated) | Characterization, comparability, site-specific CQAs |
| Intact / subunit mass | Intact or IdeS-digested mAb by LC-MS | Global glycoform mass fingerprint; fast comparability readout | Comparability, in-process checks, clone screening |

Supporting assays fill gaps: monosaccharide-composition analysis for total
sugar content, sialic-acid quantitation (DMB labeling or equivalent) for
sialylated products, and MALDI-TOF MS for rapid structural confirmation.
The three levels are complementary, not interchangeable — released-glycan
profiling cannot tell you occupancy at a specific site, and intact mass
cannot resolve isobaric glycoforms.

## The Released-Glycan HILIC-FLD-MS Workflow

The workhorse release method, step by step:

1. **Denature and release** — the mAb is denatured and N-glycans are
   cleaved enzymatically with PNGase F. Complete release is a method-
   validation point: inaccessible glycans under-report.
2. **Label** — the free glycans are reductively labeled with a fluorophore
   (2-AB classic; RapiFluor-MS adds an MS-friendly basic tag for combined
   FLD+MS). Labeling efficiency must be demonstrated as uniform across
   species or corrected.
3. **Separate by HILIC** — hydrophilic-interaction chromatography resolves
   glycans primarily by size/hydrophilicity on an amide column with an
   acetonitrile/ammonium-formate gradient. Retention is calibrated against
   a dextran ladder to express glucose-unit (GU) values, making peak
   assignment transferable between systems.
4. **Detect and quantify** — fluorescence gives sensitive relative
   quantitation (area % per species); MS confirms identity by mass. Report
   the named species plus grouped attributes: % afucosylated, %
   high-mannose, % galactosylated, % sialylated.
5. **System suitability** — a characterized glycan standard verifies
   resolution of critical pairs (e.g. G1F isomers) and quantitation
   precision before sample data are valid.

The method is validated per ICH Q2(R2) with emphasis on repeatability of
the area-% reportable values and specificity against co-eluting species —
see [Analytical Method Validation](/library/analytical-method-validation).

## Glycopeptide Mapping: When the Site Matters

Released-glycan profiling averages over the whole molecule; it cannot tell
you whether a glycan sits on Fc-Asn297, an Fab site, or a second Fc site —
and those are different CQAs. Glycopeptide mapping answers the site
question:

1. **Digest** the mAb with a protease (trypsin, or an Fc-specific enzyme
   such as IdeS combined with further digestion) under conditions
   demonstrated not to alter glycans.
2. **Separate** the peptides/glycopeptides by reversed-phase LC.
3. **Detect by MS(/MS)** — each glycopeptide appears as a family of masses
   (same peptide, different glycans); MS/MS confirms assignments.
4. **Quantify per site** — relative abundance of each glycoform at each
   site, plus the unglycosylated peptide as the occupancy denominator.

Glycopeptide mapping is the comparability workhorse: after a process or
site change, it is the method most likely to catch a site-specific shift
that a whole-molecule average would dilute away.

## Glycosylation in Comparability

Because glycans are both functionally critical and process-sensitive, the
glycan profile carries heavy weight in comparability exercises (scale-up,
site transfer, process changes — per ICH Q5E). A defensible comparability
package compares released-glycan profiles *and* site-specific occupancy,
against pre-defined equivalence criteria, alongside potency. "Profiles look
similar" without predefined criteria is not comparability.

## Documentation Package

A defensible glycosylation control file contains: the CQA rationale linking
each monitored glycan attribute to function; the cell-line and raw-material
assessment for non-human glycans; released-glycan method validation
(release efficiency, labeling uniformity, GU-based identification);
glycopeptide-mapping characterization data; specification ranges with
functional justification; cross-campaign trending with alert rules; and
comparability protocols/results for process changes.

## What QC Actually Controls

A release and stability panel typically tracks:

- **% afucosylation** (or its inverse, fucosylated total) — tied to the
  ADCC-relevant potency claim;
- **% high-mannose** — clearance and process-maturity marker;
- **% galactosylation (G1+G2 relative to G0)** — CDC-relevant and a
  sensitive culture-consistency readout;
- **% sialylation** — for sialylated products, a half-life attribute;
- **Site occupancy** — by glycopeptide mapping, where partial occupancy is
  itself a CQA.

Each attribute carries a range justified by functional data (potency,
PK, or clinical exposure), not by process capability alone — the CQA
logic of ICH Q8.

## Process Sensitivity: Why Glycans Move First

Glycosylation is installed in the Golgi and is exquisitely sensitive to
upstream conditions: media composition and trace elements (notably
manganese as a galactosyltransferase cofactor), feed strategy and nutrient
depletion, pH and dissolved-oxygen shifts, culture temperature, and harvest
day. A glycan drift is therefore usually the *first* CQA to signal a cell-
culture change — often before titer or other attributes move. That makes
glycan trending both a quality control and a process-monitoring tool.

## Investigating a Glycan Shift

1. **Confirm the assay** — release efficiency, labeling, HILIC resolution,
   standard chromatography; rule out an analytical artifact first.
2. **Look upstream, not at the molecule** — a media or feed lot change, a
   temperature/DO excursion, a harvest-day shift, a scale-up or site
   transfer. Glycan shifts almost never originate downstream.
3. **Map the direction** — which species moved (galactosylation down?
   high-mannose up?) points to the mechanism: galactosylation loss suggests
   cofactor/feed or culture-age issues; high-mannose rise suggests
   processing-time or stress.
4. **Assess functional impact** — tie the magnitude of change to the
   potency/clearance rationale, and involve QA on comparability if the
   process change was intentional.

## Common Failure Modes (Inspection Themes)

- A glycan specification with no stated functional rationale, so OOT
  results cannot be assessed for impact.
- Released-glycan profiling used to claim *site* occupancy — a level-of-
  detail mismatch the data cannot support.
- Incomplete PNGase F release never checked, under-reporting total glycan
  and skewing relative percentages.
- Retention-time-only peak assignment without a GU ladder or MS
  confirmation, so a co-eluting species is misidentified across a column
  or method change.
- Glycan results reviewed per batch with no cross-campaign trending,
  missing the slow upstream drift.
- Comparability after a process/site change assessed without glycopeptide
  mapping, so a site-occupancy shift is missed.
- Non-human glycans (α-Gal, NGNA) not assessed for a new cell line or raw
  material despite the immunogenicity risk.
