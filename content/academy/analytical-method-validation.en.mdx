# Analytical Method Validation (ICH Q2)

Method validation is the documented evidence that an analytical procedure is fit
for its intended purpose. It is what separates a method that merely produces
numbers from a method whose numbers a QA unit can use to release product.

The governing framework is **ICH Q2(R2) Validation of Analytical Procedures**
(adopted in 2023), read together with **ICH Q14 Analytical Procedure
Development**. Q2(R2) defines which validation characteristics apply to which
type of procedure and how they are demonstrated; Q14 covers the development and
lifecycle knowledge that makes validation rational rather than ritual. The
underlying expectations also appear in USP &lt;1225&gt; and FDA's 2015 guidance
on analytical procedures and methods validation.

## Which Characteristics Apply to Which Test

The first validation decision is classifying the procedure, because that drives
the whole study design.

| Procedure type | Characteristics typically required |
| --- | --- |
| Identification | Specificity |
| Impurity test — quantitative | Specificity, accuracy, precision, linearity, range, quantitation limit |
| Impurity test — limit test | Specificity, detection limit |
| Assay / content / potency | Specificity, accuracy, precision, linearity, range |
| Dissolution / performance tests | Specificity, accuracy, precision, range, as applicable |

Two rules of thumb: identification tests only need to show the method sees the
right analyte, while quantitative tests must prove the result is *right*, not
just specific. LOD alone is never enough for an impurity you must quantitate.

## The Characteristics, Designed as Experiments

### Specificity

Show the procedure measures the analyte unequivocally in the presence of
impurities, degradants, excipients, and matrix. For chromatographic assay, this
means forced degradation samples (acid, base, oxidation, heat, light) with peak
purity or resolution evidence that degradants do not co-elute with the main
peak. A stability-indicating method claim lives or dies here.

### Accuracy

Closeness to the true value, usually shown as recovery of known added analyte.
Common industry practice (not a compendial mandate) is **three concentration
levels across the range, three replicates each**, with recovery acceptance such
as 98.0–102.0% for assay of a drug substance and wider, justified limits for
trace-level impurities.

### Precision

Three nested levels, all reported as %RSD:

- **Repeatability** — same analyst, same equipment, short time. Typical design:
  six determinations at 100% of the test concentration, or nine determinations
  across the range.
- **Intermediate precision** — within-laboratory variation: different days,
  analysts, or instruments. This is the characteristic that predicts QC
  transfer pain.
- **Reproducibility** — between laboratories; relevant for method
  standardization or transfer, not required for every validation.

### Linearity and range

Demonstrate a proportional response across the working interval. ICH Q2
recommends a **minimum of five concentration levels** for establishing
linearity, evaluated visually and by regression (correlation coefficient,
y-intercept, residual plot — a good r value with a biased residual pattern is
not linearity). The range is the interval over which accuracy, precision, and
linearity were all shown to be adequate.

### Detection limit and quantitation limit

For impurity methods, ICH Q2(R2) accepts several approaches: visual evaluation,
**signal-to-noise (about 3:1 for LOD, 10:1 for LOQ)**, or calculation from the
standard deviation of the response and the calibration slope. Whatever the
approach, the LOQ must be confirmed experimentally with accuracy and precision
at that level — an LOQ you cannot measure precisely is a paper number.

### Robustness

Deliberate small variations in method parameters (mobile phase composition, pH,
column lot, temperature, flow rate) show the method stays reliable under normal
use. Under the Q14 lifecycle model, robustness knowledge is built during
development; validation confirms it. Skipping it entirely is a common reason a
method fails at transfer.

## Designing Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria belong in the protocol **before** the first injection, and
they must be derived from the method's job, not negotiated from the data after
the fact.

| Characteristic | Example criterion (industry practice — justify per method) |
| --- | --- |
| Assay accuracy | Mean recovery 98.0–102.0% |
| Assay repeatability | %RSD ≤ 2.0% (n = 6) |
| Impurity LOQ | Accuracy/precision demonstrated at or below the reporting threshold |
| Linearity | r ≥ 0.999 plus acceptable residuals |
| Specificity | No interfering peak; resolution to nearest degradant ≥ 1.5 |

The logic: a method controlling an attribute with a 90.0–110.0% specification
needs far better precision than one measuring a 0.5% impurity limit. Write the
criterion from the specification's tolerance and the decision the result feeds,
then record the justification in the protocol.

## The Validation Report

The report is the deliverable an inspector actually reads. It should contain:

1. the method's intended purpose and procedure reference/version;
2. the approved protocol, including pre-defined acceptance criteria;
3. the design executed per characteristic (levels, replicates, analysts,
   instruments, column lots);
4. all raw and summarized data, with statistical treatment;
5. every deviation from the protocol and its disposition;
6. a clear pass/fail conclusion against each criterion;
7. the validated range and any restrictions on method use;
8. revalidation triggers: method changes, synthesis route changes, new
   impurity profiles, or instrument/platform changes.

A report that lists results without comparing them to pre-set criteria is not a
validation; it is a data dump.

## Common Inspection Findings

- Acceptance criteria written (or relaxed) after the data were in.
- Specificity claimed without forced degradation or peak-purity evidence.
- Intermediate precision omitted — then the method fails during transfer to QC.
- LOQ calculated but never confirmed with experimental accuracy and precision.
- Validation run once at development and never revisited after a route,
  formulation, or instrument change.
- System suitability drift treated as random noise instead of evidence the
  validated state is eroding.
- Compendial method used for a new matrix without verification that it is
  suitable under actual conditions of use.
