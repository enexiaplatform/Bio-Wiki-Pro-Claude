# Acceptance Sampling & AQL (ANSI/ASQ Z1.4)

Acceptance sampling lets you make an accept/reject decision about a lot by
inspecting a sample instead of 100% of the units. The governing standard for
attribute (pass/fail) inspection is **ANSI/ASQ Z1.4**, which is technically
identical to **ISO 2859-1** and MIL-STD-105E before it. It is widely used in
pharma for incoming components, packaging materials, and visual-inspection
attribute checks. (For measurement data rather than counts, the variables
counterpart is ANSI/ASQ Z1.9 / ISO 3951.)

The essential mindset: a sampling plan does not prove a lot is defect-free. It
bounds the **risk** of accepting a bad lot. Every plan is a documented risk
decision, and inspectors will ask for the rationale.

## How a Z1.4 plan is built

1. **Lot size + inspection level** determines a **sample size code letter**
   (A through R). General inspection level II is the default; level I samples
   less (lower discrimination), level III samples more. Special levels S-1 to
   S-4 take small samples for costly or destructive tests.
2. **Code letter + chosen AQL** gives the **sample size (n)** and the
   **acceptance (Ac) / rejection (Re)** numbers from the master table.
3. Inspect the n units: observed defects at or below Ac means accept; at or
   above Re means reject.

Example shape of a plan (values from the Z1.4 single-sampling normal table;
verify against the standard before use):

| Code letter | Typical lot range (level II) | Sample size | At AQL 0.65: Ac/Re | At AQL 2.5: Ac/Re |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F | 51-90 | 20 | 0/1 | 1/2 |
| J | 501-1,200 | 80 | 1/2 | 5/6 |
| L | 3,201-10,000 | 200 | 3/4 | 10/11 |
| N | 35,001-150,000 | 500 | 7/8 | 21/22 |

Note the pattern: bigger lots get bigger samples, but the *proportion* sampled
shrinks - the plan's protection comes from lot-to-lot consistency, not from
counting a fixed percentage.

## AQL is a risk decision

The **Acceptable Quality Limit** is the worst tolerable *process average* that
the plan will accept most of the time. It is not a target for defect rate, and
it does not guarantee that every accepted lot meets it - it defines the quality
level the plan routinely lets through. A lower AQL is stricter.

Defects are classified because their risks differ:

| Defect class | Meaning | Typical AQL choice (industry practice, site-defined) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Could harm the patient or render the product unusable or non-compliant. | Very low, often 0.1-0.65, frequently with Ac = 0. |
| Major | Likely to cause failure, complaints, or reduced usability. | Commonly 0.65-1.5. |
| Minor | Cosmetic or unlikely to affect function. | Commonly 2.5-4.0. |

The specific AQLs above are common industry practice, not mandated values. The
defensible approach is to derive them from risk assessment: what defect rate
in this class could hurt the patient, trigger a complaint, or break a
downstream process?

## OC curves, conceptually

Every sampling plan has an **operating characteristic (OC) curve**: the
probability of accepting a lot plotted against the lot's true defect rate. Two
points frame the risk conversation:

- At the **AQL**, a normal plan accepts with high probability - the
  **producer's risk** of rejecting good product is small.
- At the **LTPD** (lot tolerance percent defective, the quality level you
  really do not want), acceptance probability is low but not zero - the
  remaining chance of accepting bad product is the **consumer's risk**.

Steeper curves (larger samples, stricter levels) separate good lots from bad
lots more sharply but cost more inspection. Choosing a plan is choosing where
on this trade-off you sit. A plan with Ac = 0 looks strict, but on small
samples its OC curve can still pass lots with meaningful defect rates - check
the curve, not just the Ac number.

## Switching rules

Z1.4 section 9 defines **normal / tightened / reduced** switching:

- Start at **normal**.
- A defined pattern of non-acceptance forces **tightened** inspection (more
  protection for the customer).
- A sustained history of acceptance under stable production can earn
  **reduced** inspection (smaller samples), with defined rules for reverting.

Skipping these rules is a common audit finding: sites run "normal" inspection
forever regardless of lot history, which means the plan never responds to
deteriorating supplier or process quality.

## When sampling is not the answer

- **Sterility, identity, safety-critical attributes**: a sample result cannot
  release the uninspected units; these rely on process control and validated
  systems, with testing as confirmation.
- **Low, stable, well-characterized processes**: variables sampling (Z1.9),
  parametric release, or statistical process control may give more information
  per unit of effort.
- **New or unstable suppliers/processes**: tighten or move to 100% inspection
  until the data earn a sampled approach.

## Common mistakes and inspection findings

- AQLs copied from a template with no risk rationale.
- Using "Ac = 0" as if it guaranteed defect-free lots.
- Inspecting a convenience sample (top of the box) instead of a representative
  random sample.
- No switching-rule history; normal inspection regardless of past failures.
- One AQL applied to all defect classes.
- Sampling by fixed percentage ("10% of every lot"), which weakens protection
  on small lots and wastes effort on large ones.
- Inspectors not qualified for the defect library they are scoring.
