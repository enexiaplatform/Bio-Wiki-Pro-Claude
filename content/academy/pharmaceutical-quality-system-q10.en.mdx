# The Pharmaceutical Quality System (ICH Q10)

ICH Q10 (adopted 2008; FDA guidance published 2009) describes a model
**Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS)**: the framework that holds all the GMP
pieces together across the entire product lifecycle, from pharmaceutical
development to product discontinuation. It builds on ISO 9000 quality concepts,
complements regional GMP requirements, and connects ICH Q8 (pharmaceutical
development) and ICH Q9 (quality risk management) into one operating model.

The practical answer to the auditor's question "show me your quality system" is
not a binder of SOPs. It is these elements working together: monitoring feeding
CAPA and change management, all of it reviewed by management, with knowledge
and risk thinking running through everything.

## Three objectives

1. **Achieve product realization**: establish, maintain, and deliver products
   with the quality attributes appropriate to meet patient and regulatory
   needs.
2. **Establish and maintain a state of control**: develop and use effective
   monitoring and control systems for process performance and product quality.
3. **Facilitate continual improvement**: identify and implement improvements
   to products and processes.

## Four PQS elements

| Element | What it does | Concrete examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process performance and product quality monitoring system | Provides assurance that processes remain capable and in control. | Trend analysis, control charts, continued process verification, APR/PQR data. |
| Corrective action and preventive action (CAPA) system | Addresses deviations, complaints, OOS, audit findings - and prevents recurrence. | Structured root-cause investigation, actions with owners, effectiveness checks. |
| Change management system | Ensures changes are evaluated, approved, and implemented without breaking the validated state. | Change control with quality risk assessment and regulatory evaluation. |
| Management review of process performance and quality | Leadership governance of the PQS itself. | Scheduled reviews of metrics, audit outcomes, CAPA health, resource adequacy. |

Two **enablers** run through all four elements:

- **Knowledge management**: capturing and using what the organization learns
  from development through commercial life, so decisions at year ten can draw
  on data from year one.
- **Quality risk management (ICH Q9)**: proportionate, science-based
  assessment that decides where monitoring, CAPA, and change effort go.

## The lifecycle model

Q10's key insight is that the same four elements apply at every stage - with
different emphasis:

| Lifecycle stage | What the PQS elements look like there |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmaceutical development | Monitoring = experiments and design space exploration; CAPA = learning from failed development batches; change management = capturing design intent. |
| Technology transfer | Monitoring = comparability data between sites/scales; change management = transfer protocols; knowledge management = transferring process understanding, not just documents. |
| Commercial manufacturing | Full-strength operation: process monitoring and CPV, deviation/CAPA, change control, management review. |
| Product discontinuation | Monitoring = stability and complaints to end of shelf life; change management = retention, documentation, and registration wind-down. |

Knowledge and control should carry forward across these stages, not restart
each time. Sites that treat transfer as "send the batch records" rather than
"transfer the knowledge" discover the gap during their first commercial
deviation.

## Management responsibility

Q10 is explicit that **senior management owns the PQS**. Its named
responsibilities include:

- defining and communicating the **quality policy** and quality objectives;
- ensuring the PQS is defined, implemented, and maintained;
- providing **adequate resources** - people, facilities, equipment, training;
- conducting or overseeing **management review** of process performance,
  product quality, and the PQS itself;
- acting on review outcomes, including escalation of supply and quality risks.

Quality is therefore not "the QA department's problem." QA administers the
system; leadership is accountable for it. Inspection findings about resource
starvation, ignored metric trends, or rubber-stamp management reviews land on
management, not on the QA manager who flagged them.

## How Q10 relates to GMP

Q10 does not replace regional GMP (21 CFR 210/211, EU GMP Part I); it models
how a quality system can *exceed* minimum GMP across the lifecycle. FDA has
stated that ICH Q10 describes a comprehensive model that complements CGMP
requirements. Sites often map their existing systems - deviations, CAPA,
change control, APR, self-inspection - against the Q10 diagram to find
disconnections: monitoring that never triggers CAPA, CAPA that never feeds
management review, changes that bypass risk assessment.

## PQS maturity signals

A quick self-diagnostic for where your site sits:

- **Siloed**: deviations, CAPA, change control, and APR each have an SOP, but
  data does not flow between them; the same root cause recurs across systems.
- **Connected**: monitoring triggers CAPA, CAPA triggers change control, and
  management review sees all of it in one picture.
- **Improving**: the system generates proposals - process capability gains,
  specification tightening, monitoring optimization - and tracks them to
  closure, not just fixing what breaks.

Q10's continual-improvement objective is the difference between the last two:
a compliant site stays in control, a mature site gets better while in control.

## Common mistakes and inspection findings

- The four elements exist on paper but operate in silos; monitoring data never
  reach CAPA or change management.
- Management review is a slide-deck ritual with no decisions, actions, or
  resource outcomes recorded.
- Knowledge from development (design space, failure modes) is lost at
  transfer and relearned through deviations.
- Continual improvement is claimed but no improvement proposals are generated
  or tracked.
- QRM is applied inconsistently - formal for some changes, absent for others,
  with no proportionality rationale.
- The PQS stops at commercial manufacturing; discontinuation-phase stability
  and complaint monitoring are unmanaged.
