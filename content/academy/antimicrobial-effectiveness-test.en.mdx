# Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing (USP 51)

Multi-dose and most non-sterile aqueous products contain a **preservative
system** to control microorganisms introduced during manufacture or — more
importantly — by the patient during repeated use. Antimicrobial Effectiveness
Testing (AET, also called preservative efficacy testing) is the compendial
proof that this system works. The governing chapters are **USP &lt;51&gt;
Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing** and **Ph. Eur. 5.1.3**, which use the
same logic with different criterion tables.

## How the Test Works

The product is inoculated directly with a panel of five challenge organisms, at
a final concentration between **1 x 10^5 and 1 x 10^6 CFU per mL** of product.
The inoculated containers are held at controlled room temperature, and viable
survivors are enumerated at defined intervals — **7, 14, and 28 days** under
USP &lt;51&gt;. Results are expressed as log reductions from the initial
calculated count.

| Organism | Type | ATCC reference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staphylococcus aureus | Gram-positive bacterium | 6538 |
| Pseudomonas aeruginosa | Gram-negative bacterium | 9027 |
| Escherichia coli | Gram-negative bacterium | 8739 |
| Candida albicans | Yeast | 10231 |
| Aspergillus brasiliensis | Mould | 16404 |

The panel spans the classic contamination risks for aqueous products; labs may
add justified organisms (e.g. relevant isolates), but the five compendial
strains are the baseline.

## Product Categories and Criteria

USP &lt;51&gt; divides products into four categories by route and risk, each
with its own required performance:

| Category | Products | Bacteria requirement (summary) | Yeast/mould requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Injections and other parenterals, ophthalmics, sterile nasal, otic | ≥ 1.0 log at 7 days, ≥ 3.0 log at 14 days, no increase from day 14 at 28 days | No increase from initial at 7, 14, 28 days |
| 2 | Topical aqueous products, nonsterile nasal | ≥ 2.0 log at 14 days, no increase from day 14 at 28 days | No increase from initial at 14 and 28 days |
| 3 | Oral aqueous products (not antacids) | ≥ 1.0 log at 14 days, no increase from day 14 at 28 days | No increase from initial at 14 and 28 days |
| 4 | Aqueous antacids | No increase from initial at 14 and 28 days | No increase from initial at 14 and 28 days |

Compendial "**no increase**" is defined precisely: not more than **0.5 log10
higher** than the previous value measured. Parenteral and ophthalmic products
(Category 1) demand the fastest, deepest kill — they carry the highest patient
risk.

Ph. Eur. 5.1.3 expresses the same idea through **criteria A and B**: criterion
A is the recommended efficacy target, while criterion B is acceptable where A
cannot be achieved for justified reasons (for example, when the preservative
concentration needed for A would raise toxicity concerns). The Ph. Eur. tables
also add earlier evaluation points (6 and 24 hours for parenteral/ophthalmic
bacteria under criterion A).

## Neutralization: The Step That Makes the Data Valid

At each pull, surviving organisms must be recovered *without* the preservative
continuing to act during plating. If residual preservative carries into the
recovery step, organisms are killed on the plate and the test overstates
efficacy. Standard neutralization approaches:

- **dilution** below the preservative's effective concentration;
- **membrane filtration** with rinsing;
- **chemical neutralizers** — e.g. lecithin and polysorbate 80 for common
  preservative classes.

The neutralization scheme itself must be validated: demonstrate the neutralizer
both stops the preservative's activity and is not toxic to the challenge
organisms. Skipping this verification invalidates every log-reduction number
downstream.

## When AET Is Required

- **Development**: to design and justify the preservative system, typically at
  the lowest effective concentration.
- **Registration and release justification**: as part of the product's quality
  dossier for preserved multi-dose and aqueous non-sterile products.
- **Stability**: preservatives degrade and migrate into closures — efficacy at
  **end of shelf life** is what the patient actually experiences, and
  compendia expect it to be demonstrated (often as part of stability or
  development studies).
- **Change control**: formulation, preservative level, or container changes
  that could affect preservation.

The formulation goal is the **minimum effective preservative level**: enough to
meet the category criteria across shelf life, not a milligram more —
over-preservation is a toxicity problem, not a safety margin.

## Practical Controls at the Bench

- Verify the actual inoculum delivered per container; the recovered initial
  count at time zero anchors every log-reduction calculation.
- Test the product in its market container where feasible — headspace,
  closure contact, and fill volume all affect preservation.
- Hold inoculated containers at the compendial temperature, protected from
  light; do not refrigerate or shake.
- Count in duplicate with dilutions that stay countable at every interval,
  including the low survivor levels expected at 28 days.

## Common Mistakes and Inspection Findings

- No neutralizer validation — recovery numbers inflated by on-plate kill.
- AET performed only at release, never at end of shelf life.
- Wrong category applied (e.g. topical criteria used for an ophthalmic).
- "No increase" read as "roughly similar" instead of the compendial 0.5 log
  definition.
- Inoculum level outside the compendial 10^5–10^6 CFU/mL window, or not
  verified per container.
- Challenge strains not traceable to the compendial culture collection, or
  over-passaged.
- Preservative level reduced for cost without a new AET.
