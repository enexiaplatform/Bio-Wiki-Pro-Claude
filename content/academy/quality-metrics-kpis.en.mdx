# Quality Metrics & KPIs

Quality metrics translate the health of a quality system into numbers that
leadership - and increasingly regulators - can track. FDA's quality metrics
program (draft guidance 2015, revised draft 2016) proposed collecting site-level
metrics such as lot acceptance rate, product quality complaint rate, and
invalidated OOS rate as signals of manufacturing quality, partly to inform
risk-based inspection scheduling. Although the mandatory reporting program was
not finalized as drafted, the metrics themselves - shaped with industry through
the ISPE Quality Metrics initiative - became common practice. ICH Q10's
management-review element expects this kind of measurement: you cannot review
what you do not measure.

## Leading vs lagging indicators

The single most useful design distinction:

| Type | What it tells you | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lagging | What already went wrong; the outcome. | Lot acceptance/rejection rate, complaint rate, recall count, OOS rate, regulatory observations. |
| Leading | Where the system is straining before failure. | Right-first-time rate, deviation reporting rate, overdue CAPA/deviation rate, repeat CAPA rate, training currency, audit finding recurrence. |

Lagging metrics are necessary but late: by the time complaints rise, the
process has been drifting for months. Leading metrics give you the chance to
act while the problem is still cheap. A mature dashboard carries both, paired.

## A practical core set

- **Lot Acceptance Rate (LAR)**: accepted lots as a share of lots started - the
  basic output measure.
- **Right-First-Time (RFT)**: batches completed with no deviation or rework; a
  strong process-health signal and the natural partner of LAR.
- **Invalidated OOS rate**: share of OOS results concluded as laboratory error.
  FDA explicitly flagged this one: a high rate suggests testing-into-compliance
  or weak investigations, not a strong lab.
- **Deviation reporting rate and closure timeliness**: both volume (too low is
  as suspicious as too high) and aging.
- **CAPA health**: on-time closure, overdue count, and recurrence rate - CAPAs
  that close on paper but reopen in the next audit.
- **Complaint rate** (per units distributed) and **recall events**: the voice
  of the patient.
- **Training currency**: percent of staff current on required training.

Definitions must be fixed and documented - numerator, denominator, data source,
and counting rules - before the first data point. Metrics whose definitions
shift every quarter are worse than no metrics.

## What FDA actually proposed

The 2016 revised draft guidance proposed three site-level metrics, worth
knowing because they shaped industry dashboards:

| Proposed metric | Definition (as drafted) |
| --- | --- |
| Lot acceptance rate | Accepted lots divided by lots started in the reporting period. |
| Product quality complaint rate | Complaints received divided by lots distributed. |
| Invalidated OOS rate | OOS results invalidated as lab error divided by total OOS results, for finished product and stability testing. |

The reporting program was not finalized as drafted, but the definitions remain
a useful, regulator-fluent starting vocabulary for your own dashboard.

## Choosing metrics that matter

A good metric passes four tests:

1. **Linked to quality risk**: it tracks something that could hurt the patient
   or product, not something merely easy to count.
2. **Actionable**: a named owner can act when it moves. A metric nobody can
   influence is decoration.
3. **Hard to game**: single metrics tied to rewards invite manipulation;
   balanced pairs (LAR with RFT; deviations reported with complaints) expose
   cosmetic improvement.
4. **Trendable**: stable definition and enough volume to show a signal.

Ten well-governed metrics beat fifty orphaned ones. Every KPI needs an owner,
a target or control limit, a review cadence, and an escalation path when it
drifts.

## The gaming trap

The moment a metric drives a bonus or a hard target in isolation, behavior
bends toward the metric: deviations go unrecorded ("not a real deviation"),
OOS results get reclassified, CAPAs get closed with cosmetic actions to hit
the due date. Guard against it:

- pair output metrics with behavior metrics;
- treat a suspiciously perfect trend as an audit trigger, not a trophy;
- review metric definitions and counting rules in the audit program;
- make escalation safe - a culture where raising a deviation is punished will
  produce beautiful dashboards and bad product.

## Governance: from numbers to decisions

Metrics earn their keep in review forums:

- **Monthly operational review**: area-level metrics, deviations and CAPA
  aging, emerging trends.
- **Management review (ICH Q10)**: the cross-site quality picture, resource
  implications, systemic actions - with decisions and owners recorded.
- **Annual Product Review / Product Quality Review**: product-specific trends
  feeding specifications, process improvements, and regulatory commitments.

Statistical thinking matters here: distinguish common-cause noise from a real
signal (control-chart logic) before reacting. Overreacting to noise creates
churn; underreacting to signals creates recalls.

## Common mistakes and inspection findings

- Metrics reported but never reviewed, or reviewed with no actions recorded.
- Definitions that change silently, destroying trend continuity.
- Targets gamed by narrowing definitions ("that batch doesn't count").
- All lagging indicators; the first sign of trouble is the complaint.
- Invalidated-OOS rate high and unquestioned.
- CAPA on-time closure at 100% while the same findings recur every audit.
- Site metrics disconnected from APR/PQR and management review.
