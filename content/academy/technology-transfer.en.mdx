# Technology Transfer (Process)

Technology transfer moves a product and its manufacturing process - together
with the knowledge needed to reproduce it reliably - from development to
commercial manufacturing, or from one manufacturing site to another. It is
high-stakes because it sits at the exact point where the industry's two most
common failure patterns meet: knowledge that was never written down, and
processes that were never as robust as their documentation claimed. A failed
transfer surfaces months later as OOS trends, yield erosion, or a PPQ that
cannot pass.

The governing framework:

- **ICH Q10 (Pharmaceutical Quality System, 2008)** - names technology
  transfer as one of the four lifecycle elements of the PQS, spanning
  development-to-manufacturing and site-to-site transfers.
- **WHO TRS 961, Annex 7 (Guidelines on transfer of technology in
  pharmaceutical manufacturing, 2011)** - the most operational public
  framework: SU/RU model, transfer protocol contents, documentation packages.
- **FDA Process Validation guidance (2011)** - transfer batches link directly
  into Stage 2 Process Performance Qualification at the receiving site.
- **EU GMP Chapter 4 and Annex 15** - documentation and qualification
  expectations at the receiving site.
- **ISPE Good Practice Guide: Technology Transfer** - detailed industry
  practice for governance and knowledge packages.
- For the analytical half: **USP &lt;1224&gt; Transfer of Analytical
  Procedures** and **FDA/EU method-transfer expectations** - see the
  [Analytical Method Transfer lesson](/library/analytical-method-transfer).

## The Sending Unit / Receiving Unit Model

WHO TRS 961 Annex 7 frames every transfer as two parties with distinct
accountabilities:

| | Sending Unit (SU) | Receiving Unit (RU) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owns | Process and product knowledge: development reports, CQAs, CPPs, control strategy, validated methods, specifications, historical data | Facility, equipment, utilities, local operations, and the demonstration that the process runs correctly there |
| Must provide | Complete knowledge package, training, on-site support, honest statement of known weaknesses | Gap assessment, qualified equipment and methods, trained staff, transfer protocol execution, comparability data |
| Success looks like | The RU can reproduce the process without the SU in the room | Predefined acceptance criteria met on qualification batches, feeding PPQ and routine manufacture |

Both parties need a named **transfer team** with a project manager, technical
leads (process, analytical, engineering), QA from both sides, and regulatory
affairs. Transfers fail organizationally before they fail technically: unclear
decision rights between SU and RU QA is the classic one.

## The Knowledge Package: What Actually Moves

The SU's documentation package should include, at minimum:

- development and process-characterization reports (DoE, robustness, PAR
  studies where they exist);
- the **control strategy**: CQAs with specifications and rationale, CPPs with
  proven acceptable ranges, in-process controls;
- master batch record and process description at SU scale, with critical
  hold times;
- validated analytical methods, reference standards, and method-transfer
  history;
- raw-material specifications and approved supplier list, with any
  material-attribute sensitivities;
- equipment train description with critical design parameters (geometry,
  working volumes, shear, heating/cooling rates);
- process validation history, APR/PQR trend data, complaint and deviation
  themes;
- regulatory commitments: the registered process description and any filed
  ranges the RU must stay inside.

The last item quietly constrains everything: the RU does not have freedom to
"improve" the process outside registered parameters without a variation. That
is a regulatory question, not an engineering one.

## Gap Assessment: Finding the Variables Before They Find You

The gap assessment is a structured SU-vs-RU comparison; every difference is a
potential variable that must be controlled, justified, or included in the
qualification scope. Standard dimensions:

| Dimension | Typical gaps | Control approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equipment train and scale | Different granulator geometry, blender fill ratio, tablet press speed, heat-transfer area | Engineering comparability assessment; scale-up/down models; include in qualification batches |
| Utilities and environment | Water quality, HVAC class, compressed air, steam quality | Qualification evidence at RU; impact assessment vs process needs |
| Raw materials and suppliers | Same material, different supplier or grade; different excipient lots | Risk assessment; comparative testing; supplier qualification at RU |
| Analytical capability | Method never run at RU; different instruments, columns, software | Formal analytical method transfer per USP &lt;1224&gt; |
| People and tacit knowledge | SU operators know the "feel" of the process; RU does not | Person-to-person transfer: SU staff at RU for first batches, RU staff at SU beforehand |
| Procedures and documentation | SU tribal knowledge not in any SOP | Document gaps found during on-site observation before protocol freeze |
| Regulatory status | Filed parameters, site-specific commitments, market registrations | Regulatory strategy agreed before execution; variations filed where needed |

Score each gap for impact on CQAs (a Q9-style risk assessment works well) and
record the disposition: **close before transfer, control during transfer, or
accept with justification**. "No gaps found" on a complex transfer is a red
flag, not good news.

## The Transfer Protocol

The formal protocol - approved by both SU and RU **before execution** -
defines:

- scope (products, strengths, equipment trains, methods);
- responsibilities and decision/escalation paths, including joint QA
  disposition of deviations;
- the parameters and quality attributes to be compared, with **predefined
  acceptance criteria**;
- number and type of batches: engineering/demonstration batches (not for
  release) vs qualification batches (potentially releasable, feeding PPQ);
- sampling plans, test methods (post-transfer), and statistical comparison
  approach where applicable;
- deviation handling, success/failure decision rules, and the report
  structure;
- training plan and knowledge-transfer verification (how you will know the
  tacit knowledge moved).

Writing acceptance criteria after seeing the data is the single most
cited transfer-integrity failure. Criteria come from the control strategy and
SU historical performance - not from what the first RU batch happened to
deliver.

## Comparability and Acceptance Criteria

Comparability is demonstrated, not asserted. Typical evidence:

- **Process performance**: CPPs within proven acceptable ranges; yields,
  cycle times, and in-process results vs SU historical distributions.
- **Product quality**: CQAs (assay, content uniformity, dissolution,
  degradation products, physical attributes) meeting specification and
  statistically consistent with SU data where a comparison is pre-specified.
- **Analytical comparability**: the RU's QC lab must demonstrate it can run
  the methods - comparative testing on shared samples, co-validation, or
  re-validation per risk, per USP &lt;1224&gt; logic. A process transfer that
  forgets method transfer stalls at the RU's QC bench: you cannot release
  what you cannot reliably test.

Where statistics are used (equivalence tests, tolerance intervals, trend
comparison), pre-specify the method and criteria in the protocol. A result
outside criteria triggers **investigation**, never a quiet widening of
limits. If the RU genuinely cannot match SU performance, that is information
about the process's robustness - escalate it into the control strategy
discussion instead of forcing a pass.

## Linkage to PPQ and the Validation Lifecycle

Under FDA's 2011 process validation model, transfer qualification batches at
the RU typically constitute or feed directly into **Stage 2 Process
Performance Qualification** - see the
[Process Validation Stages lesson](/library/process-validation-stages).
Practical implications:

- Design transfer batches to PPQ standards from the start: enhanced sampling,
  defined CPP monitoring, releasability criteria.
- The transfer report and the PPQ report can be one document or linked
  documents - but the comparability conclusion must be explicit either way.
- After successful PPQ, the RU enters Stage 3 continued process verification
  with its own trending baseline; early RU batches deserve heightened
  attention in CPV because the transfer is precisely when latent differences
  surface.
- Changes introduced during transfer (equipment, scale, supplier) must be
  traceable through change control into the validation rationale.

## Regulatory Strategy

Site and process changes are post-approval changes in most markets. Align
early with regulatory affairs on:

- filing category per market (e.g. US PAS/CBE/annual-report logic, EU
  variation classification) for the site addition or process change;
- whether transfer batches can be commercially released in each market and
  when;
- stability commitments: transferred-product batches typically enter the
  stability program; significant process/site changes usually trigger
  commitment batches - see the
  [Ongoing Stability Program lesson](/library/ongoing-stability-program).

Discovering the filing requirement after the batches are made is an expensive
way to learn this section.

## Worked Example: Tablet Process to a Second Site (Fictional but Realistic)

An immediate-release tablet transfers from Site A (pilot-commercial) to Site
B (high-volume). The gap assessment finds: Site B's high-shear granulator has
different bowl geometry and tip speed; Site B uses a different magnesium
stearate supplier; Site B's QC has never run the dissolution method. Risk
assessment rates granulation endpoint and lubrication as high risk. Actions:
granulation endpoint transferred with a quantitative marker (power
consumption plus granule size distribution) rather than time; comparative
lubricant testing on SU lots precedes the transfer; the dissolution method is
transferred by comparative testing on three shared lots (analyst-to-analyst
and site-to-site agreement within pre-set limits). The protocol defines two
engineering batches and three qualification batches at commercial scale, with
acceptance criteria on blend uniformity, tablet hardness range, dissolution
profile similarity vs Site A historical data, and assay/degradants vs
specification. One qualification batch shows borderline dissolution at the
45-minute point; investigation traces it to over-lubrication from a longer
blending time, the lubrication step is re-specified, and a replacement batch
meets criteria - the deviation and resolution are documented in the transfer
report, which then supports the PPQ conclusion. Site B enters CPV with weekly
dissolution trending for the first three months.

## Common Failure Modes and What Inspectors Look For

1. **Tacit knowledge never transferred** - the process works only when the
   SU's senior operator is watching. Defense: person-to-person exchange, and
   verification that RU staff execute unaided on qualification batches.
2. **Acceptance criteria written or relaxed after seeing data.** Defense:
   protocol approved by both QAs before execution; changes only via
   deviation/change control with rationale.
3. **Gap assessment as a formality** - "no gaps" claimed despite different
   equipment and suppliers. Defense: structured dimensions, documented
   dispositions, QA challenge.
4. **Method transfer forgotten or done after process batches.** Defense:
   analytical transfer on the critical path with USP &lt;1224&gt;-aligned
   criteria; QC readiness as a protocol prerequisite.
5. **Engineering batches silently released** - demonstration material shipped
   without qualification status. Defense: batch disposition rules in the
   protocol; QA control of release status.
6. **Registered parameters breached** - RU "optimized" outside the filing.
   Defense: regulatory review of the transfer plan; variations filed first.
7. **No stability commitment for transferred product.** Defense: stability
   protocol linkage in the transfer plan.
8. **SU historical data never used** - comparability asserted without
   baseline. Defense: SU trend data extracted into the protocol as the
   comparison reference.
9. **Deviations resolved by SU and RU arguing** - no joint disposition
   authority. Defense: escalation and decision rules in the protocol.
10. **Transfer report without conclusions** - data dump with no comparability
    statement. Defense: report template requiring explicit criteria-vs-data
    disposition and PPQ linkage.
