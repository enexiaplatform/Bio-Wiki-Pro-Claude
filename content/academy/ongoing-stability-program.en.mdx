# Ongoing Stability Program

Registration stability studies prove the shelf life at approval. The
**ongoing stability program** proves the product *stays* stable in routine
commercial manufacture - through supplier changes, equipment replacements,
site transfers, and the slow drift of a process over years. It is the
quality system's early-warning radar for product already in patients' hands,
which is why inspectors treat it as a window into the whole PQS: a weak
ongoing program usually signals weak change control and weak trending
everywhere else.

The governing framework:

- **ICH Q1A(R2) (2003)** - stability testing of new drug substances and
  products; its post-approval sections define commitment and ongoing
  stability expectations.
- **ICH Q1E (2003)** - evaluation of stability data: statistical analysis,
  extrapolation, and the decision rules behind shelf-life claims.
- **ICH Q1D (2002)** - bracketing and matrixing designs for reduced testing.
- **EU GMP Chapter 6 (Quality Control)** - requires an ongoing stability
  program, written protocol, and monitoring throughout shelf life.
- **21 CFR 211.137 and 211.166 (US)** - expiry dating must be supported by a
  written stability testing program.
- **WHO TRS 1010, Annex 10 (2018)** - stability testing guidance widely used
  for global supply and climatic-zone IV markets.

## Registration, Commitment, and Ongoing Batches: Three Different Jobs

| Study type | When | Purpose | Typical commitment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Registration (primary) stability | Pre-approval | Establish shelf life / retest period for the dossier | Batches, conditions, and duration per ICH Q1A (e.g. long-term 25 °C/60% RH or 30 °C/75% RH, accelerated 40 °C/75% RH) |
| Commitment batches | Post-approval, per approval commitment | Confirm the commercial-scale / approved process on stability | Often the first three commercial batches per strength/pack, long-term conditions, as filed |
| Ongoing (annual) batches | Every year of commercial life | Monitor the routine process over time | At least one batch per year per product, strength, and container-closure system (unless none manufactured that year) |

The ongoing program also has **triggered inclusion**: significant changes
(raw-material supplier, process, equipment, site, pack), reprocessed or
reworked batches, and batches with major deviations may need to be added -
the decision and its rationale belong in
[Change Control](/library/change-control), documented before the batch ships.

## Protocol Design: What Must Be Written Down

EU GMP Chapter 6 expects a written protocol per product (or justified
grouping). A defensible ongoing-stability protocol contains:

- **Scope and selection rule**: products, strengths, packs; the
  one-batch-per-year rule; which batch is chosen (commonly the first
  commercial batch of the year - state the rule and follow it; cherry-picking
  a "good" batch is a data-integrity issue);
- **Storage conditions**: long-term per the labeled storage statement and
  climatic zone of the markets served; intermediate/accelerated only as
  triggered by significant change or OOS logic;
- **Test attributes**: the stability-indicating set - assay, degradation
  products, dissolution, water content, appearance, pH, preservative content,
  microbial attributes, container-closure functionality, as relevant to the
  dosage form;
- **Time points**: e.g. annually through shelf life plus end of shelf life -
  fewer points than registration studies is normal and accepted because the
  question is confirmation, not shelf-life setting; justify the spacing;
- **Methods**: current validated methods, with version control - method
  changes mid-program need comparability assessment;
- **Acceptance criteria**: the registered shelf-life specifications;
- **OOT/OOS handling**: predefined investigation triggers and the link to
  the OOT procedure and deviation system;
- **Reporting**: annual summary per product feeding the APR/PQR, with trend
  evaluation.

## OOT Statistics: Catching Drift Before Failure

Out-of-specification is binary and late. **Out-of-trend** analysis asks the
earlier question: is this result consistent with what this batch and this
product's history predict? Three approaches in increasing rigor, all
defensible if predefined:

1. **Within-batch rule-of-thumb limits** (site practice): e.g. a change from
   the previous time point beyond a justified delta (commonly derived from
   method variability, such as a multiple of the method's intermediate
   precision). Simple, transparent, weak at slow drifts.
2. **Historical tolerance / control limits**: trend the attribute across
   batches at matched time points and flag results outside statistical
   limits derived from product history - essentially SPC on stability data
   (see [Statistical Process Control & Trending](/library/statistical-process-control)).
3. **Regression with prediction intervals** (Q1E-consistent): fit the
   degradation model (often linear in time for assay and degradants) on the
   batch's data plus history, and flag points outside the prediction
   interval; also used to project when the batch or product would cross the
   specification - the formal basis for shelf-life projection and excursion
   impact assessment.

Whatever the method: define it in an SOP **before** you need it, define who
investigates and how fast, and connect confirmed OOTs to deviation
management. An OOT that is real but still within specification is not an OOS
- it is a signal demanding investigation and, often, projection: *at this
slope, when does this batch fail?*

## Bracketing and Matrixing (ICH Q1D)

When a product family is large, full testing of every strength/pack/time
point is often unnecessary. Q1D defines two reduced designs, both requiring
justification in the protocol (and, for commitment studies, often regulatory
agreement):

| Design | Principle | Valid when | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bracketing | Test only the extremes of a design factor - lowest and highest strength, smallest and largest container - and apply results to intermediates | Intermediates are truly bounded: same formulation family (identical or proportional composition), same closure system, extremes represent worst case | If the intermediate turns out worst (e.g. headspace effects in a mid-size container), the design fails - justify with science, not convenience |
| Matrixing | Test a selected subset of samples at each time point on a planned rotation, so every factor combination is covered across the schedule | The factors are shown not to interact strongly with degradation; enough data exist to support the design | Statistical analysis is more complex; a confirmed OOT in a matrixed design complicates inference; regulatory acceptance varies - check commitments |

Neither design may be used to quietly drop a known stability risk - a
strength with historical excursions, a pack with a permeability concern.
Q1D designs are about eliminating redundancy, not eliminating surveillance.

## Roles and Documents

- **QC stability group**: chamber management, pulls within defined windows,
  testing, data entry. Pull windows (e.g. ± days around each time point -
  site-defined) must be in the protocol; late pulls without assessment are a
  common finding.
- **Analysts/reviewers**: result verification, OOT screening per SOP.
- **QA**: protocol approval, OOS/OOT oversight, excursion impact decisions,
  APR linkage.
- **Regulatory affairs**: commitment tracking, notification obligations when
  stability affects the dossier or labeled shelf life.

Documents produced: the program SOP, per-product protocols, chamber
qualification and monitoring records, pull/test records, OOT/OOS
investigations, annual stability summaries per product, and the APR/PQR
section that trends them.

## Worked Example: OOT Degradant at 24 Months (Fictional but Realistic)

A film-coated tablet, 36-month shelf life, degradant specification
NMT 0.5%. Ongoing batch #2023-14 shows 0.31% at 24 months against a
historical pattern of roughly +0.05%/year from 0.10% at release - the
expected value is about 0.20%. The OOT SOP's regression-with-prediction-
interval rule flags the result.

Investigation sequence: (1) **confirm the data** - no lab error (standard,
system suitability, re-preparation verified; this is a hypothesis to test,
not a ritual); (2) **characterize the signal** - review the batch's other
time points and attributes, the chamber records (any temperature/RH
excursion during storage?), the method version history; (3) **scope the
impact** - which distributed batches share formulation, process, supplier,
and pack, and are at or beyond this age?; (4) **project** - fit the
degradation slope including the flagged point and estimate when batches
cross 0.5%; (5) **assess patient risk** - is the degradant qualified at the
projected levels, or does ICH Q3B qualification territory get breached?;
(6) **decide** - continue monitoring with increased frequency, shorten
shelf life, notify customers, or recall, in escalating order of risk.

In this fictional case, the root cause is a chamber temperature-mapping
drift at one shelf position - the batch itself is unaffected, no field
action is needed, and the outcome is a chamber requalification plus an OOT
procedure tweak to review chamber records first. The point of the exercise:
the ongoing program caught a signal at 0.31% - years before any 0.5%
failure - with time to think.

## When Results Drift Toward Failure

A confirmed stability failure or a credible projection of failure on
marketed product is a quality event with regulatory consequences:

- investigate under the OOS/deviation system (see
  [OOS Investigation Deep Dive](/library/oos-investigation-deep-dive) and
  [Out-of-Trend Investigation](/library/out-of-trend-investigation));
- assess all implicated distributed batches;
- inform regulatory affairs early - shelf-life changes, field alerts, and
  recalls have market-specific notification duties and timelines;
- feed the outcome into change control (what changed to cause this?) and
  into the [Annual Product Review](/library/annual-product-review).

The worst possible outcome is not a recall - it is discovering at month 30
that a change made at month 0 was never put on stability, so the first
signal arrives on product already consumed. That is the specific failure
the ongoing program exists to prevent.

## Common Failure Modes and What Inspectors Look For

1. **No ongoing program at all, or "we put batches on but never trend."**
   EU GMP Chapter 6 findings classically note the absence of written
   protocols and of any evaluation of results. Defense: protocol per
   product, annual summary, APR linkage.
2. **Changes never trigger stability inclusion** - supplier/site/process
   changes approved without stability assessment. Defense: mandatory
   stability question in change control.
3. **Chamber excursions ignored** - temperature/RH alarms with no product-
   impact assessment. Defense: excursion SOP with documented impact
   decisions; qualified, mapped, monitored chambers.
4. **Late or missed pulls without assessment.** Defense: defined pull
   windows, deviation for breaches.
5. **OOT not defined** - first sign of drift is the OOS itself. Defense:
   predefined OOT method in an SOP with investigation duties.
6. **Bracketing/matrixing used to drop known risks.** Defense: Q1D
   justification reviewed by QA and, where committed, regulators.
7. **Method changes mid-program with no comparability.** Defense: method
   version control plus bridging data.
8. **Batch selection gamed** - the "best" batch chosen for the annual
   study. Defense: a written selection rule (e.g. first commercial batch of
   the year) followed and auditable.
9. **Failures investigated only as lab error until time runs out.**
   Defense: full Phase II investigation covering process, storage, and
   market impact - see the OOS lesson.
10. **Stability data invisible to the APR** - annual review repeats the
    specification table without trend evaluation. Defense: stability
    trending is a standing APR section.
