# Calibration & Metrology Programs

Every number that supports a quality decision — a chamber temperature, a batch
weight, an autoclave pressure, a pH at release — is only as trustworthy as the
instrument that produced it. A GMP calibration program is the system that makes
that trust provable: an inventory, a schedule, traceable standards, defined
tolerances, and a disciplined response when something fails.

The regulatory hooks are the equipment clauses of the GMPs (21 CFR 211.68,
211.160; EU GMP Chapter 3 and Annex 15 for the qualification interface) and,
for the calibration laboratories themselves, **ISO/IEC 17025** accreditation.

## Program Design: Start with Criticality

Not every instrument deserves the same rigor. Classify the inventory by what
each measurement feeds:

| Criticality | Examples | Program treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical (GMP decision) | Release balances, stability chamber probes, autoclave temperature/pressure, pH meters for specifications | Full traceable calibration, defined tolerance, OOT impact assessment |
| Supporting | Monitoring sensors that inform but do not decide, utility gauges | Calibration or verification at justified intervals |
| Non-critical / indication only | Convenience thermometers, general-area gauges | Clearly labeled as not for GMP decisions, or removed |

The classification must be written down with its rationale. An unlabeled gray
zone — where an "indication only" thermometer is quietly used to judge a
deviation — is a classic finding.

## Traceability

Calibration must be **traceable to a national or international measurement
standard** (NIST, NMI equivalents, SI units) through an **unbroken chain of
comparisons**, each with a stated measurement uncertainty. In practice that
means:

- working standards calibrated by an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory;
- certificates on file showing the standard used, its uncertainty, and its own
  due date;
- reference standards themselves on a schedule — the chain breaks if your
  reference thermometer is two years overdue.

"Checked against another instrument on the bench" is not calibration unless
that instrument's own traceability is documented.

## Tolerance Must Fit the Use

The calibration tolerance derives from the **process requirement**, not the
instrument's spec sheet. The rule: the instrument's tolerance, plus its
measurement uncertainty, must be at least as tight as the window it controls —
ideally with margin (a test accuracy ratio of 4:1 is common industry practice).

An incubator that must hold 36.5 +/- 1.0 deg C cannot be verified with a
thermometer good to +/- 2 deg C. Write the acceptance limits into the
calibration procedure so the pass/fail decision is made against the use, not
against whatever the device happens to achieve.

## Intervals, Labels, and As-Found Data

- **Intervals** are set risk-based and then refined by history: if an
  instrument is repeatedly found near its tolerance limit, shorten the
  interval; if years of as-found data show stability, an extension may be
  justified — with data, not convenience.
- **Identification and status**: unique ID, calibration label with due date,
  and a controlled "out of service" state. An overdue instrument still in use
  on a release decision is a finding waiting to happen.
- **As-found / as-left data**: record the reading *before* adjustment. As-left
  data alone hides drift; as-found data is what powers interval review and OOT
  assessment.

## Out-of-Tolerance: The Impact Assessment

Finding an instrument out of tolerance is not an event to recalibrate away.
The structured response:

1. **Quarantine** the instrument and record the as-found deviation precisely —
  how far out, in which direction, at which points.
2. **Bound the window**: every measurement since the last successful
  calibration is suspect.
3. **Assess impact** on each affected use: which batches, tests, environmental
  readings, or released results depended on it? Use the as-found error and its
  direction — a balance reading 0.5% high under-reported every weight by 0.5%.
4. **Decide and document**: no impact (with evidence), re-testing, batch
  assessment, deviation/CAPA, or escalation to field action. QA disposition is
  required.
5. **Fix the system**: adjust or repair, recalibrate, review the interval, and
  check whether sister instruments share the failure mode.

The impact assessment is the step teams skip, and the step inspectors ask for
first. A drifting reference thermometer found at annual calibration can
retrospectively poison a year of stability chamber data.

## Calibration vs Qualification vs Maintenance

Three related but distinct disciplines are often merged on the schedule:

- **Qualification** (DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ) proves the instrument is installed and
  performs for its intended use — done at commissioning and after major
  change.
- **Calibration** proves its measurements are traceably accurate — repeated
  on an interval.
- **Preventive maintenance** keeps it mechanically sound — lamps, seals,
  lubrication, filters.

A calibrated instrument can still be unqualified for a new use, and a
qualified instrument drifts out of calibration on schedule. Track all three
states separately, with requalification triggers defined for repairs and
relocations.

## Common Inspection Findings

- Instruments in GMP use with no calibration status or expired labels.
- Tolerances copied from the instrument manual, never reconciled with the
  process requirement.
- No as-found data recorded — adjustment without evidence of drift.
- OOT found, instrument recalibrated, no look-back at affected product or data.
- Working standards used past their own calibration due dates.
- Intervals that never change despite years of drift evidence.
- Criticality classifications undocumented or contradicted by actual use.
