# Dissolution Testing (USP 711)

Dissolution testing measures how much drug substance releases from a dosage
form into solution over time under standardized conditions. For oral solid
products it is a critical quality attribute and a recognized in-vitro surrogate
for bioavailability — which is why it appears in nearly every tablet and
capsule monograph. The governing compendial chapter is **USP &lt;711&gt;
Dissolution** (harmonized with Ph. Eur. 2.9.3 and JP).

## The Apparatus

USP &lt;711&gt; defines four apparatus; two dominate routine QC.

| Apparatus | Name | How it works | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Basket | Dosage unit held in a rotating wire-mesh basket | Capsules, beads, units that float or disintegrate into floating debris |
| 2 | Paddle | Rotating paddle stirs the medium; unit lies at the vessel bottom | Tablets — the most widely used apparatus |
| 3 | Reciprocating cylinder | Unit moves vertically through rows of media | Modified-release and bead formulations |
| 4 | Flow-through cell | Medium pumped continuously past the unit | Poorly soluble drugs, implants, special forms |

The method for a given product specifies apparatus, rotation speed (common
settings are 50–100 rpm, set by the monograph), medium composition and volume
(frequently 500–1000 mL), and sampling times.

## The Conditions That Must Be Controlled

USP &lt;711&gt; fixes the key physical conditions tightly:

- **Temperature: 37 deg C +/- 0.5 deg C** in each vessel throughout the run.
- **Medium deaeration** where required — dissolved gases form bubbles on the
  dosage unit and basket mesh, slowing wetting and biasing results low.
- **Vessel centering, shaft verticality, and wobble** — verified through
  mechanical qualification of the dissolution bath.
- **Sampling position and time tolerance** — withdrawals at the specified
  height, at times within the compendial tolerance.

Small deviations in any of these change the release profile. That is why
dissolution baths are mechanically qualified and why "the bath was a degree
cold" is an investigation, not a shrug.

## Q and the S1/S2/S3 Acceptance Logic

The **Q value** is the specified percentage of labeled content that must be
dissolved at the stated time — a single-point release criterion. USP &lt;711&gt;
then stages the test so that borderline products are judged on more units
rather than passed or failed on six.

For immediate-release dosage forms (Acceptance Table 1 of USP &lt;711&gt;):

| Stage | Units tested | Acceptance criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | 6 | Each unit is not less than Q + 5% |
| S2 | 6 more (12 total) | Average of 12 units is equal to or greater than Q, and no unit is less than Q - 5% |
| S3 | 12 more (24 total) | Average of 24 units is equal to or greater than Q, not more than 2 units are less than Q - 5%, and no unit is less than Q - 10% |

Modified-release and delayed-release products use their own acceptance tables
with level/time-point logic. The point of the staging is statistical: S1 is a
fast screen for clearly good product, while S2 and S3 protect both the patient
(no single badly under-releasing unit slips through) and the manufacturer (a
single odd unit does not fail a good batch).

## Sink Conditions

A valid dissolution method must maintain **sink conditions**: the medium volume
and composition keep the dissolved drug far below its saturation solubility —
practically, the medium should be able to dissolve at least about three times
the total drug load. Without sink conditions, the test measures solubility
limitation, not dosage-form release, and it cannot discriminate between good
and bad product.

For poorly soluble drugs this drives method design: larger volumes, surfactants
such as sodium lauryl sulfate, or pH adjustment — each justified during method
development, not improvised at the bench.

## Profiles and the f2 Similarity Factor

Beyond single-point QC release, full **dissolution profiles** (multiple time
points) are how you judge whether a formulation, site, scale, or process change
altered release behavior. The standard comparison tool is the **f2 similarity
factor** per FDA's 1997 SUPAC guidance (SUPAC-IR/MR): f2 between 50 and 100
indicates similar profiles, given adequate time points and variability limits.
Profile comparison is a development and change-control tool; the Q/S1–S3
scheme is the release tool. Do not confuse the two.

## Immediate, Delayed, and Extended Release

The Q/S1–S3 scheme above is the immediate-release logic. USP &lt;711&gt;
carries separate acceptance tables for the other families:

- **Delayed-release (enteric)**: two-tier testing — an acid stage (typically
  0.1 N HCl for 2 hours, during which no unit may substantially dissolve)
  followed by a buffer stage with its own Q-based acceptance table.
- **Extended-release**: multiple time points with per-time-point acceptance
  ranges, judging the release profile level by level instead of a single Q
  point.

## Bath Qualification

Dissolution results depend on the mechanical state of the bath, so labs
maintain **mechanical qualification** (shaft verticality, wobble, vessel
centering, rotation speed, temperature uniformity) on a schedule, with the
USP Performance Verification Test using reference standard tablets where
applicable. A failed batch on an unqualified bath is an unanswerable
question.

## Common Mistakes and Inspection Findings

- Skipping or shortcutting medium deaeration for methods that need it.
- Using Apparatus 2 for a formulation that floats or congeals without a
  justified modification (e.g. sinker use documented in the method).
- Vessels not mechanically qualified — wobble, misalignment, and speed errors
  invisible to the analyst but visible in the data's variability.
- Applying S2/S3 logic incorrectly, e.g. averaging units at S1 instead of
  requiring each unit to meet Q + 5%.
- Temperature verified only at run start, not maintained at 37 +/- 0.5 deg C
  throughout.
- Filters adsorbing drug at sample withdrawal, silently biasing early
  time-points low — filter compatibility must be demonstrated.
- Treating an OOS dissolution result as a lab error to be re-tested away
  instead of following the OOS procedure.
