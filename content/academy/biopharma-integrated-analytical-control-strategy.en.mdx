# Biopharma Integrated Analytical Control Strategy

An analytical control strategy is not a list of instruments or a release specification
with more rows. It is the product-specific measurement system
that connects what may matter about the product to where a decision is made,
what each procedure can detect, how results remain comparable, and who owns
the conclusion.

This workflow supplies no product-specific method panel, reportable range,
system-suitability criterion, specification, acceptance criterion, reference
value, sampling plan, validation design, or regulatory conclusion.

## Decision question

> For this product, process, presentation, lifecycle stage, and intended
> decision, which attributes require measurement; what complementary
> analytical evidence can detect relevant differences; where should each
> control sit; and what remains unresolved before a result, specification, or
> lifecycle change can be accepted?

## Applicability and do-not-use boundary

This workflow is designed primarily for well-characterized proteins and
polypeptides within the applicable scope of ICH Q6B. The Q2(R2) and Q14
principles apply to analytical procedures used for commercial release and
stability testing and can be applied elsewhere in a risk-based,
phase-appropriate way.

Do not apply it unchanged to:

- cell or gene therapies, viral vectors, mRNA products, vaccines,
  plasma-derived products, oligonucleotides, ADCs, or other modalities with
  materially different product and assay systems;
- defining a registered specification, acceptance criterion, reference value,
  validation protocol, transfer protocol, batch disposition, or filing action;
- replacing qualified product, bioassay, statistics, quality, or regulatory
  review with a score generated by this workflow;
- treating the Q6(R1) concept paper as an effective revised guideline;
- assuming that an official biological standard is automatically suitable as
  the product-specific primary or working reference material.

The structure can be adapted, but modality, product, process, matrix, assay,
reference, lifecycle, region, and reviewer evidence must be rebuilt.

## Controlled source map

| Source | Decision use | Important boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ICH Q6B | Characterization, biological activity, purity, impurities, quantity, reference materials, and specification principles for products in scope | Q6B supplies neither a universal method panel nor product-specific acceptance criteria; Q6(R1) remains revision activity |
| ICH Q2(R2) | Validation strategy and performance evidence based on intended use | Validation characteristics and study designs depend on the measurement objective, technology, matrix, and reportable result |
| ICH Q14 | ATP, development knowledge, robustness, analytical procedure control strategy, lifecycle change, and bridging | Platform methods and prior knowledge require science- and risk-based applicability; the guideline does not approve a procedure |
| ICH Q5E | Orthogonal and sensitive analytical comparison after a manufacturing change | Release conformance or analytical identity alone does not establish comparability |
| ICH Q5C | Stability-indicating profile, potency, reference material, and lifecycle stability evidence | It does not define a product-specific test panel, schedule, criterion, or shelf life |
| WHO biological reference standards recommendations (2026) | International biological reference-standard establishment, use, stewardship, and traceability context | A WHO International Standard does not replace product-specific in-house reference qualification or define a release specification |
| ICH Q9(R1) and Q10 | Risk-based decision quality, knowledge, monitoring, change, and lifecycle governance | Risk ranking does not create evidence or approve a control |

## Separate seven evidence objects

Do not compress analytical readiness into one score.

| Evidence object | Question | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attribute and relevance | What product, impurity, variant, contaminant, functional property, or performance feature could affect quality, safety, efficacy, process consistency, or stability? | A named CQA is a hypothesis requiring product-specific evidence, not an automatic release test |
| Intended analytical decision | What result must the procedure support: characterization, process understanding, IPC, release, stability, comparability, investigation, or another controlled use? | One procedure may not be fit for every use or matrix |
| Procedure capability | What does the method measure, distinguish, miss, or confound, and over what demonstrated performance space? | Validation does not compensate for a poorly framed purpose or blind spot |
| Reference and critical-reagent system | Which material, cell system, reagent, calibration model, or control establishes comparability and reportability? | A certificate or assigned unitage alone does not prove suitability, stability, or commutability for this procedure |
| Control placement and specification basis | Where is the attribute controlled, and why is a test included, omitted, replaced, or monitored? | A specification is one part of the total control strategy, not full characterization |
| Lifecycle state and change | What procedure, site, instrument, reagent, reference, process, product, or filing change can affect the result or decision? | Transfer, bridging, revalidation, and regulatory impact are separate evidence questions |
| Reportable result and accountable decision | Which controlled data become the reported value, how is atypical performance handled, and who accepts the conclusion? | A calculated result is not automatically reportable or acceptable |

## Build the attribute-to-decision evidence chain

### 1. Freeze product, lifecycle, and decision scope

Record the product and presentation, drug-substance or drug-product stage,
process and site versions, markets, lifecycle stage, sample matrix, intended
decision, accountable owner, and controlled references. A development
characterization question is not the same as a commercial release decision.

### 2. Build the product-quality attribute map

For each attribute, preserve:

- the desired product or intended activity;
- structural or physicochemical properties and heterogeneity;
- biological activity or potency questions;
- product-related substances, product-related impurities, and degradation
  products;
- process-related impurities and contaminants;
- quantity, formulation, delivery, or other product-specific performance;
- patient, product, process, and stability relevance;
- observed, mechanistic, platform, nonclinical, clinical, manufacturing, and
  regulatory evidence, including contradictions and uncertainty.

Do not label every measured feature critical. Do not drop an attribute only
because current lots appear consistent. The decision depends on relevance,
process capability, analytical detectability, lifecycle knowledge, and
residual risk.

### 3. State the intended analytical purpose before choosing technology

An analytical target profile, when used, describes the intended purpose,
attributes to be measured, and relevant performance characteristics and
criteria. It should drive technology choice rather than being reverse-written
to justify the instrument already available.

Separate procedures used for:

- extensive characterization and structure-function understanding;
- development and process characterization;
- in-process control or monitoring;
- drug-substance and drug-product release;
- stability and degradation detection;
- comparability after change;
- investigation, identification, or orthogonal confirmation.

A method qualified for characterization may still be unsuitable for release;
a validated release method may be too narrow for comparability.

### 4. Design complementary analytical coverage

Complex biological products often require more than one measurement principle
to cover the same broad attribute. Record the unique decision contribution and
blind spot of each procedure across identity, structure, heterogeneity, purity,
impurities, biological activity, quantity, and product-specific performance.

Orthogonal does not mean “two instruments.” It means the procedures contribute
meaningfully different evidence. Two methods sharing the same sample-preparation
bias or response mechanism may repeat the same blind spot.

### 5. Treat potency as a product and assay-system question

Map the intended biological activity, relevant mechanism or functional step,
procedure format, reference relationship, critical reagents or cells,
calibration model, sample matrix, system suitability, reportability gates, and
known sources of variability. Where more than one function may matter, record
which functions are measured, bridged, controlled elsewhere, or unresolved.

Neither binding alone nor a cell-based assay label proves mechanism relevance.
The procedure and reference system require product-specific scientific and
statistical justification.

### 6. Separate variants, impurities, contaminants, and analytical artifacts

Preserve the terminology and decision basis for desired product,
product-related substances, product-related impurities, degradation products,
process-related impurities, and contaminants. A peak, band, particle, or signal
is an observation until identity, method behavior, origin, relevance, and
control implications are supported.

Avoid converting a relative area, below-quantitation result, or method-specific
response directly into mass, clearance, safety, or clinical meaning without a
justified bridge.

### 7. Build the reference-material and critical-reagent hierarchy

For each intended use, record material identity, tier, source, lot, assigned
property or value, traceability, characterization, homogeneity, stability,
storage, handling, in-use period, qualification, bridging, inventory,
replacement trigger, and owner. For biological procedures, include cells,
reagents, controls, and calibration-model versions that materially affect the
reported result.

An international or national biological standard supports standardization for
its defined property and use. It does not automatically represent the complete
product, replace an in-house primary or working reference system, or transfer a
product-specific specification.

### 8. Justify control placement and specification logic

For every attribute, show whether and how it is addressed through raw-material
control, process design, parameter control, IPC, characterization, release,
stability, continued verification, or another lifecycle control. Then document
the evidence for including, omitting, replacing, or changing a specification
test and acceptance criterion.

Specification conformance is not full product characterization. Conversely,
not every characterized attribute belongs in routine release testing. A test
can be omitted from release only when the total control strategy and residual
risk support that decision for the product and region.

This workflow supplies no default specification or acceptance criterion.

### 9. Demonstrate procedure fitness and reportability

Connect the intended purpose to development knowledge, risk assessment,
robustness, procedure parameters, sample and standard preparation, system
suitability, calibration, calculation, replication, range, validation evidence,
data processing, atypical-run handling, and reportable-result rules.

Predefine what makes a run technically valid, what makes a result reportable,
and what requires investigation or escalation. Do not average away failed
system suitability, non-comparable curves, reference instability, or an
unresolved analytical interference.

### 10. Manage transfer, monitoring, and change as lifecycle decisions

Track procedure performance and relevant reference, reagent, analyst,
instrument, site, sample, and product signals. For a change, determine whether
the ATP or intended purpose is affected; what performance characteristics and
control elements may change; what representative samples and reference
materials are required; and whether bridging, comparative testing, partial or
full revalidation, transfer, filing, or specification assessment is needed.

Method replacement is not a file revision. It is a decision about whether the
new measurement remains fit for the intended product and control purpose.

## Decision table

| Evidence condition | Defensible action | Do not claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product relevance, method capability, reference system, control placement, and lifecycle evidence converge | Propose the bounded analytical strategy for qualified review | A universal panel or specification |
| A release test passes but orthogonal characterization or stability shows a new signal | Assess product, process, sample, method, reference, and lifecycle evidence | That specification conformance proves unchanged product quality |
| An attribute is well controlled by process evidence and routine monitoring | Evaluate whether release testing adds decision value, with regional and residual-risk review | That process capability automatically permits specification removal |
| A method is sensitive but poorly specific for the decision | Add complementary evidence or redesign the measurement strategy | That sensitivity alone makes the result meaningful |
| A new working reference lot is proposed | Bridge identity, assigned property, suitability, stability, and procedure response against the controlled reference hierarchy | That a certificate or nominal concentration establishes equivalence |
| A potency assay curve or system fails reportability gates | Investigate the assay and sample evidence under the controlled procedure | A relative potency result from an invalid or non-comparable run |
| A procedure or site changes | Assess intended purpose, affected performance, representative samples, reference materials, bridging, revalidation, transfer, and specification impact | That unchanged procedure name means no lifecycle impact |
| Evidence or reviewer coverage is incomplete | Mark evidence-required or review-required and preserve the open decision | A controlled analytical, specification, or batch conclusion |

## Worked example: fictional resin change, reference transition, and analytical signal

A fictional liquid CHO monoclonal antibody is preparing a polishing-resin
supplier change. Post-change engineering material meets the current release
specification but shows a shifted charge profile and a small change in a
cell-based potency trend. At the same time, the working reference lot is
approaching replacement and the potency procedure is being transferred to a
second laboratory.

The team does not attribute the signal to the resin, reference, method, or
product. It maps the evidence separately:

| Decision area | Evidence held | Open evidence | Accountable role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attribute relevance | Existing structure-function and process knowledge | Identity and relevance of the shifted charge population; relationship to functional activity | Product quality and analytical development |
| Method portfolio | Release, characterization, and stability procedures with controlled versions | Orthogonal charge evidence, interference review, method sensitivity and comparability across versions | Analytical development |
| Potency | Current method history and selected transfer runs | Curve comparability, critical-reagent and cell state, reference response, receiving-lab performance, product contribution | Bioassay and statistics |
| Reference system | Current primary and working reference records | New-lot characterization, assigned-property bridge, stability, commutability within intended uses, transition controls | Reference-standard owner and quality |
| Process evidence | Defined resin change and selected development data | Representative process and impurity evidence, pre/post-change scope, other concurrent differences | Downstream process development/MSAT |
| Specification | Current approved tests and criteria | Whether the observed attribute is adequately controlled and whether any filing or specification impact exists | Quality and regulatory CMC |
| Decision | Draft cross-functional evidence map | Qualified comparability, method transfer, reference transition, validation, and implementation decisions | Change owner and quality unit |

The example remains unresolved. It provides no actual product result, method
parameter, reference value, criterion, specification change, comparability
conclusion, transfer acceptance, filing category, or batch decision.

## Working asset

Use the **Biopharma Analytical Control Strategy Evidence Map** to maintain:

- one frozen product and decision scope;
- attribute relevance and control-placement records;
- method purpose, lifecycle use, capability, blind spots, validation, and
  reportability;
- specification-inclusion, omission, or change rationale;
- reference-material, critical-reagent, and bridging records;
- lifecycle changes, monitoring signals, representative evidence, actions,
  commitments, and reviewer roles;
- formula-driven completeness and qualified-review flags.

The blank workbook fails closed as `Evidence required`. A structurally complete
record can only reach `Qualified review required`; it never becomes approval.

## Limitations

- This workflow does not define product CQAs, methods, ATP criteria, procedure
  parameters, validation experiments, system suitability, reportable ranges,
  specifications, acceptance criteria, reference values, or sampling plans.
- It does not establish product identity, purity, potency, impurity safety,
  reference commutability, analytical equivalence, stability indication,
  comparability, validation, transfer, regulatory acceptability, or batch
  disposition.
- It does not reproduce licensed pharmacopoeial procedures or acceptance
  criteria.
- It does not convert platform knowledge, development data, a certificate,
  official unitage, specification conformance, or one analytical signal into a
  product-specific conclusion.
- The fictional example is a usability demonstration, not a benchmark.
- Atlas must not call this content SME-reviewed until appointment and review
  evidence are recorded.
