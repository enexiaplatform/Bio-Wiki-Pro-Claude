# Good Documentation Practice (GDocP)

Good Documentation Practice is the day-to-day discipline that makes GMP records
trustworthy. Regulators in every region work from the same premise: if an
activity is not documented correctly, it is treated as if it never happened.
The governing framework includes EU GMP Part I Chapter 4 (Documentation),
FDA 21 CFR Part 211 Subpart J (Records and Reports), WHO GMP guidance on good
data and record management practices (2016), the MHRA GxP Data Integrity
Guidance (2018), and FDA's Data Integrity and Compliance With Drug CGMP
questions-and-answers guidance (2018).

GDocP turns those expectations into behavior: what you write, when you write
it, how you correct it, and how blank documents are controlled. The ALCOA+
acronym - long attributed to FDA inspector Stan Woollen's training material
and now embedded in MHRA and WHO guidance - is the standard checklist.

## ALCOA+ applied to paper records

| Attribute | Question it answers | Paper-record practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attributable | Who recorded it, and when? | Sign or initial each entry with date; use a signature log for initials. |
| Legible | Can it be read years later? | Indelible ink (blue or black per site SOP); no pencil; consistent units and formats. |
| Contemporaneous | Was it recorded when the work happened? | Record at the time of the activity, not from memory at end of shift. |
| Original | Is this the first capture or a verified true copy? | Record directly into the controlled record, not onto scrap paper for later transcription. |
| Accurate | Is it correct, complete, and error-free? | Verify calculations; second-person check where defined; correct errors openly. |
| Complete | Is all data retained, including repeats and failed runs? | Keep raw data, printouts, and re-runs; no cherry-picking. |
| Consistent | Do time stamps and sequences make sense? | Logical date/time order; instrument clocks synchronized. |
| Enduring | Will the record survive the retention period? | Durable media; thermal printouts photocopied or scanned where they fade. |
| Available | Can it be retrieved for review, audit, or inspection? | Controlled storage; retrievable within a defined timeframe. |

A record does not need to be perfect; it needs to be honest, complete, and
traceable. Most data-integrity findings are not exotic fraud - they are
routine GDocP failures repeated at scale.

## Writing rules for paper records

- Use indelible ink. Pencil, erasable ink, and correction fluid are prohibited
  on GMP records.
- Record entries in real time. If an unusual circumstance prevents
  contemporaneous recording, document what happened and when the entry was
  actually made.
- Never leave a required field blank. If a field does not apply, enter N/A
  with initials and date so nobody can add data later.
- Sign only for work you performed or personally verified. Pre-signing,
  signing for a colleague, or back-dating is falsification.
- Do not record data before the activity exists. A time stamp ahead of the
  work is a classic red flag in an audit trail or record review.
- Record actual values, not rounded or assumed ones. If the balance reads
  10.02 g, write 10.02 g - not 10 g.

## Correcting an error the right way

EU GMP Chapter 4 requires that corrections leave the original entry readable
and record who made the change, when, and why. The compliant method:

1. Draw a **single line** through the incorrect entry so it remains legible.
2. Enter the correct value nearby.
3. Initial and date the correction.
4. Add a brief reason (for example: "transcription error", "wrong unit
   entered"). A generic reason on every correction is itself a warning sign.

Prohibited: overwriting, erasing, scratching out until unreadable, correction
fluid or tape, and re-writing the whole page to make it look clean. Each of
these hides the original data and is treated as a data-integrity breach.

A correction made by someone other than the original recorder should be
justified and traceable - some sites require a supervisor countersignature;
define the rule in the site SOP and follow it.

## Controlled forms, logbooks, and blank documents

If a blank form can be photocopied freely, records can be substituted or
re-created. That is why inspectors pay close attention to blank-document
control. A defensible system includes:

- **Version-controlled masters**: every form template has a document number,
  version, and effective date managed through document control.
- **Controlled issuance**: bound, sequentially numbered logbooks and paginated
  batch records issued against a register, with page reconciliation ("page X
  of Y") so missing pages are detectable.
- **Reconciliation at closure**: issued versus used, voided, and returned
  forms are counted; discrepancies are investigated.
- **Void handling**: voided pages are marked, retained, and signed - never
  destroyed - so the record set stays complete.
- **Controlled copies**: stamped or colored-paper copies where uncontrolled
  photocopying would create a falsification risk.

The same logic applies to electronic blank forms and spreadsheets: uncontrolled
templates with unlocked calculation cells are the digital version of a
photocopiable blank form.

## Raw data and true copies

Raw data is the original record of the activity: the notebook entry, the
instrument printout, the balance ticket. When the original medium cannot be
retained (a fading thermal printout, for example), make a **true copy** that is
verified against the original, marked as a copy, and signed and dated by the
person verifying it. Transcribing data from scrap paper into the official
record destroys originality and is a common finding; if it ever happens,
retain the scrap as raw data and cross-reference it.

## Common inspection findings

- Corrections made with correction fluid, or single-line corrections with no
  date or reason.
- Entries in pencil, or ink colors that do not match the site procedure.
- Blank fields in batch records that were completed after the fact.
- Data recorded on loose paper, then transcribed into the logbook.
- Missing pages from numbered logbooks with no investigation.
- Uncontrolled photocopies of blank forms in circulation.
- Signatures that cannot be matched to a signature log.
- Time inconsistencies: a result recorded before the instrument run finished.
