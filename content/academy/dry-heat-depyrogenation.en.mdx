# Dry-Heat Sterilization & Depyrogenation

Dry heat does two distinct jobs for heat-stable items — glass vials, ampoules,
metal instruments, some closures and equipment parts. At lower temperatures and
longer times it **sterilizes**; at higher temperatures it **depyrogenates**,
destroying bacterial endotoxin that moist heat cannot reliably inactivate and
that washing cannot fully remove. For aseptic fill-finish, depyrogenation of
glassware is the critical application: endotoxin surviving on a vial interior
goes directly into the parenteral product, and no downstream step removes it.

The governing framework: **EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)** section 8 and the
sterilization principles of Ph. Eur. 5.1.1; **FDA Guidance for Industry:
Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing (2004)** and FDA's 1985
Guideline on the same topic (which established the 3-log expectation); **USP
general chapters on dry-heat sterilization and depyrogenation** (USP &lt;1228&gt;
family); and **USP &lt;85&gt;** for the endotoxin recovery method. PDA Technical
Report No. 3 addresses dry-heat validation practice.

## Sterilization vs Depyrogenation: Two Different Bars

| Property | Dry-heat sterilization | Depyrogenation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target | Viable microorganisms (spores worst case) | Bacterial endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) |
| Typical conditions | 160–180 °C for defined holds (e.g., 170 °C ≈ 1 h; 160 °C ≈ 2 h per Ph. Eur. 5.1.1) | Commonly ≥250 °C for a defined hold in ovens; seconds-to-minutes dwell at 300+ °C in tunnels |
| Challenge device | Biological indicators (spores) | Endotoxin indicators (purified LPS) |
| Acceptance | Demonstrated sterility assurance level (e.g., 10^-6 SAL with biological indicator kill) | ≥3-log (1000-fold) reduction of the endotoxin challenge |
| Key physical limit | Heat transfer to the load | Thermal inactivation kinetics of LPS — much harder to destroy than spores |

The relationship is asymmetric: a validated depyrogenation cycle delivers far
more lethality than sterilization requires, so depyrogenation *includes*
sterilization. The reverse is false — a 170 °C sterilization cycle does **not**
reliably destroy endotoxin. State the cycle's purpose in the protocol before
choosing the challenge device.

## FH: The Lethality Currency

Analogous to steam sterilization's F0, **FH** integrates time-temperature
exposure into a single lethality number. The convention in USP &lt;1228.1&gt;
practice is a **reference temperature of 170 °C with a z-value of 20 °C** for
depyrogenation calculations (industry practice — confirm against your
validation SOP and the current USP text):

- Every measured temperature profile is converted to "equivalent minutes at
  170 °C."
- The coldest measured location's FH must meet or exceed the FH shown, by
  endotoxin indicator studies, to deliver ≥3-log reduction.
- For sterilization-only cycles, the same FH math with biological indicators
  (typically *Bacillus atrophaeus* spores, formerly *B. subtilis* var. niger,
  ATCC 9372) demonstrates microbial kill.

The z-value of 20 °C for endotoxin inactivation is much larger than the 10 °C
used for steam's F0 — reflecting how much more thermally resistant endotoxin is
than spores. This is why depyrogenation demands such aggressive cycles.

## The Validation Workflow

### Batch oven

1. **Heat distribution, empty chamber**: thermocouple mapping to establish the
   heating profile and locate cold spots.
2. **Heat distribution and penetration, loaded**: repeat with maximum and
   minimum load configurations; penetration probes inside representative items
   (wrapped loads heat far slower than the chamber air).
3. **Cold-spot confirmation**: identify the slowest-to-heat, slowest-to-cool
   positions — typically center of dense loads, bottom of the load, positions
   shadowed from circulation.
4. **Endotoxin indicator studies**: vials or carriers inoculated with a defined
   endotoxin load (commonly ≥1000 EU per indicator, providing measurable
   headroom above the 3-log claim) placed at cold spots and representative
   locations; temperature probes adjacent to correlate FH with achieved log
   reduction.
5. **Three consecutive successful runs** per load configuration: every
   indicator at worst case shows ≥3-log reduction; positive (unprocessed)
   controls recover the expected challenge within the validated recovery range;
   negative controls show no contamination.
6. **Report**: FH at cold spot, log reductions achieved, load configurations
   qualified, routine monitoring parameters.

### Continuous tunnel

Tunnels sterilize and depyrogenate glassware on a conveyor through heating and
cooling zones, feeding the filling line directly — the tunnel exit is part of
the Grade A sterile boundary.

1. **Belt mapping**: temperature distribution across belt width and zone;
   penetration studies in the container mass.
2. **Worst case = fastest belt speed** the process permits (shortest dwell) at
   the lowest permitted zone temperatures; also qualify the slowest speed for
   container thermal stress if relevant.
3. **Endotoxin indicators** travel on the belt through the worst-case
   positions (belt edges vs center).
4. **Aseptic boundary**: HEPA integrity over the sterilizing, cooling, and exit
   zones; pressure cascade toward the wash side (positive differential vs the
   infeed); particulate and viable monitoring of the exit environment.
5. **Interrupt studies**: defined behavior on belt stoppage — how long a
   stopped belt can sit before containers are rejected or diverted, validated
   against glass temperature decay.

## Endotoxin Indicators vs Biological Indicators

| Aspect | Endotoxin indicator (EI) | Biological indicator (BI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analyte | Purified lipopolysaccharide (e.g., from *E. coli*) | Spores of *Bacillus atrophaeus* (ATCC 9372 typical for dry heat) |
| What it proves | Endotoxin destruction (the harder target) | Microbial kill (sterilization) |
| Detection | BET/LAL recovery per USP &lt;85&gt;; log reduction vs unprocessed controls | Growth/no-growth after incubation; D-value characterization |
| Use case | Depyrogenation cycles (glassware, components) | Sterilization-only cycles; often run alongside EIs in depyrogenation studies |
| Recovery control | Unprocessed EI must recover the claimed challenge within validated range | Enumeration of spore population on the carrier |

Critical honesty point: a perfect temperature trace proves heat was delivered —
it does not prove endotoxin was destroyed. The EI data are the depyrogenation
claim. Sites have failed inspections on exactly this gap: FH calculations
impeccable, endotoxin reduction never directly measured, or measured with an
unvalidated recovery method (LAL extraction efficiency from the carrier must be
established).

## Endotoxin Indicator Handling

EIs are analytical challenges, and their handling is part of the validation:

- **Spiking**: purified LPS (commonly an *E. coli* reference endotoxin) applied
  to vials, discs, or coupons at a defined load (≥1000 EU typical) and dried
  under controlled conditions; the dried state is the challenge the cycle must
  destroy.
- **Recovery method**: extraction of residual endotoxin from the carrier and
  measurement per USP &lt;85&gt; (kinetic turbidimetric/chromogenic or gel-clot
  as justified); the extraction efficiency from that specific carrier material
  must be validated — glass, metal, and closures extract differently.
- **Controls**: unprocessed spiked indicators (positive recovery controls) must
  recover the claimed challenge within a validated range (commonly ±0.5 log —
  site-defined); processed-negative controls confirm no recontamination.
- **Calculation**: log reduction = log(recovered positive control) −
  log(recovered processed indicator); every cold-spot EI must meet ≥3-log.

## Routine Control After Validation

- **Ovens**: calibrated temperature recording each cycle, defined load
  configurations (no unvalidated loads), periodic requalification and
  re-mapping after maintenance (fans, HEPA, door seals, sensors).
- **Tunnels**: continuous zone temperature and belt-speed recording with
  alarms; HEPA integrity testing at defined intervals; differential pressure
  monitoring across zones; routine particulate/viable monitoring at the exit;
  belt-speed interlocks that prevent out-of-spec containers reaching the filler.
- **Container-side verification**: incoming glass endotoxin and bioburden
  control is still required — depyrogenation is validated against a defined
  incoming challenge, not an unlimited one.

## Change Control Triggers

Requalification or targeted verification may be needed after:

- heating element, circulation fan, HEPA, or sensor replacement;
- changes to load configuration, container type, or container size;
- belt, conveyor, or zone-modification work on tunnels;
- changes to cycle setpoints, belt speed, or alarm windows;
- repeated cycle deviations, adverse EI/temperature trends, or long shutdowns;
- changes to the LAL method or EI preparation used for periodic verification.

The question is always the same: could this change alter the lethality delivered
at the cold spot, or the aseptic boundary at the tunnel exit?

## Common Failure Modes (What Inspectors Find)

1. **Temperature-only "depyrogenation" claims.** FH data without endotoxin
   indicator studies. Defense: EIs at cold spots, every validation run.
2. **Unvalidated LAL recovery.** Endotoxin spiked on carriers recovered with an
   extraction method never shown to work. Defense: validate recovery
   efficiency as part of the study.
3. **Cold spot assumed, not mapped.** Studies placed probes where convenient.
   Defense: loaded heat-distribution mapping first, EI placement from data.
4. **Tunnel exit treated as ordinary space.** HEPA over the cooling/exit zone
   never integrity tested; pressure cascade unmonitored. Defense: the tunnel
   exit is in the CCS as part of the Grade A boundary.
5. **Unvalidated belt stoppage.** A line stop leaves glass in the hot zone;
   containers later used without a documented disposition rule. Defense:
   validated interrupt scenarios with defined reject/divert logic.
6. **Load creep.** Production loads denser or configured differently than the
   validated maximum. Defense: load configuration diagrams in the batch
   record.
7. **Requalification skipped after maintenance.** Fan or sensor replacement
   without re-mapping. Defense: change-control triggers tied to
   requalification scope.
8. **Sterilization cycle sold as depyrogenation.** A 180 °C cycle claimed to
   depyrogenate glass. Defense: the cycle purpose statement and EI evidence
   must match the claim.

## Worked Example: Sizing a Tunnel Cycle

*Fictional but realistic.* A site must depyrogenate 3R vials feeding a filler
at up to 400 vials/min. Development data on the LPS lot used for indicators
show a D-value of approximately 2.5 minutes at 250 °C (z = 20 °C assumption —
site data govern). Targeting a 4-log margin over the 3-log requirement at the
cold spot:

- Required lethality at cold spot: 7 logs × 2.5 min ≈ 17.5 min equivalent at
  250 °C — expressed and recorded as FH (170 °C reference) per the site SOP.
- Belt mapping shows belt edges run 8 °C cooler than center; worst case set as
  fastest qualified belt speed with edge-travelling EIs.
- Validation runs: three consecutive runs, all edge and center EIs ≥3-log
  reduction (most ≥5-log), controls recovered within 0.5 log of the spike.
- Routine parameters locked: zone setpoints with alarm windows, belt speed
  interlock, HEPA integrity semi-annual, differential pressure alarm.
- The CCS records the cycle purpose (depyrogenation including sterilization),
  the incoming glass endotoxin limit, and the interrupt disposition rule.
