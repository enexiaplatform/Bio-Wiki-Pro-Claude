# Extractables & Leachables (E&L)

Every drug product touches materials — container-closure systems, delivery
devices, and increasingly single-use manufacturing equipment — and those
materials shed chemicals into it. Extractables and leachables (E&L) work is
the disciplined answer to two questions: what *could* come out of the
material (extractables), and what *actually does* come out into the product
the patient receives (leachables)?

The governing references are **USP &lt;1663&gt; Assessment of Extractables**
and **USP &lt;1664&gt; Assessment of Drug Product Leachables**, with
**USP &lt;1664.1&gt;** covering orally inhaled and nasal drug products, and
**USP &lt;665&gt;/&lt;1665&gt;** covering plastic components in manufacturing
(single-use systems). The risk philosophy traces to the **PQRI** (Product
Quality Research Institute) recommendations for inhalation products, which
introduced the threshold framework the whole field now uses. FDA expects E&L
data in NDAs/BLAs and ANDAs for applicable dosage forms; EU expectations run
through the Ph. Eur. and the guideline on plastic primary packaging.

## Extractables vs Leachables: The Core Distinction

| | Extractables | Leachables |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Compounds released from a material under exaggerated conditions (aggressive solvents, elevated temperature, extended time) | Compounds that migrate into the drug product under actual formulation, storage, and use conditions |
| Question answered | What is the worst-case universe of migrants? | What does the patient actually receive? |
| When performed | Material characterization, early development, component selection | Development and commercial lifecycle, on the actual product over shelf life |
| Relationship | Superset | Subset of extractables — and anything leachable not predicted by extractables demands investigation |
| Governing chapter | USP &lt;1663&gt; | USP &lt;1664&gt; |

A leachable found in product that was never seen in the extractables study
is a red flag: it means either the extraction missed a compound class, or
the compound formed by reaction with the formulation — either way the
extractables study did not bound the problem.

## Risk Ranking Drives Study Depth

Risk scales with route of administration, contact intimacy, and duration.
PQRI's framework, reflected in the USP chapters, ranks dosage forms by the
likelihood that packaging interacts with the dose and the clinical
consequence if it does.

| Dosage form | Interaction risk | Typical E&L expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inhalation aerosols (MDI/DPI) | Highest | Full extractables + long-term leachables, PQRI thresholds, per-component assessment |
| Injectable / ophthalmic solutions | High | Full extractables + leachables over shelf life, AET-based identification |
| Topical / transdermal / nasal (non-inhaled) | Medium | Risk-based extractables, targeted leachables |
| Oral liquids | Medium | Screening extractables, targeted leachables for known migrants |
| Oral solids (blister, HDPE bottle) | Lower | Supplier data + documented justification often suffices |
| Single-use process components | Varies with process step proximity | USP &lt;665&gt; extractables + process-equipment-related leachables (PERLs) assessment |

Layer material factors on top: polymer type and additive package, gamma or
e-beam sterilization (which generates new extractables), surface-area-to-
volume ratio, formulation solvent power (a lipophilic or surfactant-rich
formulation extracts far more than water), and storage time/temperature.

## The Threshold Framework: SCT to AET

PQRI defined safety-based thresholds so that analytical work focuses where
it matters:

- **Safety Concern Threshold (SCT)** — the daily intake below which a
  leachable presents negligible safety concern even if unidentified. For
  inhalation products PQRI set 0.15 µg/day; for parenterals the commonly
  applied value is 1.5 µg/day (aligned with the ICH M7 TTC logic). Treat
  these as the published PQRI/USP reference values; confirm against current
  guidance for your product type.
- **Qualification Threshold (QT)** — the level below which a *non-genotoxic*
  leachable need not be further qualified (PQRI: 5 µg/day for inhalation).
- **Analytical Evaluation Threshold (AET)** — the SCT converted into a
  product concentration, so analysts know the level at or above which a
  chromatographic peak must be identified and toxicologically assessed.

```
AET (µg/g product) = SCT (µg/day) / daily dose (g or mL product/day)
```

*Worked example (fictional product, real arithmetic).* A parenteral with SCT
1.5 µg/day and a maximum dose of 10 mL/day: AET = 1.5 / 10 = **0.15 µg/mL**.
Because screening methods (GC-MS, LC-MS with generic response factors) carry
response-factor uncertainty, the practice is to divide by an uncertainty
factor — commonly 2 (i.e. ~50% response uncertainty): working AET ≈
**0.075 µg/mL**. Any screening peak at or above that level must be
identified, quantified against an authentic or surrogate standard, and
toxicologically assessed. This is what makes the study tractable: thousands
of potential peaks collapse to the handful above threshold.

## Designing the Extractables Study (USP &lt;1663&gt;)

1. **Define the purpose** — material selection, vendor comparison, or input
   to the leachables study; the purpose sets the extraction rigor.
2. **Characterize the material** — polymer, additives, processing aids,
   sterilization mode, surface-area-to-extraction-solvent-volume ratio.
3. **Choose extraction conditions** — solvents spanning polarity (e.g. water,
   alcohol/water mix, isopropanol or hexane for lipophilic extraction),
   time and temperature justified as exaggerated but not destructive.
4. **Analyze with complementary platforms** — GC-MS for volatiles and
   semi-volatiles, LC-MS (±) for non-volatiles, ICP-MS for elemental
   extractables, and often headspace GC for the most volatile fraction. No
   single platform sees everything.
5. **Report against the AET** — identify and semi-quantify everything above
   the working AET; list below-threshold signals as an inventory.

The analytical platform table below shows why "complementary" is not a
buzzword:

| Platform | Compound domain | Typical blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headspace GC-MS | Volatiles (residual solvents, small monomers) | Anything non-volatile |
| GC-MS (liquid injection) | Semi-volatiles (antioxidants, plasticizers) | Thermally labile or polar non-volatiles |
| LC-UV-MS (ESI ±) | Non-volatiles, oligomers, degradants | Non-ionizing compounds; response-factor spread |
| ICP-MS | Elemental extractables (metals from catalysts, glass, pigments) | Organic compounds entirely |
| Ion chromatography | Ionic species (acids, halides, sulfates) | Neutral organics |

A material that shows "clean" on one platform may shed a whole compound
class visible only to another.

## Extraction Conditions: Exaggerated, Not Destructive

Extraction design is a balancing act. Conditions must be aggressive enough
to reveal everything the material can plausibly release — but conditions
that melt, dissolve, or chemically degrade the polymer produce artifacts
(degradation products of the polymer itself) that never occur in use and
drown the study in false compounds. Document for each extraction: solvent
and why its polarity bracketing is relevant, temperature and duration,
surface-area-to-volume ratio, and whether the material's physical integrity
was preserved. Soxhlet, reflux, and sealed-vessel incubation are all
acceptable when justified; "we always use isopropanol at 50 °C" is not a
justification.

## Designing the Leachables Study (USP &lt;1664&gt;)

1. **Derive target leachables** from the extractables profile plus any
   formulation-reaction products anticipated chemically.
2. **Test the actual product** — real formulation, real container, real
   storage (accelerated arms are acceptable early; real-time data close the
   study), at intervals across shelf life including end-of-shelf-life.
3. **Use validated methods** where targets are known — the screening
   method's job is to catch the unexpected; validated quantitative methods
   confirm the known targets.
4. **Assess toxicologically** — every leachable above the AET gets a
   safety assessment against its daily intake; structure-activity tools and
   read-across fill data gaps, documented per ICH M7 principles where
   mutagenicity is a question.
5. **Close the loop** — if a leachable trends upward toward its
   qualification threshold, the study feeds specification decisions, shelf-
   life justification, or a material change.

## E&L and the Contamination Control Strategy

For sterile and biologic products, E&L is not a standalone study — it is an
input to the site's contamination control strategy (CCS) under EU GMP Annex
1 (2022). Single-use systems in the fluid path, filters, tubing, and
storage bags all contribute process-equipment-related leachables, and the
CCS should document how E&L risk was assessed for each contact material,
what supplier data (ideally to USP &lt;665&gt;/BPOG-style protocols) was
accepted, and where product-specific confirmation was required. A CCS that
lists single-use components with no E&L rationale is an inspection gap.

Single-use components deserve one extra note: they sit in the process, not
in the final container, so their migrants must survive downstream
processing (filtration, chromatography, formulation, filling) to reach the
patient. The risk assessment therefore weighs both the extractables burden
and the clearance the process provides — a biocontainer film with an
extensive extractables profile may still present negligible leachables risk
if the compound classes are cleared by the purification train. That
clearance argument must be made explicitly, with the process steps named,
not implied.

## Toxicological Assessment Workflow

Every compound above the AET passes through the same safety funnel:

1. **Identify** — confident structure (authentic standard or high-
   confidence MS/library match); unidentified compounds above the SCT are
   the worst outcome and drive method improvement.
2. **Compute the exposure** — concentration × maximum daily dose of
   product = µg/day intake.
3. **Classify the hazard** — screen for structural alerts (mutagenicity
   per ICH M7 principles); a genotoxic-alert compound is held to the SCT
   with no further latitude, a non-genotoxic compound to the QT.
4. **Assess against data** — literature tox data, read-across to structural
   analogues, or (Q)SAR; the assessment is signed by a qualified
   toxicologist.
5. **Decide** — acceptable as-is, monitor with a specification, mitigate
   (change material/supplier), or escalate. The decision and its basis go
   into the E&L report and, for registered products, the dossier.

## Documentation Package

A complete E&L file contains: material characterization and supplier
extractables data with gap assessment; the risk-ranking rationale per
dosage form; extraction-study protocols and reports with AET derivation;
leachables methods and validation; leachables data across shelf life;
toxicological assessments with sign-off; the leachables specification
rationale; and the CCS cross-reference for sterile products.

## Common Failure Modes (Inspection Themes)

- Extractables run on the resin or component *before* sterilization, while
  the product touches the gamma-irradiated version — the irradiation
  products are exactly the compounds of interest.
- AET calculated from the wrong SCT or the wrong daily dose, so the study
  screened at the wrong sensitivity.
- Leachables study that tests only T0 and one accelerated point — no
  end-of-shelf-life data for a 24-month product.
- Supplier extractables data accepted wholesale with no check that
  extraction solvents matched the formulation's solvent power.
- A leachable appearing in product that was absent from extractables,
  accepted without investigation of why.
- Semi-quantitation presented as quantitation — generic response factors
  used to claim a leachable is "below threshold" without uncertainty
  treatment.
- Single-use components in the aseptic path with no E&L assessment linked
  to the CCS.
- Toxicological assessment done by the lab without a qualified toxicologist
  sign-off.
