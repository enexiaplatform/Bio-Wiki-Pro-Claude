# Internal Audits & Self-Inspection

GMP expects manufacturers to inspect themselves. EU GMP Part I Chapter 9
(Self Inspection) requires self-inspections to monitor implementation of and
compliance with GMP, and to propose corrective measures. ICH Q10 positions
audit/self-inspection within the management-review element of the
pharmaceutical quality system, and FDA expects equivalent internal oversight
through the quality unit's responsibilities under 21 CFR 211.22 even though the
US regulations do not use the phrase "self-inspection."

The purpose is straightforward: find your own problems before a regulator,
customer, or - worst case - a patient does. A program that never finds
anything is not evidence of a perfect site; it is evidence of a blind one.

## Program design

A defensible self-inspection program defines, in an approved procedure:

- **Scope**: the full quality system over a planned cycle - premises, equipment,
  personnel, documentation, production, quality control, distribution,
  complaints and recalls, and the quality systems themselves (deviations,
  CAPA, change control, data integrity).
- **Risk-based schedule**: frequency weighted by product/patient risk,
  compliance history, and recent change. Sterile operations, aseptic
  processing, and data-integrity controls warrant more frequent attention than
  a low-risk warehouse. Full system coverage over one to three years is common
  industry practice; define and justify your own cycle.
- **Auditor pool**: trained auditors, independent of the areas they audit,
  with documented qualification (training in audit technique plus knowledge of
  the audited process).
- **Method**: checklists built from current regulations and SOPs - not last
  year's checklist recycled - plus interviews, record sampling, and direct
  observation of work as performed.

## Independence and evidence

An auditor cannot objectively audit their own work. Independence does not
necessarily mean a different company - it means the auditor has no direct
responsibility for the activity being audited. Larger sites use QA auditors or
cross-functional auditors from other departments; small sites sometimes use
qualified external auditors to fill the gap.

Findings must rest on **objective evidence**: a record, an observation, a
statement confirmed against a requirement. "Housekeeping seemed poor" is an
opinion; "three of five cleaned-equipment tags showed no cleaning date,
contrary to SOP CLN-004 section 6.2" is a finding.

## The audit, step by step

1. **Prepare**: define scope and criteria (which regulations, which SOPs),
   review the area's history (past findings, deviations, changes), build or
   refresh the checklist, and agree logistics with the auditee.
2. **Open**: a short opening meeting confirms scope, timing, and escorts.
3. **Gather evidence**: observe work as performed, interview staff at the
   point of work, and sample records backwards from a shipped batch or a raw
   data set - following the trail is more revealing than reading SOPs.
4. **Close**: present findings factually in a closing meeting; classify with
   the auditee present so there are no surprises in the report.
5. **Report**: findings cite the requirement, the objective evidence, and the
   classification - written so a reader two years later can reconstruct what
   was seen.
6. **Track**: CAPA owners, due dates, and effectiveness checks live in the
   CAPA system, not in the audit report's appendix.

## Finding classification

Classification schemes are site-defined (not compendial), but most follow a
three-tier pattern:

| Classification | Typical meaning | Typical response expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Direct risk to patient, product quality, or data integrity; potential regulatory action. | Immediate containment, escalation to senior management, expedited CAPA. |
| Major | Significant GMP deviation; could compromise the quality system if uncorrected. | CAPA with root-cause analysis, defined due date, effectiveness check. |
| Minor | Isolated lapse unlikely to affect product quality directly. | Correction plus trend monitoring; CAPA if recurring. |

The value of classification is proportionate response - and pattern detection.
Ten minors clustered in one process usually describe one major systemic issue.

## From finding to CAPA

A finding only improves the system if it drives action:

1. **Contain** immediate product or patient risk.
2. **Investigate root cause** - the systemic weakness, not the symptom.
   "Operator error" is almost never a root cause; ask why the error was
   possible.
3. **Assign CAPA** with a named owner and a realistic due date.
4. **Verify effectiveness** after implementation - did the weakness actually
   close, or did the paperwork close? Define the effectiveness check when the
   CAPA is approved, not at the due date.
5. **Trend findings** across audits: recurring findings in the same element
   signal a CAPA system that treats symptoms.

## Management review linkage

Self-inspection results are a required input to management review under
ICH Q10. Leadership should see the audit schedule status, findings by severity
and element, overdue CAPAs, and repeat-finding trends. An audit program that
never escalates anything to management is itself a finding waiting to happen.

## Common inspection findings about audit programs

- Self-inspections skipped or repeatedly rescheduled, with no escalation.
- Auditors auditing their own departments.
- Checklists that do not match current regulations or current SOPs.
- Findings classified as minor to avoid CAPA workload - visible when minors
  recur for years.
- CAPA closed on document change alone, with no effectiveness verification.
- No trend analysis; each audit report read in isolation.
- Audit reports written so vaguely that neither the finding nor the fix is
  reconstructable.
