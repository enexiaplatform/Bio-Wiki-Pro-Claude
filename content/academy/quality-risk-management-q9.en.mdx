# Quality Risk Management (ICH Q9)

Quality Risk Management (QRM) is the systematic process for assessing,
controlling, communicating, and reviewing risks to the quality of a drug
product across its lifecycle. The governing framework is **ICH Q9(R1)**, which
reached Step 4 in January 2023 and was subsequently adopted by FDA, EMA, and
other ICH members. The revision clarified four areas: subjectivity in QRM,
formality of QRM activities, risk-based decision-making, and the role of QRM in
supply availability.

ICH Q9 defines risk as the combination of the **probability of occurrence of
harm** and the **severity of that harm**. Detectability can be a third factor
in tools like FMEA, but Q9's definition rests on severity and probability.

## The two Q9 principles

Everything in Q9 hangs on two principles:

1. The evaluation of risk to quality should be based on **scientific knowledge
   and experience with the process**, and ultimately link to protection of the
   patient.
2. The **level of effort, formality, and documentation** of the QRM process
   should be commensurate with the level of risk.

Principle 2 is the one sites get wrong most often. A 40-page FMEA for a
low-risk labeling change and a two-line email assessing a new API supplier are
both failures of proportionality - one over-controls, one under-controls.

## The QRM process

Q9 describes a repeating cycle with four activities:

| Step | Core question | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk assessment | What might go wrong, how bad, how likely? | Hazard identification, risk analysis, risk evaluation against acceptance criteria. |
| Risk control | What do we reduce, what do we accept? | Mitigation actions with owners, or documented risk acceptance. |
| Risk communication | Who needs to know? | Shared information among assessors, QA, operations, management, and suppliers. |
| Risk review | Is the assessment still true? | Re-evaluation triggered by new data, deviations, changes, or periodic review. |

Risk review is what ties QRM into the rest of the quality system: deviation
data, complaint trends, change control, and annual product review all feed new
knowledge back into existing risk assessments. A risk assessment that is never
revisited decays into decoration.

## Where QRM plugs into the quality system

Q9 Annex II maps QRM onto everyday quality-system decisions:

| Application | How QRM is used |
| --- | --- |
| Deviations and CAPA | Sets investigation depth, impact-assessment scope, and whether CAPA is warranted. |
| Change control | Determines assessment effort, testing, and requalification needs for a change. |
| Validation scope | Focuses qualification and validation effort on the highest-risk parameters and worst cases. |
| Audits and supplier oversight | Drives audit frequency and depth by material criticality and performance. |
| Monitoring and sampling | Justifies sample locations, frequencies, and alert-level placement. |
| Complaints and recalls | Structures health-hazard evaluation and field-action decisions. |

The common thread: QRM is how you decide *how much* control each situation
deserves, and the rationale is what makes that decision defensible.

## Choosing a tool: FMEA vs HACCP vs FTA

Q9 Annex I lists recognized tools. None is mandatory; the tool should fit the
question.

| Tool | How it works | Best suited for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FMEA / FMECA | Bottom-up: each failure mode scored for severity, occurrence, detection; RPN = S x O x D. | Equipment, process steps, utilities, cleaning - many discrete failure modes. | RPN arithmetic can rank a high-severity/low-RPN risk below a trivial high-RPN one; use severity gating. |
| HACCP | Identify hazards, then define critical control points (CCPs) with limits, monitoring, and corrective actions. | Process flows where hazards can be controlled at specific points (e.g., microbial control in a process). | Over-declaring CCPs dilutes monitoring effort on the true critical points. |
| Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) | Top-down: start from an undesired event and map the combinations of causes. | Investigating how a serious failure could occur (sterility breach, mix-up). | Requires good underlying event data; garbage probabilities in, garbage out. |
| Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA) | Early, high-level hazard screening before design is fixed. | Early development, facility or process design. | Too coarse for final control decisions. |
| Risk ranking and filtering | Score risks against weighted criteria, rank, and filter for action. | Comparing many sites, products, or suppliers for audit scheduling or prioritization. | Weights are subjective; document the rationale. |
| HAZOP | Structured "guide word" deviations from design intent. | Process engineering, new equipment, tech transfer. | Team-intensive; needs an experienced facilitator. |

An FMEA scoring example (scales are site-defined, not compendial): severity 1-5,
occurrence 1-5, detection 1-5 gives an RPN from 1 to 125. Many sites add a
rule that any severity of 5 escalates regardless of RPN, because multiplying a
catastrophic severity by low occurrence and easy detection must not quietly
de-prioritize a patient-safety hazard.

## Formality and subjectivity - the Q9(R1) additions

Q9(R1) acknowledges that formality is a continuum: informal tools (an
experienced team's documented judgement) may suit low-risk decisions, while
formal tools with full documentation suit high-risk ones. What must not vary is
that the decision and its rationale are recorded.

Subjectivity is the revision's other major theme. Every risk assessment
contains judgement; the controls that keep it honest are:

- **Defined scoring scales** with written anchors for each score level, not a
  blank 1-5 column.
- **Cross-functional teams** so no single person's gut feel dominates.
- **Documented rationale** for each rating, especially severity.
- **Explicit risk-acceptance criteria** decided before the data are seen.
- **Facilitator challenge**: someone asks "why is occurrence a 2 and not a 3?"

## Common mistakes and inspection findings

- One-size-fits-all FMEAs where every assessment uses the same template and
  effort regardless of risk.
- RPN thresholds set after scoring, conveniently placing current controls on
  the acceptable side.
- Risk acceptance with no documented rationale or no QA sign-off.
- Assessments filed and never reviewed when deviations, complaints, or changes
  produce new knowledge.
- Copy-paste severity ratings across products with different patient risk.
- QRM used to justify *not* doing something GMP requires - risk assessment
  informs how to comply, it cannot exempt compliance.
- No linkage between the risk register and change control, CAPA, or audit
  scheduling.
