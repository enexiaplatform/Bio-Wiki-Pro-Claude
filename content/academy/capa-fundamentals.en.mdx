# CAPA Fundamentals

Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) is how a quality system learns from
failure - and the subsystem regulators probe hardest, because its health
predicts everything else. A site with a weak CAPA process will repeat its
deviations; a site with a strong one converts each failure into permanent
improvement.

The framework: **ICH Q10** (Pharmaceutical Quality System, 2008) names CAPA
as one of the four core elements of the pharmaceutical quality system,
expected to operate across the product lifecycle. **21 CFR 211.192** and
**211.180(e)** create the investigation and annual-review obligations that
feed it. For medical devices, **21 CFR 820.100** defines CAPA explicitly and
remains the most detailed regulatory description of the concept - FDA's
QSIT inspection approach was built around it. **EU GMP Chapter 1** requires
CAPA as part of the pharmaceutical quality system, and Annex 1 (2022)
extends it to contamination-control events.

The high-stakes version of CAPA is this: every other subsystem (deviations,
OOS, complaints, EM, audits) is a *sensor*. CAPA is the only subsystem that
*changes the machine*. If it is broken, the sensors just keep counting the
same failures.

## Correction vs corrective action vs preventive action

These three are routinely confused, and the confusion shows up in
inspection findings:

| Term | Definition | Example | What it does NOT do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Correction | Immediate action on the detected problem | Quarantine and reject the affected batch; reclean the tank | Prevent recurrence |
| Corrective action (CA) | Eliminate the cause of a detected nonconformity | Add an expiry check to the buffer prep log after an expired buffer caused an OOS | Address problems that have not happened yet |
| Preventive action (PA) | Eliminate the cause of a potential nonconformity | Roll the log fix to all labs before they experience the same failure | Fix the current batch |

A complete CAPA record usually contains all three. "We rejected the batch"
closes a deviation; it does not close a CAPA. The test question is always:
*what did we change so this cannot happen again, here and everywhere else
it could happen?*

## When do you even need a CAPA?

Not every deviation needs a full CAPA - over-raising CAPAs buries the
system in trivial records until nothing gets done, while under-raising
hides systemic problems. A defensible triage:

| Signal | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Minor, well-understood, one-off | Correction + documented rationale; monitor in trending |
| Recurring event (same or similar cause) | CAPA - recurrence is the strongest single trigger |
| Systemic or high-risk (sterility, data integrity, cross-contamination) | Full CAPA with formal root-cause analysis |
| Critical / potential patient impact | CAPA + risk assessment + possible field action |
| Trend breach (OOT pattern, rising complaint rate) | CAPA even without a single "big" event |

Tie the threshold to risk and trend, document the triage rule in the SOP,
and review borderline calls at the quality review board. The triage rule
itself is inspectable - auditors ask "show me a deviation that did NOT
become a CAPA and explain why."

## Root cause first: comparing the methods

Jumping to actions before establishing root cause is the most common CAPA
finding worldwide. Each method has a legitimate role:

| Method | Best for | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 Whys | Single-cause, well-bounded events | Fast, forces past "human error" | Linear; misses branching causes; quality depends on the asker |
| Ishikawa (fishbone) | Multi-factor problems (method, machine, material, man, measurement, environment) | Structured breadth; good team facilitation | Generates candidates, not proof - each branch still needs evidence |
| Is / Is-not analysis | Object-, location-, time-specific problems | Sharply localises where the cause acts | Weak for diffuse, chronic problems |
| Fault-tree analysis | Complex systems with interacting causes | Rigorous logic; quantifiable | Slow; needs system knowledge |
| Kepner-Tregoe | High-stakes, ambiguous problems | Disciplined separation of facts from speculation | Training-intensive |
| Change analysis | Events correlating with a change (new lot, maintenance, method version) | Directly testable | Only works if change records are complete |

Whichever tool you use, the root cause must be **verified with evidence**:
reproduce it, correlate it, or show the mechanism in the data. An unverified
root cause is a hypothesis wearing a conclusion's clothes.

### The "human error" trap

"Operator error" and "analyst retrained" are where investigations go to
die. FDA warning letters repeatedly reject human error as a root cause
without a deeper answer: *what in the system allowed a competent person to
make that error and allowed it to go undetected?* Ask instead:

- Was the procedure ambiguous, or did it conflict with another document?
- Was the interface (label, log field, software screen) error-prone?
- Was the verification step missing, or performed by the same person?
- Was workload, staffing, or time pressure a contributing system factor?

If "retraining" appears in your CAPA, it may be one component - but the
system change (checklist, forcing function, second check, redesigned form)
is what makes the CAPA real.

## Designing the effectiveness check - the part everyone skips

The effectiveness check is the difference between a CAPA and a hope. It
must be defined **before** the CAPA is closed, and it must be:

1. **Quantitative** - a metric with a target, not an activity. "Zero
   prep-related OOS across the next 20 batches" is a check; "training
   completed" is not.
2. **Time-bound and scope-bound** - over what period, which batches,
   products, or lines?
3. **Fed by an objective data source** - deviation log, OOS trend, EM
   trend, audit results - with the source named in the CAPA.
4. **Owned** - a named person verifies it, and QA reviews the outcome.
5. **Honest about failure** - the record must say what happens if the
   check fails (reopen, escalate, broaden scope).

| Weak check | Why it fails | Strong version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Training completed" | Activity, not outcome | Zero recurrence of the error type across 3 months / N batches |
| "SOP updated" | Document change, not behaviour | Next 2 audits show correct execution; zero related deviations |
| "Manager reviewed" | No measurable criterion | Defined metric met at the defined review date |
| "No complaints received" | Absence of evidence; complaints lag | Active verification: sampled records, targeted testing, or audit of the process |

Effectiveness checks need a calendar: a CAPA closed today with a 3-month
check must surface for verification in 3 months - via the CAPA tracking
system, not someone's memory.

## CAPA record - the fields that survive an audit

1. **Problem statement** - what, where, when, how detected, magnitude.
2. **Immediate correction and containment** - what was protected right now.
3. **Risk assessment** - severity, scope, other batches/products/sites
   affected (a structured QRM tool per ICH Q9 helps here).
4. **Root-cause method and the verified root cause** - method named,
   evidence attached.
5. **Corrective actions** - each mapped to a cause, each with owner and
   due date.
6. **Preventive actions** - the fix extended to similar processes,
   products, lines, or sites.
7. **Effectiveness check** - metric, target, time window, data source,
   owner.
8. **Closure approval and effectiveness-verification record** - two
   separate events, often months apart.

## CAPA metrics: how you know the system works

CAPA is itself a process, so trend it. Useful metrics (common industry
practice - set targets against your own baseline):

| Metric | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Aging distribution / % overdue | Capacity problem or approval bottleneck |
| Median days to closure | System responsiveness; watch for both rushing and stalling |
| Recurrence rate (same cause reopens) | Root-cause quality - the single most damning metric |
| Effectiveness-check pass rate | Whether actions actually change outcomes |
| % CAPAs citing "human error/retraining" as sole cause | Investigation depth |
| Source mix (deviation / complaint / audit / trend) | Whether all sensors feed the system |

Rising overdue counts plus a high recurrence rate is the classic pattern
FDA cites when concluding a CAPA system is "not adequate" - the paperwork
exists, but nothing improves.

## What inspectors look for - and how to defend

1. **Actions before root cause.** The CAPA was written backwards from a
   convenient fix. Defence: the record shows method, candidates, evidence,
   and verification of the cause.
2. **"Human error + retraining" closures.** Defence: every human-error
   cause is paired with a system change, or the record explains why the
   system is already sound.
3. **No effectiveness check, or a check that is an activity.** Defence:
   pre-defined, quantitative, time-bound criteria in every CAPA.
4. **CAPAs closed without the check ever being performed.** The calendar
   event never happened. Defence: the tracking system flags pending checks;
   QA signs the verification separately from closure.
5. **Narrow scope.** The fix applied to one line while three identical
   lines kept the defect. Defence: the risk assessment explicitly evaluates
   where else the cause could act.
6. **Overdue CAPAs with no escalation.** Defence: aging review at
   management review with documented extensions and justifications.
7. **Everything is a CAPA / nothing is a CAPA.** Defence: a written triage
   rule, applied consistently, with borderline calls reviewed.
8. **CAPA disconnected from APR/PQR and management review.** Defence:
   recurrence and effectiveness metrics feed both, per ICH Q10.

## Worked example

*Event:* OOS for assay on Batch 23.

- **Correction:** quarantine the batch; invalidate the result only after a
  confirmed, documented laboratory error.
- **Root cause (5 Whys):** the result failed because the mobile phase
  degraded; it degraded because it was prepared with an expired buffer; the
  expired buffer was used because the preparation log had no expiry field
  and no check step. Note: "analyst failed to notice" is the fourth Why,
  not the root - the missing log control is.
- **Corrective action:** repeat affected analyses with verified reagents;
  add an expiry field and a second-person expiry check to the prep log;
  verify the fix on the next five preparations.
- **Preventive action:** roll the log change across all HPLC methods in
  the lab and assess the two other labs using the same log template.
- **Effectiveness check:** zero prep-related OOS across the next 20
  batches over 3 months, verified from the OOS trend by QC management,
  with QA review before final closure.

The whole record takes two pages - but every element is evidenced, and the
effectiveness check is a number, not a feeling.
