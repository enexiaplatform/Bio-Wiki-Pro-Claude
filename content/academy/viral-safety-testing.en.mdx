# Viral Safety of Biologics

Biologics made in mammalian, avian, insect, or other human/animal-derived cell
systems carry a theoretical viral risk from the cell substrate, biological raw
materials, or the production environment. History made this concrete:
contamination events such as the Minute Virus of Mice (MVM) incidents in CHO
processes showed that a single contaminated raw material can reach commercial
manufacturing. Because no single test can prove a product is free of every
possible virus, ICH Q5A(R2) (Viral Safety Evaluation of Biotechnology
Products, revised guideline adopted at Step 4 in 2023) builds safety from
three complementary pillars.

## The three pillars

1. **Control the source** - select and thoroughly characterize the cell line,
   master and working cell banks, and biological raw materials so that known
   and detectable agents are excluded before manufacturing starts.
2. **Test at appropriate stages** - test cell banks, cells at the limit of in
   vitro cell age, and unprocessed bulk harvest, where a result can still
   stop contamination from propagating.
3. **Validate viral clearance** - demonstrate that the purification process
   removes or inactivates virus with a defensible log-reduction margin.

The pillars multiply rather than add: source control reduces what enters,
testing detects what may be present, and clearance provides margin for what
neither of the first two can see.

## Pillar 1 - source control

Source control is paperwork plus laboratory work: a documented cell-line
history, bank genealogy, raw-material qualification, and the cell-bank
characterization panel (identity, sterility, mycoplasma, adventitious and
endogenous viruses). Serum and animal-derived raw materials receive particular
scrutiny, including TSE/BSE risk assessment, because they were the route in
several historical events. Change control on raw materials and suppliers is
part of the pillar: a new serum lot or media supplier is a viral-safety
assessment, not just a purchasing decision.

## Pillar 2 - testing at the right stages

Testing is applied where results are still actionable:

| Stage | What is tested | Typical methods |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MCB / WCB | Adventitious viruses, retroviruses, endogenous agents | In vitro adventitious-agent assays on detector cell lines, in vivo assays where required, TEM, reverse transcriptase assay, targeted PCR |
| Cells at the limit of in vitro cell age (EOPC/CAL) | Agents that could emerge during production | Adventitious-agent panel, retrovirus evaluation |
| Unprocessed bulk harvest (each batch or at justified frequency) | Contamination introduced during the run | In vitro adventitious-agent assay; targeted PCR or NGS where justified |

The classic in vitro adventitious-agent assay inoculates the test article onto
several detector cell lines chosen for broad viral susceptibility and observes
them for cytopathic effects, hemadsorption, and hemagglutination over weeks.
It is sensitive and broad but slow. Molecular methods - targeted PCR for
specific agents and, increasingly, next-generation sequencing (NGS) for broad,
unbiased detection - are recognized in Q5A(R2) as supplements or, with
validation, replacements for parts of the classical panel.

### PCR versus infectivity - know what each measures

- **Infectivity assays** (cell culture, TCID50, plaque) measure functional,
  replicating virus. They answer the question regulators care about - "can
  this virus propagate?" - but are slow (weeks) and only detect agents the
  detector cells support.
- **PCR/qPCR** measures viral nucleic acid: fast, specific, and highly
  sensitive, but it cannot distinguish infectious virus from inactivated
  particles or free nucleic acid. A PCR-positive, infectivity-negative result
  can mean dead virus - or a sub-infectious dose.
- **NGS** trades sensitivity-per-target for breadth: it can find unexpected
  agents with no prior hypothesis, at the cost of complex bioinformatics and
  validation.

Clearance studies exploit the difference deliberately: infectivity assays are
the default readout, while PCR may be used for specific steps (for example
virus filtration, where large-volume sampling and partitioning effects matter)
with documented justification.

## Pillar 3 - viral clearance validation

A viral clearance study spikes a known amount of model virus into process
intermediates, runs the purification step in a **qualified scale-down model**,
and measures virus before and after. The result is a **log reduction value**:

LRV = log10(total virus input / total virus output)

Per Q5A(R2) convention, a step generally counts toward the overall clearance
claim when it achieves at least about 1 log of reduction, and reduction from
different steps is summed only when the steps act by **different mechanisms**.
Two chromatography steps with the same binding chemistry do not add; a low-pH
hold plus virus filtration do.

### Model viruses

| Virus | Role in the panel |
| --- | --- |
| Murine leukemia virus (MuLV/X-MuLV) | Model for endogenous retroviruses inherent to rodent cell lines |
| Minute virus of mice (MVM) | Small, non-enveloped, extremely resistant parvovirus; worst case for inactivation and small-pore filtration |
| Pseudorabies virus (PRV) | Large enveloped DNA virus; general clearance capacity |
| Reovirus type 3 (Reo-3) | Non-enveloped dsRNA virus of intermediate size and resistance |

The panel spans size, envelope, and genome type so that no realistic
contaminant class escapes every step.

### Clearance mechanisms

- **Inactivation**: low-pH hold (industry practice for monoclonal antibodies,
  typically around pH 3.5-3.8 for a defined hold time - product-specific, not
  compendial), solvent/detergent treatment for plasma-derived products, and
  heat where the product tolerates it. Highly effective against enveloped
  viruses; weaker against small non-enveloped viruses like MVM.
- **Removal**: virus-retentive filtration (nominal pore size around 20 nm for
  parvovirus-grade filters), chromatography, and precipitation/partitioning.
  Virus filtration is the workhorse against small non-enveloped viruses that
  inactivation misses.

A robust process shows orthogonal coverage: at least one effective step for
enveloped viruses and at least one for small non-enveloped viruses, with the
overall LRV exceeding the estimated worst-case virus load by a justified
margin. Q5A(R2) also sets expectations for the scale-down model: it must be
shown to represent the manufacturing step (comparable parameters, yields, and
product quality), or the LRV claim is unsupported.

## What good looks like

- cell banks and raw materials characterized for viral risk;
- testing panel justified for the substrate, with clear acceptance and
  escalation rules for unprocessed bulk;
- scale-down models qualified before spiking studies;
- model-virus selection and LRV claims documented with controls, assay
  suitability, and cytotoxicity/interference data;
- additive clearance only across mechanistically different steps;
- process, raw-material, and supplier changes triggering viral-safety impact
  assessment, sometimes including re-validation of a step.

## Common findings and mistakes

- Summing LRVs from mechanistically identical steps.
- Scale-down model never qualified; LRVs from a lab setup that does not
  represent manufacturing.
- Low-pH inactivation claimed without demonstrating time, temperature, and pH
  control at production scale.
- Virus-filter performance accepted from vendor data alone, with no
  product-specific validation or post-use integrity strategy.
- Adventitious-agent testing with detector cell lines of undocumented
  susceptibility.
- PCR results over-interpreted as proof of infectious virus (or its absence).
- Raw-material changes implemented without viral-safety assessment.
