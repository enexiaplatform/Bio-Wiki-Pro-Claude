# Sterilizing-Grade Filtration & Integrity Testing

When a product cannot survive terminal sterilization — most biologics, many
injectables, heat-labile small molecules — sterilizing-grade filtration is the
final barrier between the bioburden of the bulk solution and the aseptic fill.
That makes the filter and its integrity testing one of the highest-stakes
single components in sterile manufacturing: a failure is silent, invisible
during the run, and detectable only after the batch is made.

The governing framework: **EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)**, section 8 (sterilization,
including filtration); **FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products
Produced by Aseptic Processing (2004)**; **PDA Technical Report No. 26**
(sterilizing filtration of liquids); and **ASTM F838** (bacterial retention
test method). Pharmacopeial sterility assurance for the dosage form still rests
on the validated process as a whole.

## What "0.22 µm" Actually Means

The pore-size label is a convention, not a definition. A sterilizing-grade
filter is defined functionally: a membrane that, when challenged with a
suspension of **_Brevundimonas diminuta_ ATCC 19146** at a concentration of at
least **10^7 CFU per cm² of effective filtration area**, under worst-case
process conditions, produces a sterile effluent (ASTM F838; PDA TR 26).

Why *B. diminuta*? It is a small (approximately 0.3 × 0.6–1.0 µm), slender
Gram-negative rod that grows to high titers in simple media, passes through
0.45 µm membranes, and represents the practical worst-case bacterial challenge
for a 0.2 µm-class membrane. The 10^7 CFU/cm² challenge density is deliberately
brutal — orders of magnitude above any plausible process bioburden — so that a
validated retention claim has an enormous safety margin.

Two consequences that matter in practice:

1. **The pore size printed on the box is not the claim.** The retention study
   is the claim. A "0.2 µm" membrane without a validated *B. diminuta*
   retention study at your process conditions is not yet a sterilizing filter
   for your product.
2. **The claim is product- and process-specific.** Viscosity, pressure,
   temperature, flow rate, chemistry (surfactants, solvents, pH), and contact
   time can all affect retention. The filter manufacturer's generic validation
   is the starting point; worst-case conditions for *your* product must be
   covered — by the generic study's bracketing or by product-specific testing.

## Filter Validation: What Must Be Established

| Validation element | What it demonstrates | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacterial retention | Sterile effluent at ≥10^7 CFU/cm² *B. diminuta* under worst-case conditions | ASTM F838 study; product-specific or bracketed justification |
| Compatibility | Membrane tolerates the product and process conditions without degradation | Compatibility/chemical resistance data, soak studies |
| Extractables/leachables | Filter does not contaminate the product | Vendor extractables data; product-specific leachables assessment where risk warrants |
| Adsorption | Filter does not strip active or preservative from the formulation | Adsorption study at process conditions |
| Integrity test correlation | The physical integrity test limit actually corresponds to bacterial retention | Manufacturer correlation of bubble point/diffusion limits to retention data |
| Bioburden control upstream | The challenge reaching the filter stays within validated assumptions | Pre-filtration bioburden limits and monitoring |

Pre-filtration bioburden deserves emphasis: Annex 1 expects bioburden to be
tested before the sterilizing filter with defined limits — a common industry
practice limit is ≤10 CFU/100 mL, applied where justified by the risk
assessment. Filtration capacity is validated against a defined bioburden; an
uncontrolled bulk is an unvalidated challenge.

Process parameters that must stay inside the validated envelope on every batch:
filtration time (a filter is typically used for a single batch, within a
validated maximum duration), differential pressure, flow rate, temperature, and
hold times before and after filtration. Each is a retention variable, not an
engineering footnote.

## Integrity Tests: The Three Physical Methods

| Method | Physical principle | Best suited to | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bubble point | Pressure at which gas displaces liquid from the largest wetted pore; correlates to maximum pore size and retention | Small-area filters, discs, small cartridges | Subjective endpoint reading on large-area filters; diffusion can mask the transition |
| Diffusive flow / forward flow | Gas diffusion through the wetted membrane below the bubble point, measured quantitatively | Large-area cartridges and multi-round housings | Temperature and upstream volume sensitivity; requires equilibration |
| Pressure hold / decay | Pressure decay of the wetted, pressurized system over time — a decay-based variant of diffusive flow | Where diffusion meters are impractical; simple setups | Sensitive to temperature drift and fitting leaks; needs a validated reference volume |
| Water intrusion (WIT) | Water intrusion into a hydrophobic membrane under pressure | Hydrophobic vent/gas filters (PTFE) that cannot be wetted with water | Solvent-wet variant of the diffusion test; temperature control critical |

The integrity test value that matters is the **limit**: the maximum bubble
point depression or diffusion flow that the manufacturer has correlated, with
validation data, to bacterial retention. A test is not "a bubble point" in the
abstract — it is a comparison against a validated limit, at a specified
temperature, with a specified wetting fluid and test gas.

## Pre- and Post-Use Testing — and PUPSIT

Annex 1 (2022), section 8.87: the integrity of the sterilized filter must be
**verified before use** — pre-use, post-sterilization integrity testing
(PUPSIT) — and **confirmed after use**. The post-use test is the mandatory
backstop: it proves the filter was intact while your batch passed through it.
A post-use failure with no compensating controls places the entire batch under
suspicion.

PUPSIT exists to catch a distinct failure mode: a filter damaged during
sterilization or installation whose defect is then **masked during use** —
product components, particulates, or fouling can plug a flaw so that the
post-use test passes a filter that was not intact for part of the run. The
PUPSIT decision framework:

| Argument | For PUPSIT | Against PUPSIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Failure detection | Catches sterilization/installation damage before product exposure | Post-use test already catches most defects; masking is rare for well-characterized products |
| Aseptic risk | Test is downstream of the sterilizing filter in a properly designed rig | The test rig adds aseptic connections and manipulations — its own contamination risk at the worst possible location |
| Process impact | Confirms the assembly before committing the batch | Wetting/flushing consumes hold time and can complicate small batches |
| Regulatory posture | Default expectation of Annex 1 (2022) | Permitted only with a documented, rigorous, product-specific risk rationale and compensating controls |

Where PUPSIT is genuinely not possible — the classic case is very small batch
volumes — Annex 1 permits the alternative only with in-depth process knowledge
and compensating controls documented in the CCS. A "PUPSIT not applicable"
statement without that analysis is a predictable inspection finding. See
[EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) — Key Changes](/library/annex-1-key-changes) and
[Contamination Control Strategy (CCS)](/library/contamination-control-strategy).

## Redundant Filtration

A second sterilizing-grade filter in series, close to the point of fill, is the
standard risk mitigation — particularly where post-use integrity can only be
confirmed after a long run. Key design points:

- **Both filters are sterilizing-grade**, and both are integrity tested
  post-use where the configuration allows.
- If the final filter passes post-use, it supports the impact assessment of a
  pre-filter failure; if the final filter fails, redundancy upstream does not
  rescue the batch — the last barrier failed.
- Redundancy is a safety net, not a license to skip PUPSIT reasoning or
  bioburden control. The CCS should state the rationale for the configuration
  (single vs redundant) per product.
- Hold times and pressure differentials across the pair must be validated —
  prolonged filtration time and high differential pressure are both retention
  risk factors covered in the validation envelope.

## Executing the Test: Roles, Records, and Pitfalls

A defensible integrity test record captures, per test: filter type and lot
number, serial/position, wetting fluid and flush volume, test method and
instrument ID, test gas, temperature, validated limit, raw result, operator,
and pass/fail conclusion. The analyst performs the test; a second person (or
the supervisor) verifies limits and setup; QA reviews the record as part of
batch review.

**False failures — the usual suspects:**

1. **Incomplete wetting.** A dry pore reads as a leak. Flush the validated
   volume; respect soak time; verify flush effluent clarity.
2. **Wrong wetting fluid.** Limits are fluid-specific — water-wet limits do not
   apply to product-wet or solvent-wet tests.
3. **Temperature drift.** Diffusion is temperature-sensitive; let the system
   equilibrate and record the temperature.
4. **Wrong test gas or gas quality.** Use the validated gas (typically
   nitrogen, air, or as specified).
5. **Upstream volume and connections.** A leaking fitting or incorrectly
   entered reference volume mimics membrane failure. Leak-check the rig first.
6. **Hydrophobic filter tested hydrophilic.** Vent and gas filters need WIT or
   solvent-wet methods, not water bubble point.

A suspected failure is confirmed by re-wetting and retesting **before**
declaring the filter — and potentially the batch — compromised. But the
retest must be a defined diagnostic step in the SOP, not an informal "test
until it passes": the first failed result, the investigation, and the retest
rationale all belong in the record.

Filter changes are change control, not procurement events. A new membrane type,
a different pore-size designation, a new housing geometry, or even a supplier's
manufacturing-site change can alter the validated retention, extractables, and
integrity-test correlation package. The change assessment asks: does the
existing validation still cover this filter at these process conditions, and
does the integrity test limit still trace to correlated retention data?

## Batch-Impact Logic After a Post-Use Failure

If the post-use test fails and the failure is confirmed (not a method
artifact), the batch cannot be released without an impact assessment that is
rarely survivable: bioburden data for the bulk, pre-filter integrity status,
redundant filter results, fill duration, and the nature of the defect all feed
the assessment, but there is no retrospective test that proves the batch
sterile. This is why the PUPSIT decision, redundant configuration, and
pre-filtration bioburden limits exist — they are the difference between a
reportable event and a controlled one.

## Common Failure Modes (What Inspectors Find)

1. **Retention validation not linked to the product.** Generic vendor study
   filed without a bracketing rationale for product-specific conditions.
2. **No documented PUPSIT rationale.** The single most frequent Annex 1
   filtration finding since 2023.
3. **Post-use test performed but limits never verified** against the filter
   manufacturer's current specification after a filter change.
4. **"Test until pass" practices.** Failed results overwritten by retests
   without investigation records.
5. **Pre-filtration bioburden uncontrolled.** No limit, or results trending up
   without action — the validated challenge assumption quietly broken.
6. **Filter use beyond validated time/pressure.** Filtration duration in the
   batch record exceeding the validation envelope.
7. **Integrity test records missing wetting fluid, temperature, or instrument
   ID** — the metadata that distinguishes a true failure from a method
   artifact.
8. **Vent and gas filters forgotten.** Tank vents and process gas filters with
   no integrity testing schedule at all.

## Worked Example: Borderline Diffusion Result

*Fictional but realistic.* A 10-inch final filter shows a post-use forward-flow
result of 19.2 mL/min against a validated limit of 18.0 mL/min at 25 °C — a
failure. The record shows the test ran at 27.5 °C after a rushed
equilibration. Per SOP, the analyst documents the failure, QA is notified, and
the defined diagnostic sequence runs: verify instrument calibration status,
verify upstream volume entry, leak-check connections, re-wet with the validated
flush volume, equilibrate to 25 ± 2 °C, retest. Retest result: 16.8 mL/min —
pass. The investigation concludes temperature-induced artifact; the batch
impact assessment documents both results, the rationale, and the disposition.
The CAPA addresses the equilibration step — not the filter.
