# Supplier Audit Programs

You cannot test quality into a purchased material; you have to buy it from a
controlled source. GMP therefore expects manufacturers to qualify and monitor
suppliers of materials and outsourced services: EU GMP Part I Chapter 5
requires that starting materials be purchased only from approved suppliers,
Chapter 7 governs outsourced activities, FDA 21 CFR 211.84 requires that
components come from suppliers whose quality can be relied upon, and ICH Q10
places supply-chain controls inside the pharmaceutical quality system. The
audit program is the evidence engine behind the approved-supplier list.

Auditing does not end at approval. Audits verify that the supplier's quality
system, process controls, data integrity, material controls, and
change-management practices still support the approved status - and they give
you early warning when they no longer do.

## Audit types by assurance level

| Audit type | What it can verify | Appropriate use | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Questionnaire / desk assessment | QMS existence and structure, certifications, regulatory status, key documents. | Low-risk suppliers; first screen before deeper review; periodic confirmation. | Self-reported; cannot verify actual practice or data integrity. |
| Remote audit | Document review, system walkthroughs, interviews, CAPA follow-up. | Medium-risk suppliers, travel limits, targeted follow-ups between on-site visits. | Facility condition, shop-floor practice, and record culture are hard to see. |
| On-site audit | Everything above plus facility condition, actual operations, raw data, personnel practices. | High-risk suppliers: APIs, sterile components, contract sterilizers, critical labs, sole sources. | Cost and effort; a two-day visit is still a sample. |
| Shared / third-party audit | Same as on-site, via an accredited audit body or industry scheme. | Routine oversight where your own visit adds little. | You own the decision; review scope and findings critically. |

The audit type should match risk. A fixed approach for every supplier wastes
effort on low-risk suppliers and under-controls high-risk ones.

## Risk-based scheduling

Audit interval should reflect:

- material or service criticality (does it touch sterility, potency, identity?);
- supplier performance history - complaints, deviations, incoming-test trends;
- previous audit findings and CAPA closure quality;
- regulatory inspection history and certifications;
- change-notification reliability (do they tell you before things change?);
- sole-source or supply-continuity risk.

Frequency tiers are site-defined, but common industry practice looks like:

| Supplier risk tier | Typical oversight pattern |
| --- | --- |
| High (API, sterile/primary packaging, contract sterilizer, critical CMO/lab, sole source) | On-site audit every 1-3 years, plus continuous performance monitoring; audit triggered early by quality signals or major changes. |
| Medium (excipients with functional impact, secondary packaging) | On-site or remote audit every 2-4 years plus periodic questionnaires. |
| Low (non-critical consumables, general services) | Questionnaire plus incoming verification; audit on signal. |

These intervals are industry practice, not compendial numbers. What regulators
expect is a documented, risk-justified schedule - and that you actually follow
it.

## Preparing for the audit

The quality of an on-site audit is set before you travel:

- Pull the supplier's **performance data**: incoming-test results, complaint
  and deviation history, change notifications, and prior CAPA status.
- Re-read the **quality agreement** and technical specifications - audit
  against what was contracted, not generic GMP.
- Review the previous audit report and verify claimed CAPA closures first;
  uncorrected repeat findings change the tone of the whole visit.
- Build an agenda that reserves time for the **shop floor and raw data**, not
  just the conference room; request specific batch records, deviation files,
  and audit trails in advance.
- Brief the audit team on roles: lead auditor, technical specialists (QC,
  engineering, microbiology) for high-risk processes.

A questionnaire still has a role for higher-risk suppliers: send it ahead of
the visit so the on-site time goes to verification, not fact-finding.

## What an on-site supplier audit covers

- **Quality system**: deviation, CAPA, change control, complaint handling,
  self-inspection, management review - the ICH Q10 elements at the supplier.
- **Process and material controls**: manufacturing process state of control,
  cleaning, cross-contamination prevention, pest control, warehousing.
- **Data integrity**: raw data practices, audit trails, access control, record
  retention - for both paper and electronic systems.
- **Laboratory controls**: method validation status, OOS handling, reference
  standard management, instrument qualification.
- **Regulatory posture**: registration status, inspection history, and whether
  the material you buy matches what was inspected.
- **Change management**: the contractual commitment to notify changes before
  implementation - the clause that protects you more than any single visit.

## Closing the loop

An audit report is not the end of the audit. Findings should drive supplier
CAPA, approved-supplier status decisions, increased incoming verification where
justified, and follow-up to confirm effectiveness.

Strong programs define:

- finding classification and due dates;
- evidence required for closure;
- escalation when CAPA is late or weak;
- triggers for probation, restricted approval, or disqualification;
- linkage to supplier performance review and management review.

The quality agreement is the enforcement instrument: it should name audit
rights, change-notification obligations, data-access expectations, and what
happens when the supplier fails to perform.

## Common inspection findings

- Approved-supplier status with no supporting audit or assessment evidence.
- Audit schedules that exist but slip year after year with no escalation.
- High-risk suppliers managed by questionnaire while low-risk ones get site
  visits.
- Audit findings closed on the supplier's promise, with no verification.
- No quality agreement, or one without change-notification terms.
- Supplier changes (site, process, specification) discovered after the fact
  during incoming testing.
- Audits that review certificates but never raw data or the shop floor.
