# OOS Investigation — Deep Dive

An out-of-specification (OOS) result is any test result that falls outside
the specification or acceptance criteria in the approved application, the
compendia, or the manufacturer's own limits. How you investigate it is one
of the most scrutinised parts of a GMP quality system - because it sits
directly on the line between sound science and *testing into compliance*.
The framework below follows the **FDA Guidance for Industry: Investigating
Out-of-Specification Test Results (2006)** and the legal precedent set in
**United States v. Barr Laboratories (1993)**, executed under
**21 CFR 211.192** (and 211.165(f) for rejected batches).

The single most important principle: an OOS result is presumed valid until
an investigation demonstrates otherwise. You do not get to make it
disappear by re-testing until you like the answer.

## The architecture at a glance

| Phase | Question | Owner | Ends when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phase I - laboratory assessment | Is there an assignable laboratory cause? | Analyst + supervisor, QA oversight | Cause confirmed (invalidate + repeat) or ruled out (result stands) |
| Phase II - full-scale investigation | If the result stands, what caused it and what is the batch's fate? | QA-led, cross-functional | Root cause + disposition + CAPA |
| Extension | Are other batches/products/sites affected? | QA | Scope closed, trend captured |

Timelines should be defined in the SOP - FDA expects investigations to be
timely (30 days is common industry practice for completing the
investigation record - confirm against your SOP), and the batch must stay
in quarantine throughout.

## Phase I — Laboratory investigation

Phase I is a factual assessment, led by the analyst with the supervisor,
done **before** any retesting. Its job is to determine whether an
assignable laboratory cause explains the result.

### The hypothesis-test protocol

Mature labs run Phase I as a structured, hypothesis-driven protocol, not a
fishing expedition:

1. **Open the record immediately.** The analyst reports the OOS to the
   supervisor the same day; the investigation number, date, analyst, and
   result are logged contemporaneously. Preserve everything: sample
   preparations, dilutions, standard preparations, the column and
   instrument state, glassware, and the electronic data as-acquired.
2. **State the candidate hypotheses before touching anything.** Typical
   predefined hypothesis classes: calculation/transcription error;
   dilution or weighing error; standard or reagent defect (preparation,
   expiry, storage); instrument malfunction; system-suitability failure;
   integration/processing error; sampling or sample-handling error;
   method-following error (wrong parameter, wrong column, wrong
   wavelength).
3. **Test each hypothesis with a defined check.** Examples:
   - Recalculate from raw data (not from the reported worksheet).
   - Verify standard preparation records, weights, and potency
     assignments; re-assay the *retained* standard preparation against a
     fresh one if standard integrity is in question.
   - Review system suitability: did SST pass *before* the sample
     injections, and did it stay in control through the run?
   - Read the audit trail: reprocessing, re-injections, aborted
     sequences, method edits, clock events.
   - Re-inject the *retained original preparation* where the method and
     stability of the preparation permit - this tests instrument and
     integration, not the preparation itself.
   - Interview the analyst promptly and document it - memory decays and
     the Barr ruling made contemporaneous reconstruction a baseline
     expectation.
4. **Judge on evidence.** You may only invalidate the original result when
   there is a *scientifically sound, documented* assignable cause - a
   failed system suitability, a demonstrably degraded standard confirmed
   by re-assay, an instrument malfunction with an audit-trail signature.
   "Analyst error" with no supporting evidence is not acceptable, and
   neither is a hypothesis that was never actually tested.

If - and only if - a clear laboratory cause is found and documented, the
result may be invalidated and the test repeated by the original or a
second analyst. The invalidation, not the convenient number, is what
closes Phase I.

### Retesting, resampling, and averaging

These are where investigations most often go wrong:

- **Retesting** (re-analysing the *original* sample) requires a
  predefined, scientifically justified plan - how many retests, by whom,
  and the decision rule - written **before** the retests are run.
  Open-ended "test until it passes" was exactly what the Barr ruling
  prohibited.
- **Resampling** (taking a *new* sample) is only justified when the
  original sample is shown to be non-representative or compromised - not
  as a second bite at the apple.
- **Averaging** an OOS result with passing results to obtain a passing
  mean is generally **not** acceptable. Averaging is only appropriate
  where the method defines it (for example, replicate determinations that
  are part of the reportable value), and even then a large spread is
  itself a signal to investigate, not to smooth over. For tests of
  uniformity (content uniformity, dissolution), the compendial decision
  rules already build in the legitimate averaging - do not invent your
  own.
- **Outlier tests** may support a microbiological or biological assay
  where the method allows them, but a chemical assay outlier cannot be
  used to discard an OOS result. An outlier test explains variability; it
  does not invalidate a result.

## Phase II — Full-scale investigation

When Phase I finds no assignable laboratory cause, the OOS result stands
and the investigation expands beyond the lab, under **21 CFR 211.192**,
which requires the review to extend to other batches that may be
associated with the failure.

### The manufacturing review in depth

Phase II is a structured review of everything that could have produced a
genuinely failing result:

| Review area | What to examine |
| --- | --- |
| Batch record | Executed vs master; every parameter in range; second-person verifications; any undocumented adjustments |
| Raw materials | Lots used, CoA verification, supplier changes, storage conditions, retest dates |
| Process parameters | Critical parameter trends for this batch vs history; hold times; equipment train and settings |
| Equipment | Maintenance and calibration status, cleaning records, prior product, any work orders in the window |
| In-process data | IPC results, yields, reconciliation - drifts that corroborate the OOS |
| Personnel | Training status, shift patterns, concurrent deviations on the line |
| Deviations and changes | Every deviation and change control touching the batch, the line, or the method |
| Other batches | Adjacent batches of the same product, other products on shared equipment, other lots of the same raw materials |
| Laboratory context | Did other samples tested on the same instrument/standard/day show anomalies? (A lab cause missed in Phase I) |
| Stability and history | Prior OOS/OOT history for the product, complaint history, previous investigations with similar signatures |

The review uses structured root-cause tools (5 Whys, Ishikawa, change
analysis) - and the cause it lands on must be one the *evidence* supports.
"The process is variable" is a description, not a root cause.

### Disposition and scope

- **Decide batch disposition.** The QA unit makes the release/reject
  decision on the full weight of evidence. A batch may not be released on
  the basis of a passing retest that merely overrides an unexplained OOS.
- **Widen the scope.** Where a cause is confirmed (manufacturing or
  laboratory), assess every batch, product, and site that could share it;
  where no cause is found, the OOS stands and the batch is rejected -
  with the uncertainty documented, not resolved by optimism.
- **Close with CAPA.** The corrective action maps to the verified cause,
  and its effectiveness is verified later - a corrective action that is
  never checked is not a corrective action. Recurring OOS events with the
  same signature mean the CAPA system, not just the batch, has failed.

## Microbiology OOS: a different animal

Microbiological OOS investigations follow the same logic but with harder
constraints:

- **Sterility test (USP &lt;71&gt;):** a positive sterility test may only
  be invalidated and repeated when the investigation documents that the
  test itself was invalid - defined conditions such as demonstrable
  contamination of the test environment or materials during testing,
  supported by concurrent monitoring data, or a documented error in test
  performance. "No growth in the room that day" is not evidence; the
  invalidation case must be built from environmental and procedural data
  captured *during the test*.
- **Microbial limits / bioburden:** investigate method suitability
  (neutralisation, recovery), media growth-promotion status, incubation,
  and sampling - then manufacturing: water systems, equipment
  cleanliness, hold times, raw-material bioburden.
- **No retesting into compliance:** replicate plates from the retained
  sample can characterise variability but cannot average away a failing
  result.
- **The organism matters:** identification of the contaminant often
  decides between a laboratory artefact (organism matching the test
  environment flora) and a genuine product event (organism matching the
  manufacturing environment or water system).

## Inspector Q&A: the questions that decide inspections

**"Show me an OOS you invalidated. Why was the cause assignable?"**
Strong answer: the record names the hypothesis, the check that proved it
(e.g. retained standard re-assayed and confirmed degraded), and the rule
that permitted invalidation - written before the repeat test.

**"Show me an OOS where you found no cause. What happened to the batch?"**
Strong answer: rejected, with the scope assessment (other batches
reviewed) and the trend entry documented. A site with zero unexplained
OOS rejections in five years is not credible; it is lucky or dishonest.

**"How many retests does your SOP allow, and who decides?"**
Strong answer: a defined number with a predefined decision rule, approved
by QA, triggered only by the Phase I evidence - not negotiated per event.

**"Who reviews the audit trail during Phase I, and when?"**
Strong answer: a named independent reviewer, before any retesting, with
the review record attached to the OOS file.

**"How do you know your investigations are effective?"**
Strong answer: recurrence metrics, CAPA effectiveness checks, and OOS
trending reviewed in management review and the APR.

## Data-integrity traps to avoid

- Re-injecting or re-testing without a pre-approved plan and an
  assignable cause.
- Averaging a failing result into a passing mean.
- Invalidating a result on an undocumented "analyst error."
- Reviewing only the results table, not the chromatograms, audit trail,
  and integration.
- Trial injections or unofficial "screening" runs that never become OOS
  records.
- Closing an investigation with a root cause the evidence doesn't
  support.
- Letting the same root cause recur - the trend proves the CAPA failed.

## Worked example: assay OOS on a tablet batch

*Fictional but realistic.* Batch TAB-5521, registered assay specification
95.0-105.0%. Reportable result: 93.8% (mean of two preparations, 93.6%
and 94.0% - both below the limit, so no averaging rescue is even
available).

**Phase I, day 1-3.** The record opens same-day. Hypotheses listed:
standard integrity, dilution, instrument, integration, sampling. Checks:
recalculation confirms the worksheet; system suitability passed before
and during the run; the audit trail shows no reprocessing or
re-injection; the retained standard preparation re-assays at 99.7%
against a fresh standard; the retained sample preparation re-injects at
93.9% - preparation stable, instrument consistent. Balance and pipette
records verified. No assignable laboratory cause: **the result stands.**

**Phase II, week 1-4.** Manufacturing review finds granulation endpoint
for TAB-5521 at the low edge of its range, and blend-uniformity data
slightly broader than historical (RSD 3.1% vs typical &lt; 2.0% - an
in-spec OOT nobody had trended). Change analysis: a new granulation
lubricant lot entered at this batch. Comparison batches using the
previous lubricant lot assay 99.2-100.4%. Root cause verified: a
lubricant-lot-driven blend segregation tendency, corroborated by a
small-scale mixing study. Disposition: TAB-5521 **rejected**. Scope: no
other distributed batches used the new lubricant lot; retain samples of
the two adjacent batches confirm in-spec assay. CAPA: supplier change
assessment gap (the lot change bypassed comparability review), blending
endpoint study repeated with the new lot, blend-uniformity trending added
to the trend review. Effectiveness check: next three batches with the
qualified material assay 99.0-100.2% with tight blend uniformity.

Note the two system lessons: the OOS was caught properly - and it was
also *predictable*, because an in-spec OOT signal had been sitting in the
blend-uniformity data untrended.
