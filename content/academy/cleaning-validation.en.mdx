# Cleaning Validation

Cleaning validation is the documented evidence that a cleaning procedure
consistently removes product residues, degradants, cleaning agents, and
microbial contamination from shared equipment to levels that cannot harm the
next patient. Cross-contamination through inadequately cleaned equipment is
one of the oldest recall causes in the industry - which is why regulators
treat cleaning validation as a core sterility-assurance and patient-safety
control, not a housekeeping exercise.

The governing framework:

- **EMA guideline on setting health-based exposure limits
  (EMA/CHMP/CVMP/SWP/169430/2012, effective 2015)** - mandates HBEL/PDE-based
  limits and is the reason the old default criteria are no longer sufficient
  on their own.
- **EU GMP Annex 15 (Qualification and Validation, 2015)** - sections on
  cleaning validation: worst-case logic, HBEL use, campaign and hold times.
- **FDA Guide to Inspections: Validation of Cleaning Processes (1993)** -
  still the clearest statement of FDA expectations (documentation,
  rationale, worst case, analytical specificity).
- **EU GMP Chapter 3 and Chapter 5** - dedicated vs shared equipment and
  cross-contamination prevention, reinforced by the Chapter 5 revisions
  driven by the HBEL guideline.
- **PIC/S PI 006** and **PDA Technical Report No. 29 (Points to Consider for
  Cleaning Validation)** - practical inspection and industry guidance.
- **ASTM E3106** - science- and risk-based cleaning process development
  lifecycle.

## Dedicated vs Shared Equipment: The First Decision

Before any calculation, decide the contamination strategy:

| Scenario | Regulatory expectation | Cleaning-validation consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dedicated equipment (one product only) | Cleaning still required for hygiene, degradants, and microbial control | Validation focuses on cleaning-agent residues and microbiological control; carryover to other products is not the driver |
| Shared equipment, comparable products | HBEL-based MACO limits and worst-case grouping | Full MACO program with justified product/equipment matrix |
| Highly potent or sensitizing compounds (e.g. certain cytotoxics, hormones, beta-lactams, biologicals at risk levels set by QRM) | EU GMP expects dedicated facilities/equipment where risk cannot be controlled otherwise | Cleaning validation may not be an acceptable substitute for dedication - decide via documented quality risk management (ICH Q9) |

The HBEL guideline's sharpest consequence: where the HBEL-based risk
assessment shows cleaning cannot reliably control the hazard, dedication - not
a tighter swab limit - is the answer.

## Acceptance Limits: From HBEL to MACO

Historically sites used **1/1000 of the minimum daily dose** or **10 ppm** as
default carryover criteria. Those are now screening heuristics at best. The
EMA guideline requires a **Permitted Daily Exposure (PDE)** derived by a
qualified toxicologist from the compound's pharmacological/toxicological data
(NOEL/LOEL with adjustment factors - see the
[Health-Based Exposure Limits lesson](/library/health-based-exposure-limits)).
The PDE feeds the MACO:

```
MACO (mg) = (PDE of product A  ×  minimum batch size of product B) / maximum daily dose of product B
```

where product A is the residue source (previous product) and product B is the
next product manufactured on the same equipment train. All in consistent mass
units.

From MACO to the laboratory limit:

```
Limit per surface area (mg/cm2) = MACO / total shared product-contact surface area
Limit per swab (mg)             = (MACO / total surface area) × swabbed area
Reportable limit after recovery = limit per swab × recovery factor
```

Take the **lowest** value across all next-product B scenarios as the governing
limit, and compare it against the legacy criteria (1/1000 dose, 10 ppm) and
the visually-clean threshold - the strictest defensible value wins. State in
the protocol which criteria were evaluated and why the governing one was
chosen; regulators expect HBEL-based limits unless a documented rationale
shows another criterion is more conservative or more appropriate.

## Worked Example: MACO for a Tablet Train (Fictional but Realistic)

Product A (previous): an antihypertensive, PDE established by toxicology at
**0.10 mg/day**. Product B (next): a vitamin tablet, minimum batch size
**50 kg = 50,000,000 mg**, maximum daily dose **1,000 mg/day**.

1. MACO = (0.10 × 50,000,000) / 1,000 = **5,000 mg** of A permitted in the
   entire next batch of B.
2. The shared train (granulator, fluid-bed dryer, blender, tablet press,
   coater) has a total product-contact area of **250,000 cm2** (fictional;
   obtain yours from equipment drawings and as-built measurements - this
   number is a common audit weak point).
3. Limit per area = 5,000 / 250,000 = **0.02 mg/cm2 = 20 µg/cm2**.
4. Swab template area = 25 cm2, so limit per swab = 20 × 25 = **500 µg/swab**.
5. Swab recovery factor from the recovery study = 0.80 (80%). Corrected
   acceptance limit = 500 × 0.80 = **400 µg/swab** as analyzed.
6. The analytical method must quantify comfortably below 400 µg/swab with
   demonstrated specificity for A in the presence of the detergent - set the
   LOQ well under the limit (common practice: LOQ at or below 50% of the
   limit - confirm against your method-validation SOP).

If the toxicologist later revises PDE_A down to 0.01 mg/day, every number
scales by ten - which is why the PDE register and the MACO workbook must be
under change control, with a trigger to re-evaluate limits when HBELs are
updated. The
[Cleaning Validation MACO Calculator](/tools/cleaning-validation-maco-calculator)
automates steps 1-5 so you can sanity-check the arithmetic.

## Worst-Case Product Matrix and Grouping

Validating every product on every equipment train is usually impractical.
Annex 15 and PIC/S accept a **bracketing/grouping approach** if the worst case
is chosen by documented rationale. Build a matrix scoring every product on:

| Criterion | Why it matters | Scoring example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toxicity / PDE | Lower PDE = tighter limit | PDE band (µg/day vs mg/day) |
| Solubility in the cleaning medium | Poorly soluble residues persist | Freely soluble / soluble / practically insoluble |
| Cleanability | Adhesion, drying behavior, coating | Lab or pilot cleanability study ranking |
| Batch size and dose (MACO impact) | Small next batches and high doses tighten limits | Calculated limit per cm2 |
| Equipment contact area and train complexity | More area dilutes or concentrates risk | Shared trains listed per product |

The product with the worst combination - typically low PDE, poor solubility,
poor cleanability - becomes the **worst-case representative** for its group.
Validate that one; the rest of the group is covered by the documented matrix
rationale. The matrix is a living document: every new product introduction
triggers a re-assessment (via change control), and a new product can silently
dethrone the worst case - a classic inspection finding.

Equipment is grouped the same way: same design, same cleaning procedure, same
materials of construction. Different geometries or cleaning parameters break
the group.

## Sampling Methods and Recovery Studies

| Method | What it measures | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Swab (direct surface sampling) | Residue physically removed from a defined area | Direct evidence at worst-case locations (hard-to-clean: gaskets, valves, dead legs, agitator shafts) | Technique-dependent; needs recovery factor; limited area |
| Rinse sampling | Residue in the final rinse solvent/water | Covers large and inaccessible areas; easy to execute | Indirect - assumes residue dissolves into the rinse; dilution can mask localized failures; needs its own recovery rationale |
| Visual inspection | Visible residue under defined light, distance, and acuity | Fast, catches gross failures; regulators expect a quantified visual threshold where possible | Never sufficient alone; subjective without a defined and trained threshold |
| Microbial sampling (contact plates/swabs, rinse) | Bioburden and, for sterile equipment, endotoxin | Verifies hygienic state and CHT/DHT claims | Separate acceptance criteria; see EM and endotoxin references |

**Recovery studies are the step people skip - and the first thing a good
inspector asks for.** Spike coupons of each material of construction (316L
stainless steel, PTFE, EPDM, glass, silicone - whatever the train contains) at
levels bracketing the acceptance limit, let them dry as residues would in
production, then sample with the actual SOP technique and analyze. Industry
practice treats recoveries around 70-80% or better as readily acceptable,
around 50% and above as usable with documented justification and correction,
and below 50% as a signal to improve the method (swab material, solvent,
technique) - confirm thresholds against your site SOP; they are convention,
not compendial limits. Results are **corrected** by the recovery factor, and
each material/residue combination needs its own factor.

Sampling locations must be justified as worst case: hard-to-clean points
identified from equipment drawings and cleaning-spray coverage studies, not
the most convenient flat panel.

## Campaign Approaches, Dirty Hold Time, Clean Hold Time

- **Campaign production**: running multiple consecutive batches of the same
  product between full cleans reduces changeover risk. Validate the
  **longest** campaign (maximum batch count and maximum time), with data
  showing residue and bioburden do not accumulate beyond limits - typically
  microbial monitoring at campaign end plus residue data at defined points.
- **Dirty hold time (DHT)**: the maximum time equipment may sit soiled before
  cleaning. Validate it with worst-case residue drying plus microbial data -
  wet residues grow; dried residues harden.
- **Clean hold time (CHT)**: the maximum time cleaned equipment stays clean
  before use. Validate with microbial (and, for sterile product contact,
  endotoxin) data at the claimed storage duration and conditions.

All three are validated parameters with numbers in the protocol - not verbal
conventions. DHT and CHT failures trend in FDA 483s because sites claim them
without supporting data.

## Validation Execution and Lifecycle

A defensible program runs like any validation lifecycle:

1. **Development**: cleanability studies, cleaning-agent selection (and its
   own residue/toxicity assessment), parameter ranges (time, temperature,
   concentration, flow/pressure for CIP).
2. **Protocol**: scope, product/equipment matrix rationale, MACO calculations,
   sampling plan with worst-case locations, methods with LOQ and recovery
   factors, acceptance criteria (chemical, cleaning agent, microbial,
   visual), number of consecutive successful runs (commonly three - site
   convention, state your rationale), DHT/CHT challenges.
3. **Execution**: trained operators, cleaning per SOP, sampling per plan,
   deviations investigated in real time.
4. **Report**: data vs criteria, deviations dispositioned, matrix conclusions,
   routine monitoring plan.
5. **Continued verification**: periodic swab/rinse monitoring, visual checks
   each clean, trend review, re-validation triggers (new products, equipment
   modification, cleaning-agent or parameter change, repeated failures).

## Common Failure Modes and What Inspectors Look For

Recurring FDA 483 / Warning Letter and MHRA themes:

1. **No recovery data, or recovery not applied to results.** Defense:
   coupon studies per material, correction factor in every calculation.
2. **Worst-case product chosen by convenience**, matrix undocumented or never
   updated for new products. Defense: living matrix under change control with
   new-product triggers.
3. **MACO arithmetic errors or unjustified surface areas.** Defense:
   documented surface calculations from drawings; independent QA check of the
   math.
4. **Sampling only easy, flat, accessible surfaces.** Defense: worst-case
   location rationale tied to spray coverage and design review.
5. **Cleaning agent never addressed** - detergent residues unlimited and
   untested. Defense: agent-specific limit (toxicity-based) and test.
6. **Visual clean used as the sole criterion.** Defense: visual threshold as
   one of four criterion types, never the only one.
7. **DHT/CHT claimed without microbial data.** Defense: hold-time studies
   with bioburden (and endotoxin where relevant) endpoints.
8. **Failures "cleaned again until pass" without investigation.** FDA's 1993
   guide is explicit that repeated re-cleaning to pass is not acceptable
   practice. Defense: failures investigated as deviations with root cause and
   CAPA.
9. **No continued verification after the three validation runs.** Defense:
   routine monitoring plan and periodic review in the report.
10. **Lab method cannot see the limit** - LOQ above the acceptance criterion.
    Defense: method suitability demonstrated against the MACO-derived limit
    before protocol approval.
