# Out-of-Trend (OOT) Investigation

An Out-of-Trend result is **within specification** but deviates from the
expected historical or statistical pattern. It is the early-warning layer of
laboratory control: the signal that something in the process, the method, or
the product is drifting - weeks or months before the same drift would produce
an out-of-specification (OOS) failure, a rejected batch, or a recall.

OOT detection is not a regulatory nicety. The **FDA Guidance for Industry:
Investigating Out-of-Specification (OOS) Test Results (2006)** expects
laboratories to trend data and act on adverse trends. **EU GMP Chapter 6**
(Quality Control) requires results and trends to be reviewed. **21 CFR
211.180(e)** requires an annual product review that specifically looks for
trends and for the need to change specifications, methods, or processes. And
**ICH Q10** frames trending as a core element of continued process
verification across the lifecycle. For stability, **ICH Q1E** (Evaluation of
Stability Data) governs how time-point data are modelled and how extrapolation
is justified.

The high-stakes version of this topic is simple: an OOT handled well costs a
few days of investigation. The same signal ignored becomes an OOS, a field
alert, or a recall - with a regulator asking why your trending program saw
nothing.

## OOT vs OOS vs OOE: get the vocabulary right

| Term | Definition | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OOS (out of specification) | Result outside registered/compendial acceptance criteria | Formal OOS investigation under the FDA OOS Guidance and 21 CFR 211.192; batch disposition at stake |
| OOT (out of trend) | Within specification but outside the historical/statistical pattern | Trend investigation; escalate to OOS handling if the trend threatens the specification |
| OOE (out of expectation) | Within limits but atypical vs what was expected for this batch (site-defined term) | Documented assessment; often folded into the OOT procedure |

The distinction matters because the response differs. An OOS stops release
and follows the phased investigation model. An OOT does not stop release by
itself - but it must trigger a documented, timely review, because the whole
point of trending is to act *before* the specification is breached. A site
that treats every in-spec result as automatically acceptable has no trending
program, whatever its SOPs say.

## The three types of trend signal

1. **Within-batch trend** - a single result deviates from the pattern of
   comparable prior results (e.g. assay 98.1% against a tight historical
   range of 99.2-100.1%).
2. **Across-batch trend** - successive batches creep in one direction (yield
   down 0.5% per batch for six batches; dissolution slowly drifting down).
3. **Stability trend** - a stability time point falls outside the projected
   degradation line, or the slope changes versus the established model.

Each type has a different natural tool: individual-value control charts for
the first, run charts and Western Electric-style rule sets for the second,
and regression with prediction intervals for the third.

## Statistical trend triggers: the toolbox

### Control-chart logic

The core of OOT detection is a control chart built on **stable historical
data**, not on the specification:

- **Centre line** - mean of a baseline of consecutive conforming batches
  (common industry practice: at least 20-30 batches; fewer for low-volume
  products, with the limitation documented - confirm against your SOP).
- **Warning (alert) limit** - mean ± 2 standard deviations.
- **Action limit** - mean ± 3 standard deviations.
- For non-normal or count data (bioburden, particulates), use transformed
  data or attribute charts; a normal-distribution chart on heavily skewed
  data generates false alarms and learned indifference.

On top of the limits, apply **run rules** that catch drift before any single
point crosses a limit. The classic Western Electric / Nelson rules include:

| Rule | Signal | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 point beyond 3 SD | Action excursion | Special cause - investigate as an event |
| 2 of 3 consecutive points beyond 2 SD (same side) | Warning | Shift developing |
| 4 of 5 consecutive points beyond 1 SD (same side) | Warning | Sustained small shift |
| 7-8 consecutive points on one side of the centre line | Shift | Process mean has moved |
| 6 consecutive points increasing or decreasing | Trend | Continuous drift (raw material, wear, reagent aging) |
| 14 points alternating up/down | Oscillation | Over-control, alternating operators, or mixing two populations |

The exact rule set should be defined in the trending SOP *before* it is
needed. A rule invented after a surprising result is not a control - it is
rationalisation.

### Regression-based limits for stability

For stability data, a control chart on individual time points is the wrong
tool, because change over time is the *expected* behaviour. **ICH Q1E**
describes fitting a regression model to the attribute versus time and using
confidence and prediction intervals around that line:

- Fit the degradation line (usually linear; justify transformations such as
  logarithmic for assay loss where the kinetics support it).
- A time point outside the **prediction interval** for an individual future
  observation is an OOT signal even when comfortably within specification.
- The **shelf-life estimate** is where the one-sided confidence bound of the
  mean line crosses the acceptance criterion - if a new point shifts that
  crossing inside the registered shelf life, you have an emerging OOS
  scenario that QA must see immediately.
- Poolability matters: batch slopes and intercepts should be tested for
  similarity (ICH Q1E's analysis-of-covariance approach) before pooling
  batches into one model. Pooling dissimilar batches hides real trends.

### By-the-firm rules

Many sites add pragmatic stability rules on top of the statistics, such as
"any time point that deviates from the previous point by more than X% of the
range" or "three consecutive time points moving against the historical
slope". These are legitimate as **site-defined practice - confirm against
your SOP** - but they must be pre-specified, applied uniformly, and never
used to explain a point away.

## Building defensible OOT limits

The most common trending failure is a limit that was never properly derived.
A defensible limit set requires:

1. **A stable baseline.** Use batches produced under the same process,
   method version, and equipment train. Exclude batches with assignable
   special causes - but document each exclusion and its reason.
2. **Adequate quantity.** Roughly 20-30 data points for a conventional
   chart; fewer points produce unstable limits that cry wolf. For
   low-volume products, state the limitation and review limits as data
   accrue.
3. **Separation from the specification.** If the control limits sit at or
   beyond the specification, the chart is useless: by the time it alarms,
   the batch has already failed. Control limits must be visibly tighter
   than the release limits.
4. **Process and method change gates.** Re-baseline after validated process
   changes, method transfers, or instrument replacements - carrying old
   limits across a change manufactures phantom trends.
5. **Periodic re-evaluation.** Review limits at least annually (often as
   part of the APR/PQR) against accumulated data. Limits frozen since
   validation are a classic inspection observation.

## The OOT investigation workflow

| Step | Owner | Question | Record produced |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Detect | Analyst / system | Is this a genuine OOT signal per the SOP's rules? | Trend chart, flagged result |
| 2. Confirm the data | Analyst + supervisor | Is the value real - calculation, transcription, integration, units? | Data verification note |
| 3. Classify | Supervisor | Within-batch, across-batch, or stability signal? Which rule fired? | OOT record opened |
| 4. Assess impact | QA + technical lead | Does the trend threaten the specification within shelf life or the next batches? | Impact assessment |
| 5. Investigate cause | Lab / manufacturing / QA | Method drift, instrument, standard, raw material, process change, or genuine product change? | Root-cause section |
| 6. Act | QA | Monitor, corrective action, CAPA, escalation to OOS handling, or field assessment | Action plan, CAPA reference |
| 7. Close and trend | QA | Was the action effective? Did the trend recover? | Closure record, updated trend |

Timing matters. OOT reviews should happen promptly - a stability OOT found
at a scheduled data review three months after the time point was tested has
already burned the lead time that makes trending valuable.

### Decision points that need pre-written rules

- **When does an OOT become an OOS investigation?** Common triggers: the
  projected crossing of the specification within shelf life; a confirmed
  special cause that invalidates prior results; or an OOT on a
  stability-indicating attribute at the last scheduled time point.
- **When is a CAPA required?** Recurrence of the same signal, a confirmed
  systemic cause, or any product-impact conclusion.
- **Who can close it?** The laboratory closes data-verification OOTs; QA
  must close anything with product or process implications. An analyst
  closing their own trend excursion is a data-integrity red flag.

## Special case: stability OOTs

Stability OOTs carry the highest stakes because the product is already
distributed. The escalation logic:

1. Confirm the result and rule out method/lab artefacts at that time point.
2. Re-fit the model including the new point; check whether the shelf-life
   estimate moves inside the registered period.
3. Assess the remaining on-market time points: which batches, which markets,
   how much stock.
4. Decide with QA: continue the study under heightened review, add interim
   testing of retained samples, shorten the projected shelf life through
   regulatory variation, or escalate toward field action.
5. Feed the event into the ongoing stability program review - a single OOT
   that recurs across batches is a program-level signal, not a batch-level
   one.

Per ICH Q1E, limited extrapolation beyond the data range is possible only
when justified by the statistical analysis and supporting data - an OOT that
invalidates the model removes that justification.

## Other trend domains worth monitoring

OOT logic is not only for assay results:

- **Environmental monitoring** - a location trending upward within action
  levels, or a flora shift, is an OOT even when every plate is in limits.
- **Water systems** - loop counts drifting seasonally; see the site's water
  monitoring program.
- **Yields and process parameters** - continued process verification data
  under process validation Stage 3 (FDA Process Validation Guidance, 2011).
- **Method performance** - system suitability trends (tailing, resolution,
  %RSD) predict method failure before the first failing batch.

## What inspectors look for - and the failure modes they cite

1. **No trending at all.** Results reviewed one batch at a time; the APR is
   the only place data ever meets a chart. FDA 483 observations repeatedly
   cite firms that had the data but never trended it.
2. **Limits copied from a template.** Alert/action levels identical to the
   specification, or values with no derivation record. Defence: keep the
   baseline dataset, the calculation, and the approval in the trending file.
3. **OOTs documented but never investigated.** A flag on a chart with no
   follow-up record is worse than no chart - it proves the signal was seen
   and ignored.
4. **Averaging the trend away.** Pooling batches with different slopes, or
   smoothing data so the excursion disappears. Defence: test poolability,
   and chart individuals alongside means.
5. **Limits never updated.** Baseline from validation a decade old. Defence:
   annual re-evaluation tied to the APR/PQR.
6. **Stability OOT closed without on-market assessment.** The investigation
   stops at "lab check passed". Defence: every stability OOT record must
   state which distributed batches were evaluated.
7. **Trend rules applied selectively.** The 7-point run rule fires but is
   "not applicable this time". Defence: the SOP defines the rules; any
   exception needs a documented QA rationale.
8. **No link to CAPA or APR.** OOT signals die in a spreadsheet. Defence:
   the trending procedure must name the escalation routes and owners.

## Worked example: assay drift across batches

*Fictional but realistic.* A tablet product has a registered assay
specification of 95.0-105.0%. The trending SOP uses an individuals chart
with the baseline from 25 historical batches: mean 99.4%, SD 0.45%, giving
a warning limit at 98.5% and an action limit at 98.1%.

| Batch | Assay (%) | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B-118 | 99.3 | None |
| B-119 | 99.0 | None |
| B-120 | 98.9 | None |
| B-121 | 98.7 | 4th point descending |
| B-122 | 98.6 | 5th point descending |
| B-123 | 98.4 | Below 2 SD warning; 6 consecutive descending - trend rule fires |

Every result is in specification. Nothing stops release. But the run rule
has fired, so the supervisor opens an OOT record. Data verification is
clean. The investigation reviews the window from B-118 onward and finds the
site switched to a new granulation lubricant supplier lot at B-118; blend
uniformity data show a matching small shift. QA assesses impact: at the
observed drift rate, the specification would be threatened in roughly eight
more batches, and distributed batches B-118 to B-123 remain comfortably in
specification with stable trend data from retain testing.

Actions: quarantine remaining stock of the new lubricant lot pending
evaluation, open a CAPA on the supplier change-assessment gap (the lot
change was processed without a comparability check), add blend uniformity
to the trend review, and confirm recovery across the next three batches.
The record closes with the trend recovered at 99.2-99.5% - and the APR
captures the event as a supplier-management lesson.

Note the economics of the catch: six batches of lead time, zero rejected
batches, one modest CAPA. The same drift found at 94.8% would have been an
OOS with batch disposition, a possible field assessment, and a very
different inspection conversation.

## Linking OOT to APR and CAPA

OOT signals are a primary input to the **Annual Product Review / Product
Quality Review** - the APR is where slow across-batch drifts become visible
and where the effectiveness of prior CAPAs is judged. A documented OOT that
never feeds the APR or a CAPA is a detection system with no response
attached. Equally, the APR is where you prove the trending program itself
is alive: limits re-evaluated, rules followed, escalations made.
