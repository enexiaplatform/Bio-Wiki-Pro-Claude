# From testing demand to a challengeable Quality Lab capacity decision

The question is rarely "which instrument should we buy?" The useful question is:

> Can this quality organization make the decisions it must make, at the required
> turnaround and confidence, as products, processes and demand change?

That question is shared by a small-molecule API site, a biologics manufacturing
site and a laboratory serving several clients. The evidence is different, but the
planning logic is the same: start from decisions, trace the work to methods and
resources, then make uncertainty visible before a purchase or construction
commitment is made.

## The decision package, not an equipment list

A useful Quality Lab capacity decision should let a project owner answer six
questions:

1. Which products, markets, batches, studies or investigations create demand?
2. Which tests and methods produce the evidence for release, stability, process
   monitoring, investigation or change decisions?
3. Which people, skills, shifts, equipment, utilities, materials and systems are
   needed to execute that work?
4. Where do queue time, hold time, method duration, review time or equipment
   downtime create a bottleneck?
5. Which baseline, growth or resilience scenario is being compared, and what
   assumptions drive the difference?
6. What must be verified by QC, QA, engineering, procurement, finance or a
   qualified specialist before the package can be used for the next decision?

The result is a challengeable basis. It is not a facility design, validation
conclusion, batch-release decision or regulatory approval.

## Start with the portfolio and the decision owner

For a Pharma API portfolio, the input chain may include starting-material and
intermediate controls, process-development samples, impurity and residual-solvent
methods, specifications, stability pulls, method transfer and supplier changes.
For a Biopharma portfolio, it may include cell substrate and banks, raw and
ancillary materials, upstream and downstream process samples, potency and product
characterization, viral-safety studies, comparability, technology transfer and
continued process verification.

Do not flatten these into one generic test count. Capture the decision owner and
the intended use of each evidence stream. A method used for development learning
does not automatically have the same review, data, sample, training or capacity
requirements as a method used for routine release.

The first release should therefore contain:

- a decision statement and accountable owner;
- in-scope and out-of-scope products, sites, lifecycle stages and markets;
- a dated input inventory with known gaps;
- a capability map connecting each decision to methods, sample types and review;
- assumptions, confidence and material-change triggers; and
- an explicit list of questions that cannot be answered from the current evidence.

## Convert demand into work, not just samples

Sample volume is an incomplete capacity unit. A defensible model records the work
that a sample creates: preparation, instrument or incubation time, analyst touch
time, review time, repeat or invalid handling, cleaning or changeover, data review,
and any hold or stability-window constraint.

For each method or study, record the basis used to estimate work. Examples include
an approved procedure version, a controlled observation, a pilot assumption or an
explicit placeholder awaiting site data. Keep the following separate:

| Demand signal | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Batch, lot or study count | Defines how often work arrives |
| Test and method mix | Defines capability and method-specific work |
| Sample arrival and deadline | Defines queue and turnaround pressure |
| Method, incubation and review time | Defines resource occupancy |
| Repeat, investigation and change demand | Defines resilience beyond the average |
| Outsource or hybrid boundary | Defines retained capability and handoffs |

The model should show which conclusion changes when an input changes. A polished
total that hides its natural capacity unit is not a planning basis.

## Compare scenarios that a team can actually choose between

At minimum, compare a baseline scenario with one alternative that changes a real
decision. Depending on the project, that alternative may be a second shift, a
different in-house and outsourced boundary, a phased capability, a second
instrument, a different sampling cadence or a deliberate resilience reserve.

Each scenario should state:

- what is held constant;
- what changed and why;
- which evidence supports the changed input;
- which bottleneck, cost, schedule or risk moves;
- what remains unknown; and
- who must accept the trade-off.

For example, an API site may compare keeping a late-eluting impurity method
in-house against using a qualified external laboratory during a site-change
window. A Biopharma site may compare a single potency platform with a staged
internal and partner model while a new product moves from development into
routine manufacturing. Neither comparison is answered by a universal staffing
ratio. The decision depends on method capability, sample timing, review roles,
qualification state, supply continuity and the evidence available for the specific
site and product.

## Worked fictional case: one signal, two portfolios

Imagine a site that supports an API process change and a monoclonal-antibody
technology transfer in the same quarter. The first draft shows enough instrument
hours in aggregate, but the team still misses the decision because:

- the API impurity work arrives in a narrow release window;
- the Biopharma potency assay needs qualified analysts and a controlled reference
  system;
- both streams compete for the same review capacity;
- one outsourced result has a longer transport and data-review handoff; and
- the demand estimate does not include investigation repeats or change-control
  work.

The useful next step is not to declare a staffing number. It is to record the
missing arrival pattern, confirm the method and reviewer assumptions, compare an
in-house baseline with a phased hybrid scenario, and assign each open question to
an owner. The resulting package can support a procurement or resourcing decision
without pretending that a planning estimate is a validated operating limit.

## Release criteria for a useful Quality Lab package

Before a Diagnostic or Blueprint is used in a controlled project discussion, check
that it contains:

- a frozen scope, basis date, revision and document ID;
- a capability and demand chain that a reviewer can trace;
- baseline and alternative scenario logic;
- staffing, equipment, space, cost and resilience assumptions;
- evidence, confidence, limitations and material-change triggers;
- an open-question and decision register with owners and dates; and
- acceptance criteria for the next review or implementation handoff.

Atlas structures this package and exposes where the evidence is weak. The client,
qualified reviewers and accountable functions still decide whether an assumption
is acceptable, whether a method is fit for its intended use, and whether a design
or operating change can proceed.
